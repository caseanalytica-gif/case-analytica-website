## Post copy

"Reasonable suspicion" gets used like it explains everything. It's actually a specific legal standard written into a specific statute, and whether an officer had it is the question that decided one of the largest civil rights rulings against the NYPD in the department's history.

CPL 140.50 requires two separate findings of reasonable suspicion, not one: one to stop you, a second one to frisk you. A stop alone doesn't authorize a frisk. And courts have held that being in a "high-crime area" or making a "furtive movement" doesn't, by itself, add up to reasonable suspicion — an officer needs specific, articulable facts about that particular person.

That distinction was at the center of Floyd v. City of New York (2013), where a federal judge found the NYPD's stop-and-frisk practice violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments, and that the department had shown "deliberate indifference" toward a pattern of suspicionless stops.
