## Post copy

"It's been months. Sometimes over a year. Is there a law that forces my case to move?"

I hear some version of this constantly. There is a law — CPL 30.30, New York's speedy trial statute. Almost nobody explains it correctly, because almost nobody explains what it actually measures.

Here's the part that surprises people: CPL 30.30 doesn't require a trial by a certain date. It requires the prosecution to be *ready* for trial within a set window — six months for a felony, 90 days for a higher-level misdemeanor, 60 days for a lower-level one, 30 for a violation. "Ready for trial" and "went to trial" are not the same event, and the clock only tracks the first one.

It gets more complicated from there. The clock stops for pretrial motions, competency proceedings, defense-requested delays, and more. A case that's been open 14 months on the calendar might have a legal 30.30 clock of far less than that — once excludable time is calculated correctly. Nobody does that math automatically. It has to be tracked, on every open file, by someone with the hours to do it.

Since discovery reform, a prosecutor's readiness statement generally isn't valid without a proper Certificate of Compliance — which directly affects this calculation too.

If you or someone you know has an open case in New York, ask the attorney directly: "What is the actual CPL 30.30 date on my case right now, after excludable time, and can I see how you calculated it?" If the answer is vague, ask again — in writing.

## Alt shorter version (for a follow-up or carousel caption)

Most people think CPL 30.30 guarantees a trial deadline in New York. It doesn't — it sets a deadline for prosecutors to *say they're ready*. Those are two very different things, and the gap between them is where cases quietly drag on for over a year.
