## LinkedIn

A campaign promise about community safety became an actual office with actual money six months ago. Here's what it's spent it on.

Mayor Mamdani's Executive Order No. 15, signed March 19, 2026, created the Mayor's Office of Community Safety and pulled five existing city offices under one roof: crime victim services, gun violence prevention, domestic and gender-based violence, hate crime prevention, and community mental health.

The campaign version was a $1.1 billion department. What launched was $260 million and a mandate, with the mayor saying he'll pursue full department status through the City Council once there's a track record. Starting with what's available beats waiting years for the ideal version.

The model isn't untested. A John Jay College study of New York's own Cure Violence neighborhoods found homicides dropped 18 percent in program areas between 2010 and 2013, while matched neighborhoods without the program saw them rise 69 percent.

Since launch: $4.8 million to expand the Crisis Management System into three more precincts, $4.3 million for hospital-based violence intervention, $1.5 million for mobile response in neighborhoods with no provider on the ground.

Six months isn't years of citywide data. But the money is moving, and it's moving toward the neighborhoods that have carried the most gun violence in this city for decades.

## Facebook

Six months ago, New York City created an office built on a simple idea: when someone is in a mental health crisis, or a block is grieving after a shooting, the first response doesn't have to be a person with a gun and a badge.

Executive Order No. 15 created the Mayor's Office of Community Safety in March 2026 and moved $260 million into it, along with five existing city offices that had been working the same problem separately.

What that looks like on the ground: after 12-year-old Jacob Freytes was killed by a stray bullet in the Bronx this July, the office organized outreach teams to canvas against retaliation, connected the family to grief counseling and temporary housing help, and put violence interrupters, including a Spanish-language team, at a community parade the next day.

This approach has evidence behind it from this city's own streets. A John Jay College study found homicides fell 18 percent in neighborhoods running Cure Violence programs while comparable neighborhoods without them saw homicides climb 69 percent over the same years.

It's a young office and there's a lot still to prove. But the money is real and it's going where the harm actually is.
