**A judge let one mother's psychiatric records reach the jury. Four years earlier, in the same courtroom, he kept another's out.**

---

On September 4, a Massachusetts jury deadlocked 11-1 on Lindsay Clancy, the Duxbury mother who admitted to killing her three children in 2023. Her defense was postpartum psychosis. The jury saw her psychiatric records, and the eleven who voted to find her not criminally responsible had that evidence in front of them. A mistrial is not an acquittal and a retrial is expected, so this isn't a story about someone walking free.

It's what happened in the same courthouse four years earlier that's worth sitting with.

Judge William F. Sullivan presided over Clancy's trial. He also presided over the 2022 trial of Latarsha Sanders, a Black mother from Brockton who killed her two sons in 2018 and raised a defense of schizophrenia and paranoia. Same judge. Same county. Same district attorney's office. At her trial, Sullivan excluded thousands of pages of her psychiatric records. The jury never saw them. She was convicted and sentenced to two consecutive life terms without parole.

On August 6 of this year, Massachusetts' Supreme Judicial Court vacated that conviction unanimously. Justice Frank Gaziano wrote that excluding those records was "prejudicial error," and pointed to a state law saying such records "shall be admissible" on exactly that question. It took almost four years, and an appeal fought from a maximum-security cell, to establish that Sanders should have had what Clancy was simply given.

I write mostly about New York, and the reason this landed for me is that the same thing shows up in our parole data. Between 2016 and 2024, New York's Board of Parole released 41.6% of white applicants and 33.2% of applicants of color. Everyone in that pool has already served their court-ordered minimum, so the underlying offense doesn't explain the gap. What's being judged is whether someone's account of their own change is believed. Researchers estimate that if applicants of color had been released at the white rate, somewhere between 3,650 and 4,150 more people would have gone home.

A judge's evidentiary ruling and a parole board's release rate are different kinds of decisions, and I'm not claiming one proves the other. But both come down to the same question of who gets the benefit of the doubt, and both keep answering it the same way.

The full piece has the source video, the side-by-side case record, and the parole numbers with citations.

Lindsay Clancy's trial ended in a hung jury on September 4. She admitted to killing her three children in 2023 and argued postpartum psychosis. The jury saw her psychiatric records and deadlocked 11-1 toward finding her not criminally responsible. She may still be retried.

Four years earlier, the same judge heard the case of Latarsha Sanders, a Black mother from Brockton who killed her two sons and raised a defense of schizophrenia. He kept thousands of pages of her psychiatric records away from that jury. She got two consecutive life sentences without parole.

This August, Massachusetts' highest court threw out that conviction. The justices said unanimously that keeping those records out was an error serious enough to have changed the outcome, and that state law says they should have come in. Nearly four years to get there, all of it appealed from a maximum-security prison.

Here in New York, the parole numbers describe the same thing at a larger scale. From 2016 to 2024, the Parole Board released 41.6% of white applicants and 33.2% of applicants of color — and everyone in that room had already served their minimum sentence.

Different states, different institutions, same question underneath: whose account of themselves gets believed.
