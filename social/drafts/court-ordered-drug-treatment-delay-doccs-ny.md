## LinkedIn

**New York gives the prison system ten days to place someone in court-ordered treatment. One man waited 506.**

---

Michael Matzell pleaded guilty to drug possession. The judge ordered him into shock incarceration, the six-month program that ends in a certificate of earned eligibility. DOCCS screened him and found him not suitable, citing his prison disciplinary tickets for drug use. His need for treatment was the stated reason he was refused treatment.

He won in the Third Department in 2020 and again in the Second Circuit in 2023. By then he had waited 506 days past the point he became eligible. He completed the program and was released immediately.

Two provisions make that wait hard to defend. Criminal Procedure Law 410.91 remands a person sentenced to parole supervision for immediate delivery to a reception center "for a period not to exceed ten days." Correction Law 867(2-a) says a judicially sentenced person "shall promptly commence participation in the program," and it opens with "notwithstanding subdivision five," which is the provision that calls participation a privilege conferring no right. For a court-ordered placement, the Legislature switched that defense off.

DOCCS's own Earned Eligibility report for April to September 2025 shows what the waiting costs. Of 574 people found non-certifiable for a certificate, 244 were non-certifiable because they were still in reception. Those 244 were approved for parole at 24.6 percent. People holding a certificate were approved at 66.5 percent.

There is also a rule on the books that no longer matches the law. 7 NYCRR 1800.4 still says an applicant must not have reached 40 years of age. The statute says fifty. DOCCS's own report on the 2009 Drug Law Reform describes the under-40 rule as the one replaced in April 2009. Someone reading the published regulation, finding they are 43, has every reason to stop asking.

The part worth sitting with is the remedy. It works. Getting to it took an Article 78 petition, an appeal, a federal civil rights suit, and Prisoners' Legal Services of New York. Before any of that, the grievance ladder runs the facility committee, then the superintendent, then Albany, at eighty days minimum if nobody is late. The program lasts six months. The Third Department decided Matzell's appeal after it had gone moot, on the ground that the question "will typically evade review in the courts."

Whether a person gets the benefit of a clear statute turns on whether they know the deadline is there.

Michael Matzell was sentenced to four years for drug possession, and the judge ordered him into shock incarceration, a six-month program. DOCCS decided he was not suitable because he had disciplinary tickets for drug use. He needed treatment, and that was the reason given for denying him treatment.

He sued and won twice. By the time he was enrolled, 506 days had passed. He was released the day he completed the program.

New York law is not vague here. For a court-ordered drug sentence, the statute caps the stop at the reception center at ten days. For shock incarceration, the law says a person sentenced by a judge shall promptly commence the program, and it specifically overrides the usual line that participation is only a privilege.

The state's own numbers show what delay costs. In six months last year, 244 people were ruled non-certifiable for an earned eligibility certificate for a single reason: they were still sitting in reception. They were approved for parole at 24.6 percent. People who held a certificate were approved at 66.5 percent.

If you or someone you love was sentenced to a program and has not been placed, ask the attorney for the deadline in writing and for what gets filed the day it passes. ## Note on tone

The article is deliberately careful in three places, and the copy above keeps that.

It does not claim the delay is universal. It reports one documented case with a number attached
and one set of DOCCS figures, and it keeps the two populations separate: 506 days is a
court-ordered shock placement under Penal Law 60.04(7), while the ten-day cap belongs to a
parole supervision sentence under CPL 410.91. Merging them is the error that would let a
spokesperson dismiss the whole piece.

It labels the unresolved question as unresolved. Correction Law 865 contains two age clauses
that disagree at exactly fifty, and the article says plainly that no court appears to have
decided it.

It warns in both directions on the grievance process. Filing early when exhaustion was required
gets a petition dismissed, which is what happened in Matter of Jackson. Grieving all the way
through when it was not required costs eighty days of a six-month program. The post copy points
readers at the article and at the Jailhouse Lawyer's Manual rather than implying either path is
the safe one.
