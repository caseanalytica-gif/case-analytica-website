## LinkedIn

**New York's riot statute doesn't require a riot. Five people and conduct a court calls "tumultuous" is the whole threshold.**

---

At 1:45 in the morning on April 18, a crowd of about 100 people and 50 cars gathered at 69th Street and Eliot Avenue on the Maspeth–Middle Village border. Someone poured liquid on the pavement and lit it. A blue Infiniti circled inside the ring of flames. When patrol cars arrived, a traffic barrier went under one of them and two people cracked its windshield.

The Queens DA arraigned a 22-year-old driver on six counts. Reckless endangerment in the first degree, riot in the second degree, unlawful assembly, reckless driving, driving without a license, driving without insurance. No prior arrests. Up to seven years of exposure. Not bail eligible.

Here is the part most coverage skipped. Penal Law § 240.05 makes riot in the second degree a class A misdemeanor when you engage in tumultuous and violent conduct "simultaneously with four or more other persons" and recklessly create a grave risk of public alarm. Unlawful assembly under § 240.10 is the class B misdemeanor version. Neither one asks for a mob. Five people clears it.

So the riot count isn't where the seven years lives. That comes from the sixth charge — reckless endangerment in the first degree, a class D felony under § 120.25, which needs conduct "evincing a depraved indifference to human life." Two cars spinning near a hundred people and two gas stations is the fact pattern that statute was drafted around. The gap between a misdemeanor night and a felony record usually turns on how close the conduct came to killing someone, not on the headcount.

And the bail question answers itself once you read the statute. New York only authorizes cash bail for an enumerated list of charges. Reckless endangerment in the first degree isn't on it. Neither is riot or unlawful assembly. The DA's own release says so.

This is an allegation, not a finding. What's worth watching is the charging pattern forming around social-media-organized gatherings — Bay Plaza on Presidents Day, Washington Square, Brooklyn Bridge Park in March, all charged under these same statutes this year. Whether that calibration is right is a real debate. That a 22-year-old with no record is inside it is not in question.

## Facebook

**Most people think "riot" means a mob. In New York it means you plus four other people.**

---

A car meetup in Maspeth in April ended with a ring of fire lit in the middle of an intersection, two cars spinning inside it, and a cracked patrol car windshield. The Queens DA charged a 22-year-old driver with six counts. He had no prior arrests.

Riot in the second degree sounds like the serious one. It isn't. It's a misdemeanor, and under Penal Law § 240.05 it only takes five people total and conduct a court finds tumultuous enough to create a grave risk of public alarm. Unlawful assembly is a step below that.

The seven years everyone's quoting comes from one charge on the list nobody's naming: reckless endangerment in the first degree, a felony. That's the one that changes a life.

He also wasn't held on bail, and that isn't leniency or a loophole. New York's bail law only lets a judge set cash for specific enumerated charges, and none of these are on the list.

If you or someone you know went to something that turned into this, the thing to understand before anything else is which count is the felony. That's the one the whole case turns on. Talk to a defense attorney about that count specifically.
