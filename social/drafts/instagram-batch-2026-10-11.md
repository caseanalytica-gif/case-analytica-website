## 2. court-ordered-drug-treatment-delay-doccs-ny
Image: assets/social/court-ordered-drug-treatment-delay-doccs-ny-og.png

Caption:
A judge ordered him into a drug program. He waited 506 days, and walked out the day he finished it.

Michael Matzell was sentenced to four years for drug possession, and the judge ordered him into shock incarceration, a six-month program. DOCCS decided he was not suitable because he had disciplinary tickets for drug use. He needed treatment, and that was the reason given for denying him treatment.

He sued and won twice. By the time he was enrolled, 506 days had passed. He was released the day he completed the program.

New York law is not vague here. For a court-ordered drug sentence, the statute caps the stop at the reception center at ten days. For shock incarceration, the law says a person sentenced by a judge shall promptly commence the program, and it specifically overrides the usual line that participation is only a privilege.

The state's own numbers show what delay costs. In six months last year, 244 people were ruled non-certifiable for an earned eligibility certificate for a single reason: they were still sitting in reception. They were approved for parole at 24.6 percent. People who held a certificate were approved at 66.5 percent.

If you or someone you love was sentenced to a program and has not been placed, ask the attorney for the deadline in writing and for what gets filed the day it passes. Columbia's Jailhouse Lawyer's Manual has free chapters on the grievance process and on Article 78, and both are linked at the bottom of the full article.

## 3. clean-slate-lost-earnings-20-billion-ny
Image: assets/social/clean-slate-lost-earnings-20-billion-ny-og.png

Caption:
Old records cost New Yorkers an estimated $20.9 billion a year. Most of it is tied to records the state has already promised to seal.

A national study The Clean Slate Initiative released this summer followed people who had an arrest or conviction when they were young and then stayed out of trouble for more than ten years. They still earned 15 to 37 percent less than people with no record. For New York, the study puts the total at $20.9 billion a year.

New York already seals an arrest that ends without a conviction, and employers are not allowed to ask about it. Most of the rest is tied to convictions the Clean Slate Act covers: three years after sentencing or release for a misdemeanor, eight for a felony. The state has until November 16, 2027 to finish sealing older records.

If you have an old New York record, the article links to our explainers on when it should seal and what blocks it.

## 5. nyc-jobs-center-city-jobs-criminal-record-ny
Image: assets/social/nyc-jobs-center-city-jobs-criminal-record-ny-og.png

Caption:
Applying for a city job with a record? Here's what happens after the offer.

NYC opened a walk-in jobs center on October 5, and the line went down the block.

For most jobs, the city can't ask about your record until after it makes you an offer. Then it fingerprints every new hire. It can only weigh felonies and unsealed misdemeanors, and before it pulls an offer it has to give you its reasons in writing and time to respond. Use that time, and answer in writing.

The courts haven't finished sealing old records under Clean Slate. They have until November 2027. If your record qualifies but hasn't sealed, the city can still see it. A Certificate of Relief from Disabilities creates a legal presumption that you've been rehabilitated, and it's worth getting before you apply.

## 8. treatment-court-expansion-act-mental-health-diversion-ny
Image: assets/social/treatment-court-expansion-act-mental-health-diversion-ny-og.png

Caption:
If your county has no mental health court, the case can move next door. Only if you ask, and only if both DAs agree.

About half of New York's counties don't have a mental health court. Since December 2025, a law lets a defendant outside New York City ask to transfer the case to one in a neighboring county. Both district attorneys have to sign off, and nobody else has to bring it up.

At the Brooklyn Mental Health Court, the median wait from arrest to first contact with the court's clinical staff was 265 days last year.

A bill to put a diversion court in every county has been around since 2019. It has never had a floor vote, and the Senate refiled it again on September 23.
