## LinkedIn

Jose Saldaña says everyone in that parole waiting room already knew who had the better odds. The data says they were right.

He sat in front of New York's Board of Parole four times. Thirty-eight years inside before he got out in 2018. His description of the waiting room is that you look around, you count the three to five white guys in there with you, and everybody has the same thought at the same time.

NYU School of Law's Center on Race, Inequality, and the Law put numbers to it, using parole board data obtained from the Vera Institute. Across 2022 through March 2025, the Board released people of color at a rate about 33 percent lower than white applicants. In the 2016 to 2021 window, that gap was 18.8 percent. It has widened, and the report says the timing coincides with Governor Hochul's time in office.

Translated into people: had people of color been released at the same rate as white applicants, the report estimates over 1,300 additional parole grants between 2022 and June 2024 alone, and over 3,650 going back to 2016.

DOCCS says the Board cannot and does not decide on race, and points to the statutory factors members are required to weigh. The Governor's office notes the Board is independent. It's also true that its 16 members are gubernatorial nominees, and as of the report's release all but four were sitting on expired terms.

One thing worth separating out, because it gets conflated constantly: this is the release decision, not the violation process. Before anyone can violate parole they have to be granted it first, and this is the stage where the gap concentrates.

Fair and Timely Parole and Elder Parole both target this stage. Alvin Bragg, Darcel Clark, and Eric Gonzalez have endorsed both.

A denial doesn't pause the clock on someone's life. It resets it, during the exact window when a job and an apartment are what keep someone from coming back.

## Facebook

Jose Saldaña went in front of New York's parole board four times before they let him out. Thirty-eight years. He describes sitting in the waiting room with twenty other people, looking at the handful of white men in the room, and everyone silently doing the same math about who was going home.

A report out of NYU School of Law says that math was correct. Using state parole data from the Vera Institute, it found the Board released people of color at a rate roughly 33 percent lower than white applicants between 2022 and March 2025. Before 2022, the gap was about 18.8 percent. It got worse, not better.

Put in terms of actual people: if the rates had matched, the report estimates more than 3,650 additional people would have been granted parole since 2016.

The state's response is that race isn't a factor and the Board weighs the required statutory criteria. The report's co-author, Jason Williamson, told reporters there's no rational explanation for a gap that size.

This is about the decision to release someone, which is a different thing from a parole violation after release. Two stages, two sets of rules, and this is the one where the disparity shows up in the state's own numbers.

If someone you love has a hearing coming, this doesn't make it hopeless. It makes preparation matter more.
