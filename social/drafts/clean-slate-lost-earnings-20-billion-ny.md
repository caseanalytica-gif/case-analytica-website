## LinkedIn

**A new study prices New York's old criminal records at $20.9 billion a year in lost earnings. $17.5 billion of it sits on convictions the state has until November 2027 to seal.**

---

The Clean Slate Initiative released The Economic Losses from Arrest & Conviction Records in August, and it is now running as a paid TikTok ad. The study follows people who had an arrest or conviction in early adulthood and then stayed crime-free for more than a decade. From ages 26 to 40 they still earned 14.9 to 36.7 percent less than matched peers with no record.

Its state table puts New York fourth in the country at $20.94 billion a year: $3.41 billion tied to arrests with no conviction, $11.03 billion to misdemeanors, $4.53 billion to non-violent felonies and $1.98 billion to violent felonies.

The report also says plainly that it could not account for state sealing laws. New York has two that matter here.

CPL 160.50 already seals an arrest that ends in the person's favor, including one the prosecutor declines to bring. Executive Law 296(16) bars employers, landlords, lenders and insurers from asking about it. So the ad's lead example, one arrest with no conviction, is a case New York law already treats as if it never happened.

The other $17.5 billion is tied to convictions, which is what the Clean Slate Act was written for. The people in the study are past both waiting periods, three years for a misdemeanor and eight for a felony. Under CPL 160.57 the court system has until November 16, 2027 to finish sealing convictions that predate the law.

The report cannot say what the backlog itself costs. It does measure the penalty sealing is meant to end, for people who look a lot like the ones still waiting.

## Facebook

**Old records cost New Yorkers an estimated $20.9 billion a year. Most of it is tied to records the state has already promised to seal.**

---

A national study The Clean Slate Initiative released this summer followed people who had an arrest or conviction when they were young and then stayed out of trouble for more than ten years. They still earned 15 to 37 percent less than people with no record. For New York, the study puts the total at $20.9 billion a year.

New York already seals an arrest that ends without a conviction, and employers are not allowed to ask about it. Most of the rest is tied to convictions the Clean Slate Act covers: three years after sentencing or release for a misdemeanor, eight for a felony. The state has until November 16, 2027 to finish sealing older records.

If you have an old New York record, the article links to our explainers on when it should seal and what blocks it.

## Note on tone

The piece credits The Clean Slate Initiative's number and quotes its own caveat rather than
debunking it. Keep it that way in the posts: the report is not wrong, it just cannot see New
York's laws. It also keeps the Brennan Center's $12.6 billion separate from the new estimate,
since the two measure different populations. And it does not urge anyone to back S6492 or
contact lawmakers; it reports that the bill is in committee. That keeps it clear of ANF's
lobbying bar.
