# New York's Second Look Act (S158 / A1283)

## LinkedIn

A bill that had gone nowhere since 2021 just got its first real vote. Most people haven't heard about it.

On May 18, 2026, the New York Senate's Codes Committee voted 9 to 4 to advance the Second Look Act. That's further than the bill has ever gotten.

Here's what S158 would actually do. If you've served ten years in state prison, or half of a sentence of ten years or more, whichever is less, you could apply to have your sentence reduced. The application goes to a different judge than the one who sentenced you. You'd have a right to counsel for it.

It isn't automatic. A judge weighs a specific list: your record and rehabilitation while inside, the circumstances of the offense and your actual role in it, health evaluations, input from the victim or their family, whether the original sentence was inflated because you went to trial instead of taking a plea, and what continued incarceration costs the state. There's a rebuttable presumption in favor for people 55 and older, and for anyone who was 25 or younger at the time of the offense. The court cannot increase a sentence through this process.

One provision worth naming: if you wanted programming and your facility didn't offer it, the bill says that gap can't be held against you.

Chief Judge Rowan Wilson has publicly urged the Legislature to pass it. Support also comes from a former DOCCS commissioner, a former NYC corrections and probation commissioner, and the American Bar Association. A Vera Institute poll cited by Brooklyn Law School put general public support at 68 percent.

It still hasn't had a floor vote in either chamber. Nobody should plan around CPL 440.45 existing until it's signed. But knowing what the bill says is worth more than knowing that people are talking about it.

## Facebook

If someone you love has been inside ten years or more, there's a bill in Albany you should know the details of.

The Second Look Act would let a person who has served at least ten years apply to have their sentence reduced. Not to the judge who sentenced them. A different judge. And they'd have a right to a lawyer for the application.

On May 18, 2026, it cleared the Senate Codes Committee 9 to 4. It's been introduced in some form since 2021 and never made it this far.

It's not a release valve. The judge has to weigh what the person has done inside, the offense itself, health, victim input, whether the sentence was longer because they took the case to trial, and what more prison time costs the state. People 55 and older, and people who were 25 or younger when the offense happened, get a presumption in their favor. The court can't add time.

One piece I want to flag: if the programming wasn't available in the facility, the bill says a person can't be penalized for not completing it. That happens constantly and it's usually treated as the person's failure.

This is not law yet. It's cleared one committee and sits in another. But it's the furthest it has ever gotten, and it's worth calling your state senator about.
