## LinkedIn

**A commissioner told my client he wasn't safe to release because his mother had a record. It never made the written decision.**

---

New York's Correction Law § 805 flips the usual parole default. Complete your assigned program, get an Earned Eligibility Certificate from DOCCS, and the statute says you "shall be granted parole release" unless the Board makes an affirmative, on-the-record finding against you. The burden moves off the applicant and onto the panel. Executive Law § 259-i(2)(c)(A) then limits what that finding can rest on: institutional programming, disciplinary record, release plans, the offense itself. A fixed list. In King v. New York State Division of Parole, the Court of Appeals held that a proper hearing considers "only the relevant guidelines."

A third party's criminal history is not on that list.

I had a client, and I'll call him Mr. Big here because his details are changed, who was entitled to a merit interview tied to his program compliance. It never got calendared. He only got it after raising the issue himself with his ORC. That's the first thing worth knowing: the benefit the statute hands you without a fight often still requires a fight to schedule.

At his earlier interview he was denied, and a commissioner said his mother had a criminal record and that he would not be safe in the community because of his family. Spoken, not written. Which means it appeared nowhere in the paperwork an appeal or an Article 78 petition would normally have to work with.

So going into the merit interview, we prepared two sentences. Use them only if the same pattern starts up again.

"Do you have my EEC on record?"

"This interview has to be about my reentry readiness: I have a place to stay and a job offer."

That's the whole tactic. Neither is clever. Both are short, factual, and hard to answer without either engaging the statute or visibly refusing to. The first one gets a "yes" on the transcript that's difficult to write around later. The second names the § 259-i factors in the applicant's own words.

He used both. The commissioner ended the interview, cut the video feed, left him on hold about ten minutes, came back only to hang up. Fourteen days later the Board granted release.

Nobody outside that room knows what happened in those ten minutes, and one case is not a formula. I've watched the same shape play out with other clients, but I'm not going to pretend that settles causation. What I will say is that a Board with nothing on the record capable of surviving review tends to behave like one.

The Board hears roughly 10,000 cases a year. Most end in denial, and a Vera Institute review of 168 transcripts found commissioners denying people with low risk scores, finished programming, and workable release plans, focused on who someone used to be. The EEC exists to counteract exactly that. It only does the job when the interview record makes the statute impossible to ignore.

If you finish your assigned program in New York and DOCCS issues you an Earned Eligibility Certificate, the law says release is supposed to be the default. Correction Law § 805 puts the burden on the Board to justify keeping you, not on you to earn your way out. And the Board can only base that on a short list of things: your programming, your disciplinary record, your release plan, the offense.

Your mother's record is not on that list.

A client of mine, whose details I've changed, got denied at an interview where a commissioner said his family made him unsafe for the community. That comment never showed up in the written decision. Which is the part people miss. If it isn't on the record, an appeal has nothing to point at.

He was also owed a merit interview that DOCCS never calendared. He had to ask for it himself.

When he finally got in the room, he had two sentences ready:

"Do you have my EEC on record?"

"This interview has to be about my reentry readiness: I have a place to stay and a job offer."

The commissioner ended the interview and cut the feed. Two weeks later, the Board granted release.

One case proves nothing on its own, and I'm not selling this as a guarantee. But if someone you love has an EEC and a hearing coming up, two things are worth doing right now. Confirm the merit interview is actually on the calendar, because it may not happen automatically. And have a one-sentence release plan ready, out loud, in their own words.
