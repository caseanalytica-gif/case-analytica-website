Does the Parole Board give the reasons the law requires?

Read the updates Support this reporting

New York law says a person who holds an Earned Eligibility Certificate "shall be granted parole release" at the end of their minimum term, unless the Parole Board makes two specific findings. Between October 2025 and March 2026, the Board denied certificate holders 402 times, by the state prison system's own count. This project reads what the Board wrote in those denials, and reports whether the reasons the law requires are there.

What the law says

Correction Law 805 covers "an incarcerated individual who is serving a sentence with a minimum term of not more than eight years and who has been issued a certificate of earned eligibility." That person "shall be granted parole release at the expiration of his or her minimum term" unless the Board determines "that there is a reasonable probability that, if such incarcerated individual is released, he or she will not live and remain at liberty without violating the law and that his or her release is not compatible with the welfare of society."

The statute joins those two findings with "and." To deny a certificate holder, the Board has to make both.

Who this covers

The presumption reaches only indeterminate sentences with a minimum term of eight years or less. A determinate sentence does not go to the Board for discretionary release. The eight-year threshold is in effect until September 1, 2027, when a version with a six-year threshold is scheduled to take effect.

What is already known

The Department of Corrections and Community Supervision publishes a report on the Earned Eligibility Program twice a year. The most recent one, covering October 2025 through March 2026, counts 1,102 initial and merit interviews of certificate holders. The Board approved 700 and denied 402. For certificate holders convicted of violent offenses, 26 of 75 were approved. The report before it, April through September 2025, counted 369 denials in 1,101 interviews. DOCCS counts Board appearances, not individual people.

Why there is reason to look

  • The Board's own Appeals Unit has found the problem. In one published appeal challenging an April 2019 denial, the Appeals Unit found "the Board failed to consider the fact that Appellant was issued an Earned Eligibility Certificate" and recommended a new interview.
  • The complaint is common enough to have its own tag. Fordham Law's archive of parole administrative appeal decisions tags each appeal by its grounds. One tag reads "Earned Eligibility Certificate (EEC) or Limited Time Credit Allowance (LCTA) was ignored."
  • Researchers have documented boilerplate in denials. "The Problem with Parole," by NYU Law's Center on Race, Inequality, and the Law and the Parole Preparation Project, reports that written denials "typically use boilerplate language that tracks the statute." That report centers on people serving long and life sentences, most of whom fall outside the 805 threshold. Nobody has asked the question of this population specifically, as far as I can find.

What the reporting will do

  1. Collect every published Appeals Unit decision, and every decision in the Fordham archive, that raises an Earned Eligibility Certificate. Code each one for whether the Board's underlying decision stated both 805 findings. The coding rules publish before the count does.
  2. Read the court decisions that reviewed denials of certificate holders.
  3. Interview people denied parole while holding a certificate, their families, and the attorneys who handle their appeals.
  4. Put the findings to DOCCS and the Board for comment before publishing.

The results go into the site's parole data record, whose methodology and field definitions were published before any data went in. The findings publish whatever they show, including if the Board turns out to be stating both findings.

What this project will not do

  • Give legal advice or take on anyone's case. I am not an attorney. This is reporting.
  • Publish anyone's decision without their agreement. I use a written Board decision about a named person only when that person agrees to it.
  • Claim more than the evidence supports. Published appeals are a self-selected set, so this project can show what the Board writes in those cases. It cannot give a rate for every denial statewide, and it will say so wherever the numbers appear.
