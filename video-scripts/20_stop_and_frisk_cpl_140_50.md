# 20: Can NYPD Stop and Frisk You Without a Reason?

**Format:** Short · ~75 sec | **Pillar:** Rights & Process | **Slot:** Week 7 (new batch, unscheduled)

## Script

**Hook (0:00)**
"Reasonable suspicion" gets said like it explains everything. It does not. It is a specific legal standard, and whether an officer actually had it decided one of the biggest civil rights rulings against the NYPD ever handed down.

**Body (0:10)**
The law is Criminal Procedure Law 140.50. It lets an officer stop you in public if they reasonably suspect you are committing, have committed, or are about to commit a felony or a penal law misdemeanor, and ask your name, address, and an explanation. A frisk is a separate step, needing its own reasonable suspicion that you are a danger, and it is only supposed to be a pat-down for weapons, not a full search. In 2013, a federal judge ruled in Floyd versus City of New York that the NYPD had a pattern of suspicionless stops and was engaged in what the court itself called indirect racial profiling. The statute did not get struck down. What that ruling means is the standard has to actually be met, stop by stop, not assumed because of a neighborhood or a description.

**Self-advocacy line (0:55)**
If you are stopped, ask calmly: "Am I being detained, or am I free to go?" If you are frisked, you can say clearly that you do not consent to any search beyond a pat-down for weapons, without resisting. That does not stop the moment, but it preserves the record for later.

**CTA (1:10)**
