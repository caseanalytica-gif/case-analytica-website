Data

New York parole data, and how it is kept.

Numbers about parole in New York exist, but they sit scattered across reports, budget testimony and single news stories, each one a sentence inside something else. Nobody can chart them, cite them cleanly, or check this year against last year. This page is an attempt to keep them in one place, with the source attached to every figure.

The methodology and the field definitions below were published before any data went in, on purpose, so that the record was defined before it was filled rather than after. Seven figures are in so far. Each was read at its source on the date its row shows, and the gaps are listed at the foot of the page rather than left implied.

Why parole

Parole is the point in a New York case where the gap between what the statute says and what happens in the room is widest, and where the least is published. A person can hold an Earned Eligibility Certificate that creates a statutory presumption of release under Correction Law 805 and still be denied in a twenty minute interview, with a written decision that never explains how the presumption was overcome. That is a measurable thing, and almost nobody measures it.

The Vera Institute maintains New York State parole release rates, which is the closest existing thing to this and is a better source than anything here for statewide release percentages. This page is not trying to duplicate it. It tracks a narrower question: what the Board actually writes down, and whether the reasoning in a denial survives contact with the statute.

What counts as a source

Every figure carries a named, public, checkable source. In descending order of preference:

  1. The agency's own published data. DOCCS, the Board of Parole, DCJS.
  2. Sworn testimony or a budget document. What an agency told the legislature, on the record.
  3. A named research institution's published report, with the report itself linked, not a press summary of it.
  4. A named news outlet's reporting, where the outlet obtained the figure directly.
  5. A written Board decision, quoted, where the person it concerns has agreed to it being used.

What does not qualify: an aggregate with no underlying source, a number repeated between outlets with no origin, an advocacy organisation's figure that cannot be traced to its own published data, and any estimate of mine. If a number cannot be traced, it does not go in the table. It gets noted here as a gap instead, which is more useful than a plausible figure.

What each field means

Every row is one figure, not one person and not one hearing.

  • Measure. What is being counted, in plain words. "People returned to prison for a technical parole violation," not "revocation volume."
  • Value. The figure as the source states it. Never rounded, never converted, never recalculated into a different unit.
  • Period. The span the figure covers, as the source defines it. A calendar year and a fiscal year are different and are not silently merged.
  • Population. Who is counted. Statewide, a single facility, one offence class, one demographic group. This is the field most often left vague elsewhere and it is where most apples-to-oranges comparisons come from.
  • Source. The organisation and the document, linked directly to the document.
  • Retrieved. The date the figure was read from that source. Not the date the source was published.
  • Note. Any caveat the source itself attaches, carried over rather than dropped. If the source says its own count is provisional, the row says so.

Why "Retrieved" is separate from the publication date

Agencies revise figures after publishing them. A number read in March can differ from the same number read in September from the same page. Recording when it was read is what makes a disagreement between two versions traceable instead of confusing.

How often this is updated, and what that means

There is no fixed schedule, and claiming one would be the same dishonesty as a review date that nothing stands behind. Each row carries its own Retrieved date and that date is the only promise being made about it. A row read in 2026 and never rechecked says exactly that.

This is the same discipline the articles use. See how these articles are researched and sourced for the reasoning.

How to cite this

Cite the underlying source, not this page. Every row links to the document the figure came from, and that document is what belongs in a filing, an article or a brief. This page is an index, and an index is not an authority.

If you need to point at the compilation itself, for example to say where you found a set of figures gathered together:

Case Analytica, New York Parole Data, caseanalytica.com/parole-data.html, retrieved [date].

What this is not

  • Not a complete picture of parole in New York. It holds what has been found and verified, which is less than what exists.
  • Not a prediction. Nothing here estimates what a Board will do in a particular case.
  • Not legal advice, and not a substitute for an attorney or an advocate at a hearing.
  • Not anonymised research. Where a written Board decision is quoted, it is quoted with the agreement of the person it concerns, and identifying detail is kept out unless they have asked otherwise.

Known gaps

Figures that exist in Case Analytica's published articles but are not in the table yet, because they have not been re-read at their original source: technical parole violation returns for 2018 and their share of all parole reincarcerations, the size of the population under active parole supervision, medical parole approval rates for 2013 through 2015, and the racial disparity in New York City jail holds for technical violations. Each is cited in the relevant article. They go in once verified, not before.

Corrections are welcome and wanted. If a figure here is wrong, or its source has been revised, say so: caseanalytica@gmail.com.
