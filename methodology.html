Methodology

How these articles are researched, sourced, and corrected.

This site tells people what New York law says at moments when being wrong costs them something real. A page that does that owes you an account of where the information comes from and who checked it. This is that account.

Who writes this

Dean Mustaphalli writes every article. I am not an attorney, and nothing here is legal advice. I served time in New York State prison after a felony conviction. While I was inside I ran a transition program, helping other men prepare for parole board hearings and work on their appeals in the law library. I won my own parole board appeal. That is the experience this site is built on, and it is also the reason the site does not pretend to be a substitute for a lawyer. Knowing which question to ask is not the same as being able to answer it for your case.

My full background, including the conviction, is on the about page. I put it there because you should be able to weigh who is telling you this.

What "Reflects law as of" means

Every article carries a date under the byline reading Reflects law as of [date]. That date is the last time the article's legal claims were checked against the actual statute or primary source.

On many articles that date is the same as the publication date. That is not an oversight. It means the piece has not been rechecked since it was written, and you are seeing that plainly instead of seeing a freshness badge that was refreshed by a script. New York criminal law changes constantly. Bail rules have been amended more than once since 2019, Clean Slate is still being implemented, and at least one provision this site explains is scheduled to be repealed in 2027. A date that means nothing would be worse than no date at all.

When an article is reviewed against the statute, the date moves. When it has not been, it does not.

How an article gets sourced

The standard is primary sources. Statutes link to the section text on the New York Senate's own legislation site. Court rules, locations and hours link to the Unified Court System. Cases link to the opinion. Reporting links to the outlet that did the work, named, with the reporter credited where the piece is the origin of a quote or a figure.

Every article ends with a Sources list collecting those citations in one place, so you can check the article without hunting links out of the prose.

Three rules govern what goes in:

  • No invented numbers. No statistic, case outcome, statute detail, dollar figure or date gets written unless it traces to a real source. Where a number is my own estimate rather than a sourced figure, the article says so in those words.
  • A link is not a check. Confirming that a statute exists is not the same as confirming the article describes it correctly. The claim gets read against the section text, not just cited near it.
  • The statute wins. When the text of the law contradicts something already published here, the article changes.

Corrections

A sourcing review of the whole site in September 2026 found seven substantive errors and fixed them. Every one was in an article that had cited nothing external. Among them: this site told readers the Clean Slate sealing clock starts once someone is off parole, when the statute starts it at release from incarceration, which would have led someone on parole to believe their eight years had not begun. It described the federal reporting limit in a way that let a reader conclude a conviction ages off after seven years, when convictions are carved out of that limit by name. It gave a bail bondsman's fee as "around 10 percent" without saying that the fee is capped by statute on a sliding scale, which on a $50,000 bail is the difference between $5,000 and $3,260.

Those are named here rather than quietly patched because a site that claims never to be wrong is not telling you the truth about how this work goes. Errors get fixed in place, the as-of date moves, and the change is recorded in the site's public commit history.

If you find something wrong, say so: caseanalytica@gmail.com. Point at the sentence. I would rather hear it from you than leave it up.

What this site will not do

  • Give legal advice. Articles explain what the law says and end with the exact question to ask your attorney. They do not tell you what to do in your case, because that depends on facts I do not have and judgment I am not licensed to exercise.
  • Argue a specific person's guilt or innocence. Articles anchored to a real case stay on the systemic question and use the same "alleged" standard the courts do.
  • Use a booking photo, ever. No mugshots. No images of minors, regardless of source. Photos of people run only where the image is genuinely public and credited to where it came from.
  • Name and shame an individual judge or prosecutor. The criticism here is of how the system works, not of who happened to be standing in it.

Why any of this matters

Most people meet the criminal legal system once, at the worst moment of their life, with no idea what the next step is. The gap between what the law allows and what anyone tells you is where this site lives. That only works if what is on the page is right, and if you can check it yourself when it counts.

Case Analytica: general information about the New York State justice system, not legal advice for your specific case. Always confirm specifics with a licensed attorney.
