Glossary

The words a New York criminal case actually uses.

Nobody explains these to you, and most of them get used in front of you as if you already know them. These are the ones that come up on this site, defined once. Where a term has its own article, it is linked. Where it has a statute, that is linked too, so you can read the text yourself.

730 exam
A competency examination under CPL Article 730. If there is reason to think someone cannot understand the proceedings or assist in their own defense, the case pauses while they are examined. A finding of unfit does not end the case, it stops the clock.Full explainer  ·  CPL 730
ACD
Adjournment in Contemplation of Dismissal. The case is adjourned, usually six months or a year, and if nothing goes wrong it is dismissed and sealed. Not a conviction and not an admission.Full explainer  ·  CPL 170.55
Arraignment
The first appearance before a judge. The charges are read, a plea is entered, almost always not guilty at this stage, and the judge decides on release or bail. In New York a delay past 24 hours is presumptively unreasonable.Full explainer
Bench warrant
A warrant a judge issues when someone does not appear for a court date. It does not expire on its own, and how it is handled depends heavily on whether the person comes in voluntarily.Full explainer
Certificate of Relief from Disabilities
A court order lifting most automatic bars to employment and licensing that a conviction triggers. Covers one conviction, so more than one conviction means more than one application. Sometimes shortened to CRD or COR.Full explainer  ·  Correction Law 701
Clean Slate Act
New York's automatic sealing law, in effect since November 16, 2024. Three years for a misdemeanor and eight for a felony, both measured from release from incarceration. The courts have until November 16, 2027 to build the system that does the sealing.Full explainer  ·  CPL 160.57
Conditional discharge
A sentence with conditions but no probation officer. Different from probation, which carries supervision, reporting, and its own violation process.Full explainer  ·  Penal Law 65.05
CPL
Criminal Procedure Law. The New York statute governing how a criminal case runs, from arrest through appeal. Cited constantly on this site.
CPLR
Civil Practice Law and Rules. The civil counterpart to the CPL. It matters in a criminal context mainly through civil asset forfeiture under Article 13-A.Full explainer
DAT
Desk Appearance Ticket. Release at the precinct with a date to appear instead of being held for arraignment. A DAT case can sometimes be resolved through Project Reset before it ever reaches court.Full explainer
DCJS
New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services. Holds the state's criminal history records and is where a record review request goes.
Discovery
The material the prosecution has to turn over: police reports, body camera footage, witness statements, lab results. New York's discovery rules were rewritten in 2019 and carry real deadlines.Full explainer
Diversion
Any track that resolves a case without a standard conviction, usually by completing conditions. Judicial diversion for felony drug cases is the statutory version; many other programs are local and discretionary.Full explainer  ·  CPL Article 216
DOCCS
New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision. Runs the state prisons and supervises people on parole and post-release supervision.
DVSJA
Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act. Lets a judge impose a lighter sentence when abuse was a significant factor in the offense. It has to be requested; it is not applied automatically.Full explainer  ·  Penal Law 60.12
EEC
Earned Eligibility Certificate. Issued by DOCCS for program compliance. Under Correction Law 805 it creates a presumption of release at the minimum sentence, which the Board has to specifically overcome on the record.Full explainer  ·  Correction Law 805
Expungement
Destroying a record so it no longer exists. New York almost never does this. What New York does is sealing, which hides a record from most public view while leaving it intact. The marijuana expungements under the MRTA are the main exception.Full explainer
Grand jury
A panel that decides whether there is enough evidence to indict on a felony. A defendant has a statutory right to testify before it, but only if notice is filed in time, and that window is short.Full explainer  ·  CPL 190.50
HALT Act
The Humane Alternatives to Long-Term Solitary Confinement Act, which limits segregated confinement in New York. Parts of it have been suspended.Full explainer
Indictment
The grand jury's formal charge in a felony case. Until there is an indictment the case runs on a felony complaint, which is what the 180.80 clock attaches to.Full explainer
Judicial diversion
The statutory track that lets a judge send an eligible felony drug defendant to treatment instead of prison, in some cases over the prosecutor's objection.Full explainer  ·  CPL Article 216
Juvenile offender
A defined category for the youngest defendants charged in adult court, set out at CPL 1.20(42). Distinct from youthful offender status, which is a sentencing outcome.Full explainer  ·  CPL 1.20
Less Is More Act
The 2021 law limiting incarceration for technical parole violations and adding due process to revocation. It changed what a technical violation can cost, not whether one can be charged.Full explainer
MRTA
Marijuana Regulation and Taxation Act. Automatically expunged many pre-2021 marijuana convictions. Whether a specific conviction was cleared depends on the exact Penal Law section it was under.Full explainer
Order of protection
A court order restricting contact. A full stay-away order and a limited order are different things, and violating either is a separate criminal charge that does not make the original case go away.Full explainer
Parole
Release under supervision before the maximum expires, decided by the Board of Parole. Distinct from post-release supervision, which is built into a determinate sentence rather than granted.Full explainer  ·  Executive Law 259-i
Predicate
A prior conviction that raises the sentencing range on a new case. Whether a prior counts as a predicate turns on its class and how long ago it was.
Remand
Held without the option of bail. Different from having bail set at an amount you cannot pay, though the practical result can look the same.Full explainer
ROR
Released on Own Recognizance. Released without money bail on a promise to return. Since the 2019 reform this is the default for most misdemeanors and non-violent felonies.Full explainer  ·  CPL 510.10
Sealing
Hiding a record from most public view while leaving it intact for courts, law enforcement, and certain licensing purposes. New York seals in several different ways, and they have different rules.Full explainer  ·  CPL 160.50
Source hearing
A hearing on where bail money came from. It can keep someone in custody for a week or more even when the bail amount itself was never the real obstacle.Full explainer
Speedy trial
The prosecution's clock to be ready for trial, counted in days and subject to excludable time. The statutory clock under CPL 30.30 is separate from the constitutional right under CPL 30.20.Full explainer  ·  CPL 30.30
Suppression
A motion to exclude evidence obtained unlawfully. If a stop or search cannot be justified on the record, what it produced can go with it. It has to be raised before a plea.Full explainer
Technical violation
A parole or probation violation that is not a new crime: a missed curfew, a failed check-in, a missed appointment. What it can cost changed under the Less Is More Act.Full explainer
Vacate
To undo a conviction after the fact, on grounds that were never in front of the judge. The motion is made under CPL 440.10 and it has procedural traps that can close the door permanently.Full explainer  ·  CPL 440.10
Youthful offender
A sentencing outcome, decided by the judge at sentencing, that replaces a conviction with a youthful offender finding. No criminal conviction on the record and the case is sealed. It has to be asked for.Full explainer  ·  CPL Article 720
