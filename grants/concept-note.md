# Concept note: Does an Earned Eligibility Certificate actually get people home?

Case Analytica, a fiscally sponsored project of the Alternative Newsweekly Foundation
(501(c)(3), EIN 30-0100369). Contact: Dean Mustaphalli, [email], [phone].

## The problem

New York Correction Law § 805 says a person who holds an Earned Eligibility Certificate "shall be
granted parole release at the expiration of his or her minimum term." The Board of Parole can
deny that person only by finding, on the record, that there is a reasonable probability the
person "will not live and remain at liberty without violating the law" and that release "is not
compatible with the welfare of society." The statute joins the two findings with "and."

People with an EEC are denied anyway, often after an interview of about twenty minutes, in
written decisions that never say how the presumption was overcome. Nobody publishes how often
this happens. DOCCS and the Board report release rates, but not release rates for EEC holders,
and not whether denial decisions make the two findings the statute requires.

## The pilot

A [six]-month pilot that answers the question with documents and helps the people it concerns
while it does.

1. **Collect.** Gather written Board decisions from EEC holders, with each person's consent,
   through readers, families and mail into DOCCS facilities. File a Freedom of Information Law
   request with DOCCS and the Board for the number of EEC holders interviewed and released, by
   year.
2. **Read every decision against the statute.** Record, for each denial, whether the Board made
   both § 805 findings, what reason it gave, and whether the reason concerns the present or only
   the offense. The coding sheet is published before the first decision is read, the same way
   the site's parole data page was built.
3. **Prepare people before the interview.** Mail a packet to EEC holders with an upcoming
   interview: the site's EEC interview guide, the parole file worksheet and the hearing
   preparation guide. Each fits DOCCS's five-page limit for printed mail.
4. **Publish and deliver.** Post the findings on the site's New York parole data page, write
   them up as reporting, and send them to the Senate and Assembly corrections committees and to
   parole appeal attorneys.

## What the pilot will produce

- [40] written decisions collected and coded, published with names removed.
- One FOIL request filed and its response published, including any refusal.
- [100] preparation packets mailed into DOCCS facilities.
- One public report and at least [3] articles on the findings.
- A record of every EEC holder in the pilot who was released, and of every one who appealed a
  denial.

## Who runs it

Dean Mustaphalli founded Case Analytica and writes all of its reporting. He served time in New
York State prison. While he was inside he ran a transition program, helping other men prepare for
parole board hearings and work on their appeals in the law library. He won his own parole board
appeal and came home in December 2023. He is not an attorney, and the pilot gives no legal advice.
When a person needs representation, the pilot refers them to one.

## What already exists

- 83 published articles as of October 11, 2026, including guides to the EEC at a parole
  interview, reading the parole file, hearing preparation and the racial gap in release rates.
- A public New York parole data page with a methodology published before any figure went in.
  Every figure carries its source and the date it was read.
- A sourcing review in September 2026 that found and corrected seven errors, with every
  correction made in public.
- [Monthly readers, email subscribers, and intake calls or letters from people facing parole.]
