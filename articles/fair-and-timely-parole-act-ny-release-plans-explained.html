New York's Parole Board Can Deny You Even With a Job and an Apartment Lined Up

New York law already tells the Board of Parole to look at whether someone has a place to live and a job lined up before deciding whether to release them. It's written directly into Executive Law § 259-i: release plans, including community support, employment, education, and reentry services, are one of the factors commissioners are supposed to weigh. So is participation in programming, and so are letters of support from staff and other people who know the person.

None of that stops the Board from denying release anyway, over the crime itself, a fact that never changes no matter how much time has passed or how ready someone is to leave. In a 2021 analysis of 168 parole hearing transcripts that all ended in denial, the Vera Institute of Justice found that commissioners justified 60 percent of those denials based solely on the original crime, even though 85 percent of the people in that sample had risk and needs assessment scores indicating they were ready for release. A more recent sponsor memo for the state's reform bill puts the current share of denials tied to the nature of the original offense at 90 percent. Most of the people in Vera's sample had participated in prison programming, held jobs while incarcerated, and shown up with detailed housing and employment plans. They were denied anyway.

This is a different question from who gets released and who doesn't by race, which Case Analytica has covered separately, and it's a different question from what happens to someone already out on parole, covered in Case Analytica's piece on the Less Is More Act. This is about the release decision itself, and about a specific, well-documented pattern: the law already asks the Board to weigh a person's readiness to work and to house themselves, and the Board routinely doesn't.

What the law actually asks commissioners to weigh

Under Executive Law § 259-i(2)(c)(A), the Board of Parole is required to consider several categories of evidence at a release hearing: the person's institutional record, including program participation and vocational training; release plans, including community support, employment, and reentry services; any deportation order; the sentence they'd face under current determinate-sentencing law for certain drug felonies; any victim statement; the seriousness of the offense; and the person's prior criminal record. The statute doesn't rank these factors. It leaves commissioners free to weigh the seriousness of a decades-old offense as heavily as they want, against a release plan built around a confirmed job and a lease.

That discretion is the whole problem, according to Vera's 2021 report, Toward a Fairer Parole Process. "The current parole process in New York is very focused on who someone was, rather than who someone is now," Benjamin Heller, one of the report's authors, told Vera. "For most of the people in our sample, the crime that we're talking about happened decades ago." Heller's team found that when commissioners cited public safety, in many of those cases the person's own COMPAS risk assessment score directly contradicted that reasoning. The board isn't required to explain why it's discounting a positive risk score in favor of the original charge. It just can.

The throughline

A confirmed job and a confirmed address are two of the strongest predictors that someone stays out of the system after release. New York's own parole statute already says so, by listing them as factors the Board has to consider. But when a person shows up to a hearing with both of those things already lined up, and gets denied anyway over an offense from years or decades earlier, the state isn't protecting public safety. It's manufacturing more time inside for someone who was already ready to leave, which is the opposite of what a release plan is supposed to buy you.

The release rate has been falling, not rising

Every year, between 10,000 and 12,000 people appear before New York's Board of Parole to make their case for release, according to a September 2023 Vera Institute fact sheet. The statewide release rate rose from 30 percent in 2017 to 42 percent in 2018, held near 40 percent for two years, then fell to 36 percent in 2021 and 33 percent in 2022, ticking back up to 38 percent for the first half of 2023. New York's rate isn't the worst in the country. It's also nowhere close to the 60 and 70 percent release rates some other states report.

The same fact sheet found that in 2022, less than a quarter of people who had been eligible for parole for more than ten years were actually released. People under 25 have the lowest release rates of any age group, between 20 and 30 percent. People 55 and older fare somewhat better, but more of them are still denied than granted parole even at that age, despite state data showing a very small share of people 50 and older return to custody for a new conviction within three years of release.

A bill to change the standard has been stuck for years

State Senator Julia Salazar has sponsored a version of the Fair and Timely Parole Act in every legislative session since 2021, under different bill numbers: S7514 in 2021 and 2022, S307 in 2023 and 2024, and now S159 for 2025 and 2026, with a companion bill, A127, in the Assembly. The bill would rewrite § 259-i(2)(c)(A) to presume release for anyone eligible, unless the record shows a "current and unreasonable risk" that can't be managed through parole supervision. It would also bar the Board from basing a denial solely or primarily on the victim's statement, the seriousness of the original offense, or the person's prior record, the three factors Vera's research found commissioners lean on almost by default. Denials would have to come with a written, individualized explanation, and the Board would have to file quarterly public reports on who it denies and why.

The bill cleared the Senate's Crime Victims, Crime and Correction Committee by a 5-2 vote on May 20, 2026, the same margin it passed by in April 2025. From there it moved to the Finance Committee, where it remains as of this writing. It has never reached a floor vote in either chamber in five legislative sessions.

The sponsor memo for the current bill cites a November 2024 report from the Center on Race, Inequality and the Law at NYU School of Law, which THE CITY reported on at the time: New York's parole release racial disparity is now the widest gap the state has recorded since it began publishing this data in 2016. Case Analytica's earlier piece on that report covers those numbers in full. What the current bill adds to that picture is the mechanism underneath the gap: a standard broad enough that a commissioner can lean on the original crime for almost any case, and a record showing that's exactly what tends to happen.

What this means if someone you love has a hearing coming up

The release-plan factors in the law are real, and building the strongest possible one, verified housing, a real job offer, program completion certificates, letters of support, still matters and still helps. Case Analytica's guide on how to actually prepare for a New York parole hearing covers what the Board evaluates and how to build a release plan that holds up. But it's worth going into a hearing with a clear-eyed understanding that a strong release plan is not, under current law, a guarantee of anything. The statute lets the Board weigh it against an offense from years ago and come out the other side with a denial, and the data says that's the more common outcome, not the exception.

If the goal is actually reducing how many people cycle back through the system, the state doesn't need a new theory. It needs its own parole board to start crediting the release plans the law already asks it to consider. Until the Fair and Timely Parole Act, or something like it, actually reaches a floor vote, that's a decision left entirely to each commissioner, case by case, with no requirement to explain why a confirmed job and a signed lease weren't enough.
