New York Can Take Your Guns Without a Criminal Charge: The Red Flag Law Explained

A New York court can order every firearm taken out of someone's home without filing a single criminal charge against them. No arrest, no indictment, no trial. It happens through a civil case, and the law that allows it moves fast enough that a lot of people facing one don't fully understand what's happening until the guns are already gone.

What an Extreme Risk Protection Order actually is

New York's Extreme Risk Protection Order, commonly called the red flag law, is codified in CPLR Article 63-A. It lets a court order someone not to purchase, possess, or attempt to purchase or possess a firearm, rifle, or shotgun, and to surrender any they already have. According to the state court system's own explainer, the petition can be filed by a police officer, a district attorney, a family or household member, or a school administrator. The person the order is filed against, called the respondent, does not need to be charged with, or convicted of, any crime for a court to grant it. It's a civil order. But violating it, by trying to buy or possess a gun while it's in effect, can turn into a criminal case on its own.

How fast this actually moves

Under CPLR § 6342, a judge can issue a temporary order the same day the petition is filed, and can do it ex parte, meaning the person it's filed against isn't even in the room when it's granted. Once that temporary order is served, the law requires the court to schedule a hearing no sooner than three and no later than roughly ten business days later, to decide whether a final order should be issued for up to a year. The statute does let the respondent ask for more time to prepare. But the standard for that first, same-day order is only probable cause: enough to believe someone is likely to cause serious harm to themselves or others, not proof of it.

You're told you can get a lawyer, not that one will be appointed for you

CPLR § 6342 requires the court to inform the respondent that they may seek the advice of an attorney and should consult one promptly. That's different from a criminal case, where a public defender gets appointed if you can't afford one. Whether a respondent facing an ERPO has a right to appointed counsel is still an unsettled question. Don't assume a lawyer will simply show up for you here the way one does at a criminal arraignment. Find one, fast, and bring them everything the petition claims.

The law has already been tested in court

This isn't an untested statute. In Matter of R.M. v. C.M., 2024 NY Slip Op 01545 (2d Dept 2024), New York's Appellate Division, Second Department, upheld the red flag law against a Second Amendment challenge, ruling it consistent with the state's authority to keep firearms away from people found dangerous. That means the standard the law uses, and the process behind it, has already survived appellate review. Fighting an ERPO on the theory that the law itself shouldn't exist is a much harder path than fighting the specific facts the petition is built on.

An ERPO isn't the only civil process that can restrict someone or take their property without a criminal conviction. See how a criminal court order of protection actually works for a related but legally distinct process, and how civil asset forfeiture lets police take cash before you're ever charged for another example of the same pattern: the civil side of the system moving on a lower burden of proof than a criminal case ever would.

The exact questions to ask

If you're served with a temporary order: what specific acts is the petition based on, exactly when is the hearing date, and have you already reached out to a lawyer before that date arrives. Under CPLR § 6342, you're entitled to ask the court for more time to prepare if you need it. Use that window. Don't walk into the hearing having never spoken to anyone about what the petition actually says.
