Read the Whole Parole File Before the Board Does

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-03

Reflects law as of October 3, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

The Parole Board decides from a folder. Before my own interview, the ORC walked me through what was going to the Board. Then I got the full file and found a lot more in it.

This is the method I used for my own interview, and the one I used to help other men prepare for theirs. It has five steps: build the portfolio first, get the whole file, learn every document and its date, put those dates on the record, and know that the transcript is what any appeal will read.

Print the worksheet first

Every step below has a page on the parole file worksheet. Print it and mail it in. Families can fill in the release-plan section from the outside.

What the Board is told to weigh

Executive Law § 259-i(2)(c)(A) lists what the Board has to consider. Three items matter most for this method:

  • Your record inside: "the institutional record including program goals and accomplishments, academic achievements, vocational education, training or work assignments, therapy and interactions with staff and incarcerated individuals."
  • Your release plan: "release plans including community resources, employment, education and training and support services available to the incarcerated individual."
  • The offense, and the letters about it: "the seriousness of the offense with due consideration to the type of sentence, length of sentence and recommendations of the sentencing court, the district attorney, the attorney for the incarcerated individual, the pre-sentence probation report."

Read that last one closely. The statute tells the Board to weigh the district attorney's and the sentencing court's recommendations. Those letters belong in the folder. This method will not get them taken out. It makes sure the Board also sees everything written since, and that the record shows how old the sentencing letters are.

Step 1: Build the portfolio first

The portfolio is your story and your release plan. It is due well ahead of the interview, long before you ever get a look at the file. Build it around the statute's own categories:

  • Programs and accomplishments: completion certificates, program goals met, therapy.
  • Education: diplomas, transcripts, college credits.
  • Vocational training and work: trade certificates, work assignments, evaluations.
  • Release plan: a housing letter naming the address, a job offer or employment program, and the support services lined up.
  • Letters of support that are dated, specific, and say what the writer is offering. See how to prepare for a New York parole hearing for what makes a letter count.

Send it in ahead of time. The DOCCS parole process page says "all letters of support should be sent to the ORC," that any written statement "should be submitted to the ORC in advance of their Board interview," and that "Letters and documents received by ORC will be placed in the case folder and provided to the Board at the time of appearance." It also says to address materials to the Supervising Offender Rehabilitation Coordinator (SORC) and not to send letters directly to the Board. Keep a full copy for yourself.

That page says nothing about carrying a portfolio into the interview, and I have not found a written rule either way. When I built mine, I was told I could not take it in. I took it anyway. Whatever happens at the door, send it through the SORC first so it's in the folder.

If your facility does let you take it in, use it. It keeps you on point. When the commissioner's questions drift back to the crime, point to the documents in front of you and bring the interview back to your record and your release plan.

Step 2: Get the whole file

The portfolio is what you give the Board. The file is what the Board already has, and knowing what is in it makes you effective in the interview.

You can ask to see the parole case record before you go in. Under 9 NYCRR 8000.5(c), an incarcerated person or their lawyer "may have access to information contained in the parole case record" before "a scheduled appearance before the board." Here is how to ask:

  • Send the request in writing to your ORC. The ORC has the file and prepares it, and the ORC is the one who has to show you the final file that goes to the commissioners. DOCCS's own parole process page says "The folders are maintained at the facility."
  • Include your name, your identification number, the kind of hearing, and the facility you're in. The regulation asks for all four.

It won't be everything. The regulation holds back diagnostic opinions, information from confidential sources, and anything that "might result in harm" to a person. It also says access through this route will not be granted to "reports, documents and materials of other agencies," and names probation reports and the DCJS report among them.

At the facility where I was held, the other men told me to file for the folder about a week before the interview. I did. The folder I saw that way held far more than what the ORC had shown me. That's how I learned what was going to be used against me.

Here is what the review looked like. They brought the file into the package room and opened it in front of me. They showed me each item for a few minutes, then closed the file, and that was it. I could not copy anything. I could not bring a pen or a piece of paper. I had to memorize what was in the folder and the date on each document.

One bill in Albany would change the timing. S3918 would give applicants the chance to review all documents in their parole file at least one month before the hearing. As of October 3, 2026, it is in committee and is not law.

Step 3: Learn every document and its date

Go through the folder one document at a time. For each one, get two things: who wrote it and the date on it. You won't have a pen or paper in the room, so memorize those two things and fill in the worksheet as soon as you're back. Then mark each document: was it written at or before sentencing, or since? You need to get those documents and their dates on the record.

Then count. In my folder, the sentencing-era side included letters from FINRA, the SEC, and the local district attorney, all from the time I was sentenced. When you see your own count laid out on one page, you know what the Board is reading, and how much of it describes a person from years ago.

Step 4: Put the dates on the record

Once you know what's in the folder, you can use it in the interview. If the conversation keeps going back to the crime, ask the commissioner to confirm the documents in the folder, the dates on them, and today's date. Do it calmly. Ask it as a question and let the answer go on the transcript.

In my interview, I got all of it on the record: the FINRA, SEC, and DA letters, and when each was written. I also said that the Board had my Earned Eligibility Certificate, and that this interview was about my release plans. If you hold an EEC, read Two Questions on the Record first, including who the certificate's presumption covers and who it doesn't.

Don't argue with the Board. Ask the calm question about a date, and the transcript keeps the answer.

Step 5: The transcript is what the appeal reads

If the Board denies release, the written decision has to give its reasons "in detail and not in conclusory terms," under § 259-i(2)(a)(i). You can appeal. The rules are in 9 NYCRR 8006.1 and 8006.2:

  • File a notice of appeal "within 30 days" of receiving the written decision. Missing that deadline waives the appeal.
  • When you file the notice, ask for a copy of the interview transcript.
  • The appeal must be perfected within four months of filing the notice, unless the appeals unit grants an extension.

Here is why everything above has to be said out loud. Each appeal "will be reviewed and decided on the basis of the written record. A personal appearance and/or oral argument is expressly prohibited." The commissioners who decide it never meet you. Under § 259-i(4), "any board member who participated in the decision from which the appeal is taken may not participate in the resolution of that appeal." Under 9 NYCRR 8006.4, three Board members decide it by majority and "may affirm, modify or reverse the decision." They decide from the written record, and the transcript is the only part of it that shows what was said in the room. If you never said the dates out loud, the appeal panel has no record that you raised them.

When an appeal wins, the Appeals Unit's written findings say who holds the new interview. DOCCS publishes some of these findings. In one from Fishkill, the Appeals Unit found "an insufficient amount of detailed questions" had been asked, and that the decision lacked "any detail as to why the instant offense, standing alone, justifies the denial." It recommended that the decision "be vacated, and that a de novo interview in front of a different panel of Commissioners be held." Others say the same thing in different words: "before a panel of new Board members," "before different Board members." The three Board members who decide the appeal make the final call.

I won my appeal, and my decision said it too: the commissioners who denied me could not interview me again. It said the same for every man I helped win a de novo interview. If the same commissioners show up anyway, you can refuse the interview and point to the decision. I had made sure the right things were on my transcript. That is one case, and I can't tell you which part of the record decided it. But nothing I said in the room would have reached the appeal panel if it hadn't been on the transcript.

The exact question to ask

As soon as you get notice of the interview date, put this in writing to your ORC: "I'm requesting access to my parole case record under 9 NYCRR 8000.5(c). Is what you've shown me everything the Board will see, and if not, how do I review the rest before my appearance?" If the answer is that you've already seen the folder, ask whether that was the full case record or a summary. "You've seen what you need to see" doesn't answer the question.
