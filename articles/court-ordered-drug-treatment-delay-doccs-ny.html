A Judge Ordered Him Into Drug Treatment. He Waited 506 Days.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-25

New York law gives the prison system ten days to move someone into a court-ordered drug program. When it takes longer, the only thing that reliably fixes it is a lawsuit, and most people never learn that.

Michael Matzell pleaded guilty to drug possession and was sentenced to four years. The judge also ordered him into shock incarceration, the six-month program that, on completion, makes someone eligible for a certificate of earned eligibility. He was screened in January 2018 and told he was not suitable, because he had prison disciplinary tickets for drug use.

He needed drug treatment. That was the reason he was denied drug treatment.

Matzell sued, and won twice. The Appellate Division, Third Department affirmed in his favor in 2020, and the federal Second Circuit ruled for him again in 2023. By then, as the Second Circuit put it, "506 days after he became eligible, Matzell was enrolled in the program; upon completion he was immediately released from prison."

He was let out the moment he finished. Every one of those 506 days was time he served because an agency overrode a judge.

The deadline is ten days, and it is written down

For one kind of court-ordered treatment, New York puts a number on it. Criminal Procedure Law 410.91 governs a sentence of parole supervision, the sentence that sends someone to a ninety-day drug treatment campus. The statute says the court

"shall remand the defendant for immediate delivery to a reception center operated by the state department of corrections and community supervision ... for a period not to exceed ten days."

Immediate delivery. Not to exceed ten days. Then ninety days of treatment, and release.

Reception is the processing stop every newly sentenced person passes through on the way upstate. It is where classification happens, where the tests are given, and where program needs are first written down. It is supposed to be brief.

For court-ordered shock, the statute says "promptly"

Shock incarceration has no ten-day clock. It has something else. Correction Law 867(2-a) says:

"Notwithstanding subdivision five of this section, an incarcerated individual sentenced to shock incarceration shall promptly commence participation in the program when such incarcerated individual is an eligible incarcerated individual pursuant to subdivision one of section eight hundred sixty-five of this article."

Read the first four words again. Subdivision five of that same section is the provision that says participation "shall be a privilege" and that nothing in the article "may be construed to confer upon any incarcerated individual the right to participate." For someone a judge ordered into the program, the Legislature switched that off.

So the agency's usual answer, that shock is a privilege and nobody is entitled to it, is the exact answer the statute removes in the one situation where a court has already spoken.

General information, not legal advice

This explains how the process generally works and what two courts have said about it. Every case is different. Confirm specifics with a licensed attorney before acting on anything here.

What the courts have said

In Matter of Matzell v Annucci, 183 AD3d 1, decided February 27, 2020, the Third Department took up whether the Department of Corrections and Community Supervision can decide someone is unsuitable after a judge has already ordered the program. The court said no, in plain terms:

"Once an inmate has been judicially ordered into the program, DOCCS' participation under Penal Law § 60.04 (7) is expressly limited to its administration of the program, i.e., the completion, discipline and removal of an inmate from the program. If the Legislature intended DOCCS to have administrative discretion as to the eligibility criteria, it could have said so."

The court then named what the agency's reading would amount to:

"DOCCS' interpretation would permit it to administratively modify a criminal sentence, rendering the Legislature's grant of judicial authority under the statute meaningless."

It also pointed at Criminal Procedure Law 430.10, which says a lawful sentence "may not be changed ... once the term or period of the sentence has commenced." Once a judge orders the program, the only things DOCCS gets to decide are whether the person has a medical or mental health condition that would stop them completing the program, and which facility they do it in. That limit comes from Correction Law 867(2-a).

Matzell was represented by Prisoners' Legal Services of New York.

Every exit is timed to close

DOCCS's position is that you grieve first, and there is case law behind it. The program in 7 NYCRR 701.5 runs three steps: the committee at your facility, then the superintendent, then the Central Office Review Committee in Albany. Sixteen days, then twenty, then thirty, with seven days to appeal at each stage. Eighty days at the floor, if nobody is late.

Shock incarceration lasts six months.

Skipping a step ends it. In Matter of Jackson v Administration of Bare Hill Corr. Facility, 139 AD3d 1191, a man removed from his prison work program grieved it, lost twice, and then wrote a letter to the Commissioner instead of appealing to CORC. The Third Department affirmed the dismissal:

"Inasmuch as petitioner failed to appeal to CORC, Supreme Court properly dismissed the petition for failure to exhaust administrative remedies."

No court ever looked at whether his removal was justified. He was representing himself.

There is a counterargument, and it is old. The Court of Appeals said this in Watergate II Apartments v Buffalo Sewer Auth., 46 NY2d 52 (1978):

"The exhaustion rule, however, is not an inflexible one. It is subject to important qualifications. It need not be followed, for example, when an agency's action is challenged as either unconstitutional or wholly beyond its grant of power ... or when resort to an administrative remedy would be futile ... or when its pursuit would cause irreparable injury."

Matzell's claim was exactly that. The Third Department agreed that DOCCS's authority was "expressly limited," and that decision does not discuss exhaustion anywhere.

The two men drew the same court and the same Assistant Attorney General. One was pro se and framed it as a grievance. One had Prisoners' Legal Services and framed it as the agency exceeding its power.

The Attorney General represents DOCCS, and failure to exhaust is a standard threshold defense. Raising the exception means litigating that question first, before anyone looks at the merits. That is a fight, not a shortcut, and it is a hard one without a lawyer.

Before you file anything

Ask counsel which path fits your case. Filing early when exhaustion was required gets the petition dismissed, as it did in Jackson. Grieving all the way through when you did not have to costs eighty days of a six-month program. Both mistakes are expensive and they run in opposite directions.

The Third Department has already said this openly. It decided Matzell after the case had gone moot, because the question

"will typically evade review in the courts ... specifically due to the six-month time period necessary to complete the program."

The court took the case because the process outlasts the thing being fought over.

The cost of sitting

DOCCS publishes a report on the Earned Eligibility Program twice a year. Earned eligibility is the certificate that, under Correction Law 805, shifts the burden at the parole board for people serving indeterminate sentences with minimums of eight years or less. Finishing shock incarceration makes someone eligible for one too, under Correction Law 867(4), which routes completion straight back to section 805.

The report covering April through September 2025 contains a line that is easy to read past. Of 574 people found non-certifiable for a certificate, 244 were non-certifiable for one reason: they were still in reception.

Not because they refused programming. Because they had not been given any yet.

People held in reception were released at almost exactly the rate of people who refused or were removed from their programs. The gap between them and the people who got a certificate is nearly 42 points.

The rule DOCCS still publishes is seventeen years out of date

Shock incarceration has an age limit. Correction Law 865 puts it just under fifty, which DOCCS itself describes as "up to and including 49 years of age":

"who has not reached the age of fifty years ... and who was between the ages of sixteen and fifty years at the time of commission of the crime"

The regulation in Title 7 of the state codes, 7 NYCRR 1800.4, says something else. It says an applicant must have "not reached 40 years of age" and must have been "at least 16 but less than 40 years of age at the time of commission of the crime."

That is not a typo. It is the rule that existed before April 2009.

DOCCS says so itself. Its own report on the 2009 Drug Law Reform Act describes the old criteria: "Prior to April 2009, inmates were eligible for Shock if they were new court commitments under the age of 40 ... Offenders must have been between the ages of 16 and 39 years when they committed their instant offense. Inmates who had prior felony convictions for which they received prison sentences were not eligible for Shock." And it describes what changed: "Shock became available to inmates up to and including 49 years of age."

Line up the published regulation against the rule DOCCS says was replaced in 2009 and they match clause for clause. The age, the age at the offense, and the bar on anyone with a prior prison sentence. The statute now bars only prior violent felonies, and it covers determinate sentences as well as indeterminate ones. The regulation reflects neither change.

A statute beats a regulation. But a person reading the state's own published rule, finding they are 43, or that they have an old felony, or that their sentence is determinate, has every reason to conclude they are ineligible and stop asking.

A note on sourcing

The official compilation of the state codes is served through a subscription interface that would not render this section for us. The regulatory text quoted above is identical across the Cornell Legal Information Institute and Justia, and it matches DOCCS's own account of the pre-2009 rule. Anyone relying on it for a filing should confirm it against the compilation filed with the Secretary of State.

The age trap nobody has fixed

Correction Law 865 contains two age clauses, and only one of them says when it applies.

One is anchored: a person must have been "between the ages of sixteen and fifty years at the time of commission of the crime." On an ordinary reading, someone who was exactly fifty at the time of the offense is inside that range.

The other is not anchored at all: "who has not reached the age of fifty years." Someone who is exactly fifty has reached fifty, and is outside it.

Same sentence, same person, two answers. And because the unanchored clause has no moment attached to it, it is unclear what happens to someone who enters custody at 48, sits, and turns 50 while waiting.

There is an argument that it does not matter. Section 865(2) says a person may be selected for the program either at a reception center or later, at a general confinement facility, "when the otherwise eligible incarcerated individual then becomes eligible." DOCCS's own 2009 report calls this the ability to "age into" the program, and says that before the change, "eligible inmates had to be selected for the program from reception centers."

If someone was otherwise eligible on the way in, the later selection is about timing. That is a reading, not a ruling. No court appears to have decided it.

What this adds up to

The remedy works. Matzell got his program and walked out the day he finished it. Two courts said the agency had no authority to do what it did.

Getting there took an Article 78 petition, an appeal, a federal civil rights suit, and a legal services organization willing to carry it.

That is the part worth sitting with. The law is clear, the deadline is written down, and the fix is available. Whether any particular person gets it turns on whether they happen to know the deadline exists.

The exact question to ask

Ask your attorney before sentencing if you can, and the same week you arrive if you cannot: "My sentence orders me into this program. What is the deadline for them to place me, and what do we file the day it passes?" Get the deadline as a date, written down, and get the name of the filing. A general answer that these things take time is not an answer to that question. If you are already past it, Prisoners' Legal Services of New York litigated the case that settled this point.
