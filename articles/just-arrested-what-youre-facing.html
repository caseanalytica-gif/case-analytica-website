If this just happened (to you, or to someone you love) you don't need a lecture on the criminal justice system. You need to know two things: what's actually happening right now, and what to do in the next few days that could change the entire outcome. This is that, in order.

Want the short version, plus a way to actually talk to someone? See help after an arrest in New York: what to do first.

What you're actually facing in the first 24 hours

After an arrest, New York generally requires the person to be brought before a judge for arraignment within 24 hours, the first real checkpoint. At arraignment, the charges are read, a plea is entered (almost always "not guilty" at this stage, regardless of what happened, because this isn't the moment to argue the case), and the judge decides on bail or release. Since 2019 bail reform, most misdemeanors and non-violent felonies are released with conditions rather than cash bail, but a subset of charges, mostly violent felonies and repeat cases, are still bail-eligible.

That's the mechanical part, and we cover it in full in what actually happens after an arrest, step by step. This article is about something more urgent than the mechanics: the decision window that opens right now and closes faster than most people realize.

The single most important thing to understand this early

Restorative justice and diversion programs exist in New York. They can resolve a case without a standard conviction ever going on the record: through a facilitated dialogue with whoever was harmed, community service, treatment, or educational programs. Complete the conditions, and a charge can be reduced or dismissed entirely.

Here's what almost nobody gets told at this stage: none of this is offered automatically. A defense attorney (public defender or private) has to specifically request it, and a public defender carrying dozens of active cases is not always going to raise it unprompted. That's not a failure of the attorney. It's what a heavy caseload does to attention: see why your public defender might be carrying 100+ cases for exactly what that looks like in New York. The responsibility for making sure this gets asked about falls on the person facing the case, or their family, and it has to happen before a plea is entered, not after. Once a plea is in, this window is closed.

This is one piece of a wider pattern: the same information and representation gaps show up across the whole process, not just at the diversion question. See our full breakdown in The Two Failures Nobody Warns You About.

The exact question to ask

As soon as counsel is assigned (public defender or private), ask directly: "Is there a diversion program or restorative justice option available for this case?" Not eventually. This week. See our full checklist of follow-up questions to ask once you've opened that conversation.

What "navigating toward the best outcome" actually looks like this week

Four things matter more than anything else in the first several days, regardless of what the charge is:

  • Get counsel assigned or hired fast. If a public defender hasn't been assigned yet, that should happen at or before arraignment, ask the court clerk if it hasn't. If considering private counsel, earlier is better, because the work that actually shapes the outcome happens in these first weeks, not before trial.
  • Ask the diversion/restorative justice question directly, and ask it early. Don't assume it will come up. Bring it up yourself, in the first conversation with counsel if possible.
  • Start building a record now, regardless of which path the case takes. Notes on what happened while memory is fresh, any witnesses, a simple written timeline. This helps a defense either way, and it also becomes the foundation for a diversion application or a release plan later if the case goes that direction.
  • Don't rush into a plea before the diversion question has been answered. New York's discovery reform generally gives a window of a couple of weeks after arraignment before evidence deadlines and plea pressure really start. There's usually more time than the moment feels like it allows. A fast plea taken out of panic can close a door that didn't need to close yet.

If diversion isn't available right now

Sometimes eligibility isn't fixed permanently at arraignment: a different charge, more information, or a program completed first can change the answer later. Don't treat "not now" as "never." Our restorative justice guide covers what to ask if the first answer is no.

General information, not legal advice

This is general information about how the process and these programs generally work, not legal advice for a specific case. Every case turns on details a general article can't cover. Confirm specifics with a licensed attorney or the assigned public defender's office.
