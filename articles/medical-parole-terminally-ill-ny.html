Medical Parole in New York: How a Terminally Ill or Debilitated Person Can Actually Get Out

New York Focus profiled a man named Jose Medina in 2022: 68 years old at the time, missing most of both lungs to cancer surgery, dependent on inhalers and a breathing machine, and still incarcerated. He first applied for medical parole in 2006. He was denied, with no explanation beyond a form letter stating he was "not an appropriate candidate." He applied again in 2020, during the first year of Covid. Denied again, same form letter, same missing reason. New York has a real law that lets a terminally ill or severely debilitated person come home. Almost nobody who needs to hear about it ever does.

The two statutes that actually apply

New York's medical parole law lives in two sections of the Executive Law. Executive Law § 259-r covers people with a terminal condition, disease, or syndrome. Executive Law § 259-s covers people with a significant and permanent non-terminal condition. Both require the same core finding: the person is so physically or cognitively debilitated that there is a reasonable probability they no longer present a danger to society. Neither statute requires a conviction to be reversed or a sentence to be served in full. Neither one is automatic.

Some convictions are excluded outright. Nobody serving a sentence for first-degree murder, or an attempt or conspiracy to commit it, is eligible under either section. A second group of convictions, including second-degree murder, first-degree manslaughter, and sex offenses under Penal Law Article 130, requires the person to have already served at least half their sentence before medical parole can even be considered. Everyone else can apply at any point during their incarceration.

Who can actually make the request

The request does not have to come from the incarcerated person. Under DOCCS Directive 4304, a spouse, relative, attorney, or a Department employee can request that someone be considered for medical parole, at any time, by emailing Health.MedicalParole@doccs.ny.gov or writing to DOCCS Health Services in Albany. A family member watching someone decline does not have to wait for the person inside to be well enough to file the paperwork themselves.

Four gates, and most requests don't clear them

A request has to pass four separate reviews before it ever reaches a parole hearing: a medical evaluation by a licensed physician, a review by the DOCCS chief medical officer, sign-off from the DOCCS commissioner, and only then a hearing before the Board of Parole. Denial can happen at any of the first three stages, and when it does, the incarcerated person and their family are typically told only that they were "not deemed an appropriate candidate," with no further reasoning required.

The numbers show how narrow that path is. A 2018 report by the Vera Institute of Justice, examining medical parole requests filed in New York State prisons from 2013 through 2017, found that only 12 percent of requests filed from 2013 through 2015 ended in release, and that twice as many applicants died in custody as were ever released. Of 476 requests filed in that three-year window, only 240 were approved by prison staff at the first stage, and only about half of those made it as far as the parole board. When a request did reach the board, Vera found it was granted roughly two-thirds of the time, a far higher rate than the board's overall parole approval rate. The bottleneck isn't the hearing. It's everything standing in front of it.

The Covid spike that proved the point

According to New York Focus's 2022 reporting, DOCCS received 1,049 medical parole requests in 2020 as Covid tore through state prisons. It approved 18. In 2021, requests dropped to 82, and 10 people were released. A department spokesperson told the reporters that fear of contracting Covid on top of an existing condition is not, by itself, a legal basis for medical parole. Whether that's a fair reading of the statute is a separate question from what it did to the number of people actually approved.

What "terminal" means to the person deciding your case

New York Focus's reporting also surfaced how narrowly DOCCS has interpreted the word "terminal." A state judge required the department's chief medical officer to explain, in writing, why he denied a man named Wilfredo Lopez, who had been diagnosed with a condition a prison physician had already called terminal, with a prognosis of one to two years to live. The chief medical officer's answer: "'Terminal' suggests a life expectancy of six months or less." That is not language written into either statute. It is a threshold set by the official reviewing the file, and it is worth knowing that threshold exists before assuming a terminal diagnosis alone will clear the first gate.

The exact question to ask

If someone you love is seriously ill in DOCCS custody, ask the facility's medical unit directly: "Has a request for medical parole under Executive Law 259-r or 259-s been submitted, and if it was denied, at which of the four stages, and why?" Put the request in writing yourself if nobody inside has done it. A denial at the chief medical officer or commissioner stage is not the end of the road. The directive allows reapplication, and a documented change in condition is grounds to try again.

And if a medical parole grant is ever at risk of being revoked, that runs through the same violation process as any other release: see what happens if you violate parole in New York under Executive Law 259-i.

Medical parole isn't the only path built for someone who doesn't fit the standard release timeline. A pending bill, the Second Look Act, would let anyone who's served at least ten years, not just someone terminally ill, ask a different judge to reconsider the sentence itself. It's a separate mechanism with its own eligibility rules, but the same basic premise: continued incarceration stops making sense at some point, and someone has to be able to ask the question directly.

The bigger cost nobody totals up

Vera's researchers didn't just count releases. They counted deaths. People waiting on a medical parole decision died in custody at twice the rate of people who were released. That is not a statistic about crime. It is a statistic about paperwork, review timelines, and who bothers to ask the question in time.
