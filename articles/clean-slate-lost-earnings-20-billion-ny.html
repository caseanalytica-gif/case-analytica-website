A New Report Says Old Records Cost New Yorkers $20.9 Billion a Year. New York Already Promised to Fix Most of It.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-02

Reflects law as of October 2, 2026

A national study published this summer puts the earnings New Yorkers lose to arrest and conviction records at $20.9 billion a year. New York has already passed laws aimed at most of that penalty. The open question is how much of it people are still paying while the state finishes sealing.

The number comes from The Economic Losses from Arrest & Conviction Records, released in August by The Clean Slate Initiative, a national organization that campaigns for automatic record sealing. It is now running as a paid ad on TikTok. The ad leads with one finding: one arrest, no conviction, and a person earns about 15 percent less than a comparable peer with no record.

That line is accurate to the report. It is also the part of the report that applies least to New York, and the report says so itself.

What the study measured

The report uses the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1997, a federal survey that has followed the same group of Americans since they were teenagers. The author, data scientist Liz Johnson, took people who had an arrest or conviction in early adulthood and then stayed crime-free for more than a decade. She compared their earnings from ages 26 to 40 with statistically matched people who had no record at all.

The gap held at every level:

  • Arrest, no conviction: 14.9 percent lower earnings, about $4,400 a year, and 3.8 fewer weeks worked a year among those employed.
  • Misdemeanor conviction: 17.8 percent lower, about $6,400 a year, and just over three fewer weeks worked.
  • Non-violent felony conviction: 31.8 percent lower, about $9,300 a year.
  • Violent felony conviction: 36.7 percent lower, about $9,800 a year.

Over those fourteen working years, that adds up to an estimated $72,000 lost for someone with an arrest and no conviction, and $102,000 for someone with a misdemeanor. These are people who did not reoffend. The penalty follows them anyway.

New York's share

The report spreads its national estimate of $440 billion a year across the states. New York comes in fourth, behind California, Florida and Texas:

Record typeEstimated lost earnings a year, New York
Arrest, no conviction$3.41 billion
Misdemeanor conviction$11.03 billion
Non-violent felony conviction$4.53 billion
Violent felony conviction$1.98 billion
Total$20.94 billion

Misdemeanors are the largest piece by far, more than half of New York's total.

The report cannot see New York's laws

The public version of the federal survey does not say which state a person lives in. So the report took regional earnings penalties and applied them to each state's estimated population with a record. The author is direct about what that leaves out. The state estimates, the report says, are made without being able "to account for state-level factors that may impact earnings losses post arrest or conviction like pre-existing sealing laws." And:

"States with long-standing policies that limit employer access to records, seal records to public-use, or restrict the use of arrest and conviction records in employment decisions may experience different impacts than estimated here."

New York does all three. That is why the $20.9 billion needs to be read in two pieces.

The $3.4 billion New York already addresses

When a New York case ends in the person's favor, the record is sealed under CPL 160.50 unless a prosecutor or the court shows the interests of justice require otherwise. That covers a dismissal or an acquittal. It also covers an arrest that never became a case, when the prosecutor declines to prosecute or the police decline to go forward before charges are filed. Our CPL 160.50 explainer covers what gets sealed and who can still see it.

A second law stops employers from using what the first one seals. Executive Law 296(16) makes it unlawful for any employer, landlord, licensing agency, lender or insurer to ask about an arrest that ended in the person's favor, or to act against someone because of it. The person may answer as if the arrest never happened. The exceptions are narrow: police and peace officer hiring, and gun licensing.

So the ad's headline case, the arrest with no conviction, is one New York law already treats as if it never happened. The report cannot say how much of the national penalty survives here anyway, and it does not claim to.

The $17.5 billion Clean Slate was written for

Conviction records make up the rest: $17.5 billion of New York's estimated total. Those are what the Clean Slate Act covers. Under CPL 160.57, a misdemeanor conviction seals automatically three years after release from incarceration, or three years after sentencing if there was no jail time. A felony seals after eight years, measured the same way. Sex offenses and most class A felonies are excluded. A record will not seal while a new charge is pending in New York or while the person is still on probation or parole.

Set that against the people the report measured. Every one of them stayed crime-free for more than a decade after their record, which is past both waiting periods. If their convictions had been in New York, most would qualify for automatic sealing today.

Most of those records are not sealed yet. The law took effect on November 16, 2024, and it gives the court system until three years after that date to finish sealing the convictions already on the books. The statute tells the Office of Court Administration to make "diligent efforts to promptly seal" them and to complete the work "no later than three years after such effective date." That deadline is November 16, 2027.

We have reported on why the wait may run that long. The agencies building the system told lawmakers in 2025 that they were not asking for extra money or staff to do it. And a bill to fix a drafting flaw that blocks sealing for some multi-count cases has sat in committee since March 2025.

Two estimates, two different questions

This is not the first price tag on New York's records. In 2023 the Brennan Center estimated that underemployment tied to a criminal record costs New Yorkers about $12.6 billion a year, counting roughly 2.1 million adults with conviction records.

The two numbers are not in conflict, and they should not be added together. The Brennan figure counts convictions only. The Clean Slate Initiative counts arrests without a conviction too, and builds its state numbers from regional penalties rather than New York data. Its conviction-only total for New York, $17.5 billion, sits above Brennan's $12.6 billion. Either way, the penalty in New York runs to billions of dollars a year.

The November 2027 deadline

The report cannot tell New York what the sealing backlog itself is costing. It was never built to. What it does measure is the size of the penalty sealing is meant to end, for people who look like the ones still waiting on it: an old record, a decade or more without a new one, and earnings that never caught up.

New York decided in 2023 that those records should be sealed. The statute sets the outer limit at November 2027. Whether the state gets there before then, and how much of that $17.5 billion people are still paying in the meantime, is what to watch.
