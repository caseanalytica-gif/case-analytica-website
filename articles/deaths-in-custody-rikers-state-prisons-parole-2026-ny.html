New York publishes two death counts this year, and neither tells a reader much about why people died. As of November 10, nine people have died in New York City jail custody in 2026, according to the Queens Daily Eagle and The City. As of November 6, the state's prison system lists 149 names on its 2026 incarcerated deaths page, by our count. The state's annual mortality report counted 144 deaths for all of 2024. The page gives each person's name, birth date, ID number, date of death and prison. It gives no cause.

Why Case Analytica is studying this

The Parole Board decides how long people stay in that system, and the state's own numbers show how often it says no. Case Analytica's parole reporting project reads what the Board writes when it denies people who hold an Earned Eligibility Certificate, a group the law presumes will go home. This article puts the death counts, the staffing numbers and the parole numbers in one place, with each figure tied to its source and its limits.

I put the first number in a video this week, the ninth death at Rikers, and said the state prisons are worse. The counts below bear out the first part. The second needs care, and the section on what these numbers do not say explains why.

Rikers: the ninth death

Cesar Gabriel Medero Curet, 36, was found in his bed at the Robert N. Davoren Center on Rikers Island early on Tuesday, November 10, and was pronounced dead at 6:03 a.m., amNY and The City reported. He had been held about 20 months awaiting trial, the Eagle reported, on an attempted murder charge brought in March 2025. Those are allegations, not convictions. The Department of Correction had not released the circumstances. The Legal Aid Society, which represented him, said it had no information about his final moments and called for a swift, independent and impartial investigation.

The Eagle and The City give the same death counts for earlier years. amNY attributes its 2025 figure to the Vera Institute.

The city jails held 6,602 people on November 12, according to the Department of Correction's daily count in NYC Open Data, which our data hub records.

The state prisons: a list of names

The Department of Corrections and Community Supervision, DOCCS, runs 41 prisons that held 34,143 people on August 1, 2026. Its August fact sheet counts 101 deaths from January 1 to August 1 and sorts them by manner, using preliminary reports that change when the medical examiner reports.

One dot for every name.

Each grey dot is a name on DOCCS's 2026 list. Each gold dot is a death in city jail custody.

State list through Nov. 6 (our count of DOCCS's page); city count through Nov. 10 (Queens Daily Eagle, The City). Different agencies, counted differently.

The most deaths in that period were at Mohawk (12, 11 of them natural causes) and Wende (12), then Green Haven (10), Elmira (8) and Attica (7). Since then the page has grown to 149 names, through November 6. DOCCS's 2024 mortality report counted 122 deaths in 2020, 137 in 2021, 113 in 2022, 107 in 2023 and 144 in 2024. This year's list was already past the 2024 total on November 6, with about eight weeks left. The two publications may not count the same way, so read that as a rough comparison.

Staffing is the other number the state publishes. The same fact sheet puts uniformed security staff at 11,139 in July 2026, down from 22,112 at the end of 1999, while the incarcerated population fell from 72,649 to 34,143. Correction officers alone fell from 17,627 in December 2013 to 9,839 in July 2026. DOCCS says the staff reductions have tracked the population decline. Its own table shows incarcerated people per filled uniform position at 1.9 in 2022 and 3.0 in 2025. Neither the fact sheet nor the death list says whether staffing and deaths are connected.

The State Commission of Correction, the agency that reviews each death, was without a quorum in May. Our earlier article covers what changed.

Parole: who goes home

The Parole Board's 2024 report to the Legislature, the latest full-year report we could read, counts 7,225 Board interviews. The Board granted release in 2,956, or 40.9 percent. Initial interviews, leaving out Shock, were approved 41.4 percent of the time. Reappearances, the later interviews after a denial, were approved in 461 of 1,528, or 30.2 percent.

For 2025, DOCCS's open data lists 2,409 approved, 1,893 denied and 1,370 postponed initial interviews. Counting postponements in the total the way the 2024 report does, that is 42.5 percent approved. It is our arithmetic, and we get 41.4 percent for 2024 the same way, which matches DOCCS.

For people with an Earned Eligibility Certificate, the law is stricter. Correction Law 805 says a certificate holder "shall be granted parole release" unless the Board makes two findings. In DOCCS's latest report, covering October 2025 through March 2026, the Board held 1,102 certificate interviews. It approved 700 and denied 402. Conditional release, the date-based exit for people the Board turns down, now covers about as many releases as parole does, as we reported in October.

On parole's other end, DOCCS counts 24,655 parolees, a figure that includes people in the violation process and absconders, and supervises 17,534 active releasees with 527 parole officers, supervisors included, as of August 2026.

What these numbers do not say

No source here links a parole denial to a death.

What these numbers do not say

No source here links a parole denial to a death. Rikers is a jail, where most people are waiting for trial. The state prisons hold people already sentenced. The two agencies count differently, and amNY, The City and the Eagle report the Rikers figures without a Department of Correction table behind them. The state's "unknown" means the manner is not yet determined, not that anything suspicious happened. The 149 is our count of the entries on DOCCS's page, which has no total and no date for its last update.

On "the state prisons are worse": 149 deaths among about 34,143 people is roughly 44 per 10,000, and 9 among about 6,602 is roughly 14 per 10,000. That is our arithmetic, using today's populations as the denominators. A rate that fits one year of one agency's records is not an exact comparison between the two systems. DOCCS's own 2024 rate was 43.38 per 10,000.

What we do not know yet

We do not know how Mr. Medero Curet died. The medical examiner and the Board of Correction will answer that, and the Department of Correction said it notified both. We have not read DOCCS's November fact sheet, which its deaths page links to, so the manner-of-death table here stops at August 1. We also have no number for how many of the deaths involved someone with a parole hearing pending, because no public source lists it. The same gap exists for Clean Slate sealing: the city counts the people in its jails every morning, and no one publishes a count of records sealed.
