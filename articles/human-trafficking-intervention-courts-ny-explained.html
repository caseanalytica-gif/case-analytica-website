New York Calls Them Victims, Then Keeps Their Case Open Anyway: Inside Human Trafficking Intervention Courts

When New York launched its Human Trafficking Intervention Courts in 2013, the message to the public was direct. Then-Chief Judge Jonathan Lippman said the courts would treat the people appearing in them "as victims, not criminals." That's the pitch anyone facing a prostitution-related charge in New York is likely to hear before they agree to walk into one of these courtrooms. Here's what the same court system's own data, and two separate research studies, say actually happens once someone accepts that offer.

What an HTIC actually is

New York's Human Trafficking Intervention Courts, described on the Unified Court System's own HTIC page, operate in courthouses in the Bronx, Brooklyn, Manhattan, Queens, Staten Island, and several counties outside the city. They're built on the same "problem-solving court" model as drug treatment courts and mental health courts: a specially trained judge, a set of mandated services instead of standard prosecution, and the premise that most people charged with prostitution-related offenses are trafficking victims who need help, not defendants who need punishment.

The court system's own page states plainly that New York law criminalizes sex trafficking and removes the need to prove force, fraud, or coercion for victims under 18. It also acknowledges, in the same breath, that "arrests for sex trafficking remain relatively low." The state's own 2023 arrest figures show exactly how low. According to the court system's page, that year New York recorded 385 prostitution-related arrests, 118 arrests for patronizing a person engaged in prostitution, 84 arrests for compelling, permitting, or promoting prostitution, and 6 arrests for sex trafficking itself. The people selling sex get arrested more than three times as often as the people buying it, and more than sixty times as often as anyone charged with trafficking them.

Read that again

A court built around the idea that the people in front of it are trafficking victims is fed almost entirely by arrests of the person selling sex, not the buyer and not the trafficker. If trafficking were actually driving these numbers, the arrest data would look nothing like this.

The mechanism: an ACD that isn't actually a dismissal

Here's how a case moves through an HTIC. A defendant, usually a woman, is arrested on a prostitution-related charge and offered a track in the specialized court instead of standard prosecution. She's required to complete a set number of mandated services, counseling, case management, sometimes drug treatment, and in exchange the court typically offers an Adjournment in Contemplation of Dismissal, an ACD. As Case Analytica has explained elsewhere, an ACD isn't a dismissal on the spot. It keeps the case open, usually for six months, and only dismisses and seals it if the person doesn't get rearrested and finishes what the court required in the meantime.

Researchers from the Yale Global Health Justice Partnership and the Sex Workers Project, in a 2018 study covered by The Appeal, put the obvious question to the court system directly: "If someone is a trafficking victim, why should there be an ACD at all?" It's a fair question. A victim of a crime isn't usually asked to complete a service plan under threat of prosecution in order to have the charge against her go away. That's what happens to a defendant.

What the researchers actually found

The study found the HTICs they observed in the Bronx, Brooklyn, Manhattan, and Queens don't do any systematic screening to determine whether a given defendant was actually trafficked. As one of the study's co-directors, Alice Miller, told The Appeal: "Nobody can actually account for what they are doing, and yet they claim these are evidence-based interventions." A corrections officer working inside one HTIC told researchers other court staff referred to the court's day on the calendar as "hoe day."

Not everyone in the room is actually a trafficking victim

The court's own framing assumes the person in front of the judge was coerced into selling sex. A 2017 study from the Center for Court Innovation, Navigating Force and Choice: Experiences in the New York City Sex Trade and the Criminal Justice System's Response, found that only 45 percent of the people appearing in New York City's HTICs actually met the federal legal definition of a trafficking victim. That means more than half the people being run through a court built on the "victim, not criminal" premise don't fit the premise the court was built around, and the court isn't set up to tell the difference before it mandates services and holds a case open over someone's head.

That mismatch cuts both ways. Some people who are genuinely being trafficked may not disclose it at arrest, out of fear, distrust, or active control by a trafficker, and never get connected to the resources they actually need. Others who entered sex work without coercion get treated as if they must have a trafficker, whether or not that's true. Jessica Peñaranda of the Sex Workers Project described it to The Appeal as a "rescue mentality" that assumes "anyone engaging in any kind of sex work had to be trafficked." Neither error serves the person standing in front of the judge.

Mandated help isn't the same as informed consent

Some defendants told researchers the courts treated them with real care, and some public defenders said HTICs produce better outcomes than standard prosecution for their clients, at least some of the time. That's worth saying plainly, because this isn't a case where every single account is negative. But "sometimes better than standard prosecution" isn't the same question as "did this person actually get to choose." Red Umbrella Project organizer Jenna Torres told a 2015 City Council hearing on the courts, as reported by The Appeal, that she had to drop out of school to complete her service mandate or risk being arrested again. "I didn't need to be treated for sex work," she said. "That isn't an illness."

That's the same problem this site keeps coming back to, no matter what courtroom it shows up in. A defendant told she's being treated as a victim, offered a plan that sounds like help, and warned that the alternative is prosecution, is not in a position to give informed, pressure-free consent to that plan. She's in the same position as anyone else in the system who's handed a choice that isn't really a choice: take what's offered, or risk something worse. The label on the courtroom door doesn't change that dynamic. It just makes the pressure sound like compassion.

The remedy that actually exists for real trafficking victims

For someone who was genuinely trafficked, New York already has a more direct legal tool than a mandated service plan and a six-month ACD. Under CPL 440.10(1)(i) and 440.10(6), first enacted in 2010 and expanded in 2016 to cover labor trafficking, a person can petition a court to vacate a conviction, not just seal it, if the underlying offense was a result of having been trafficked. Vacatur is a stronger remedy than an ACD: it doesn't require completing a service plan first, and it directly acknowledges that the person didn't belong in the criminal system in the first place, rather than requiring months of compliance before the record clears. That statute exists specifically because New York was the first state in the country to recognize that a trafficking survivor's conviction is fundamentally different from an ordinary one.

The question to actually ask before agreeing to an HTIC track

If you or someone you know is offered a path through an HTIC, ask directly: what happens to the case if the mandated services aren't finished on the court's timeline, is a straight ACD available without the service mandate, and does CPL 440.10 vacatur apply instead if trafficking can actually be documented. Those are different legal outcomes, and the difference matters for what shows up on a record afterward.

A criminal record, even one from a case that started with an ACD, can still complicate a job application or a lease until it's actually sealed. See Does a Conviction Actually Show Up on a Background Check in New York? for how that timeline works, and The Clean Slate Act for what automatic sealing does and doesn't cover. None of that erases the real support some people do get through an HTIC. It just means the "victim, not criminal" framing shouldn't be the only thing anyone hears before deciding whether to walk into one.
