The Protect Our Courts Act: Why ICE Needs a Judicial Warrant to Arrest You at a NY Courthouse

Going to court should not require calculating the odds of being arrested by a different agency for a different reason on the way in. For a stretch of years, that was exactly the calculation some New Yorkers had to make. Federal guidance changed in 2017, and civil immigration arrests at New York state courthouses jumped 1,200 percent over the next three years, according to the case record cited in a November 2025 federal ruling. New York passed a law to shut that down. It is still standing, and a federal judge just said so in writing.

What the law actually says

The Protect Our Courts Act is codified at Civil Rights Law § 28. The text is direct: a person "duly and in good faith attending a court proceeding" as a party or potential witness, or whose family or household member is a party or potential witness, is privileged from civil arrest while going to, remaining at, and returning from that court proceeding. The only exception is a civil arrest supported by a judicial warrant or judicial order. Violating that privilege is contempt of court and false imprisonment, and a person who believes it happened or is about to happen can sue for equitable relief, as can the state attorney general on the public's behalf.

"Judicial warrant" means what it sounds like, and ICE usually doesn't have one

The statute defines a judicial warrant as one issued by an actual judge or magistrate, federal or state, sitting in the judicial branch. An administrative warrant, the kind ICE typically carries, signed by an ICE supervisor rather than a judge, does not count. That distinction is the entire law in practice: it does not ban immigration enforcement, it bans doing it at a courthouse without a judge's signature on the paper.

Signed into law in December 2020, the statute applies specifically to civil arrests, defined as arrests that are not for initiating a criminal prosecution and not for contempt of the court where the proceeding is happening. It protects a "family or household member" of a party or witness too, not just the person with business before the court that day. It also covers the walk to the courthouse and the walk away from it, not just the hallway outside the courtroom door.

New York just won the fight to keep it

In June 2025, the federal government sued to strike the law down, arguing the Constitution's supremacy clause put courthouse arrests beyond a state's power to restrict. On November 17, 2025, U.S. District Judge Mae D'Agostino dismissed the case, ruling that New York was not regulating federal agents but simply setting rules for its own state-owned buildings, and that the Tenth Amendment lets a state make that call. Her ruling also upheld a pair of related executive orders limiting state cooperation with ICE inside other state facilities. New York Attorney General Letitia James called the outcome confirmation that "anyone can use New York's state courts without being targeted by federal authorities."

What the ruling does not cover

This decision protects state, city, and municipal courthouses. It says nothing about federal immigration courts, where ICE has continued to wait outside courtrooms to detain people immediately after their own hearings. A separate federal lawsuit over that practice is pending in the Southern District of New York. If the proceeding you or a family member is attending is a federal immigration hearing rather than a New York State criminal, family, or civil case, this law does not reach that building.

The exact question to ask

If ICE agents approach someone at or near a New York state courthouse, the first question that matters is simple: do you have a judicial warrant, signed by a judge, and can I see it? An administrative warrant is not enough under this statute. Do not assume the agents standing outside know or care about that distinction on their own. The law exists because they didn't, until a court made them.
