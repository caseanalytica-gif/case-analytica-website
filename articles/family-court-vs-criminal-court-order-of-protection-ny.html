Family Court or Criminal Court? What Decides Where a NY Order of Protection Case Goes

A family offense in New York doesn't automatically land in one court. It can go to family court, criminal court, or both at the same time, and almost nobody explains that this is actually a choice, one with real consequences attached, rather than something a clerk or a police officer simply decides for you. Know the difference before you file anything, because the two systems ask for different proof, hand control of the case to different people, and end in different places.

Two courts, the same underlying conduct

Under Family Court Act § 812, family court and criminal court have concurrent jurisdiction over a specific list of offenses, including harassment, menacing, stalking, criminal mischief, reckless endangerment, strangulation, and assault, when they happen between people the law defines as family or household members. That definition is broader than most people assume: it covers spouses and former spouses, parents and children, relatives by blood or marriage, people who have a child together regardless of whether they were ever married, and people who are or were in an intimate relationship, even if they never lived together. If the conduct and the relationship both fit, you generally get to choose where the case is heard, unless the police make that choice for you first by making an arrest.

What actually changes depending on which one you pick

The New York State Unified Court System's own comparison lays out the practical differences, and they are not small. A family court case is civil, brought by you against the other party, and decided on a "preponderance of the evidence," the lower civil standard. You control it: you can withdraw the petition if you change your mind. The records stay private, though the courtroom itself is open to the public. A criminal court case is brought by the District Attorney on behalf of the People of the State of New York, decided on the higher "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard, and only the District Attorney can withdraw it, not you. A criminal case can also end in an actual conviction and, in some cases, prison. A family court case cannot; the most it can do is issue an order of protection or a related civil disposition.

Filing in one doesn't close off the other

Choosing family court does not stop you from also going to the District Attorney about the same conduct, and vice versa. The two systems run independently. But be clear-eyed about one thing the court system itself flags: sometimes the decision gets made without you, if police respond to an incident and arrest the other person. Once that happens, the criminal case moves forward under the DA's control regardless of what you'd have chosen.

How long an order actually lasts, and what it can require

An order of protection issued out of family court is governed by Family Court Act § 842. The default cap is two years. A judge can extend that to five years, but only with a specific finding on the record, either that aggravating circumstances exist or that the person violated a prior valid order. Beyond keeping someone away from a home, school, or workplace, the statute lets a court order a range of other conditions: temporary child custody and support, surrender of firearms, return of identification documents, participation in a batterer's education program, and more. None of this is automatic. It depends on what's actually requested and proven at the hearing, and on the record the judge is required to build to justify anything beyond the baseline.

The exact question to ask

Before filing anywhere, ask directly: "If I go to family court, does that affect whether the District Attorney can also bring criminal charges over the same conduct, and which court gives me more control if I later want the case dropped?" If a lawyer or an advocate glosses over the difference between "preponderance of the evidence" and "beyond a reasonable doubt," ask them to explain, in plain terms, what each one actually requires to win.

This same choice-of-venue issue shows up on the other side of a criminal charge too. See how a criminal court order of protection actually works in New York for what happens when the order arises as a condition of someone else's criminal case, not your own petition. And if the underlying conduct involves abuse that later becomes relevant to a defendant's own sentencing, see what the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act actually allows a judge to consider. Know which system you're actually in, and what it requires, before you sign anything or agree to withdraw anything.
