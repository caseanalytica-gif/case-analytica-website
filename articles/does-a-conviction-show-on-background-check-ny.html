Does a Conviction Actually Show Up on a Background Check in New York?

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-07-30

Reflects law as of September 19, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn Republish

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

Short answer: it depends on whether the record has been sealed, and if it hasn't been sealed yet, yes, it can show up, and it can cost someone a job over something that happened years ago.

The direct answer

A conviction that hasn't been sealed is generally visible to standard employment background check companies, since those checks pull from public court records. Once a record is sealed under New York's Clean Slate Act, it's removed from public view, which means it becomes invisible to most employers, landlords, and background check services running a standard check.

That word "most" matters. Certain employers, including law enforcement, some licensing boards, and specific state agencies, can still access sealed records for particular purposes even after sealing. For the vast majority of job applications, though, sealing is the practical dividing line between a record that follows someone indefinitely and one that stops appearing.

Federal law adds another layer

Separately from state sealing, the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act limits how far back a background check company can reach. Do not read that as a seven year clock on a conviction. The limit covers arrests, civil suits and civil judgments, and the statute carves convictions out of it by name. Under federal law a conviction can be reported indefinitely. Sealing under state law, not the passage of time under federal law, is what takes a New York conviction off a standard check. If a specific job application is at stake right now, confirm how the two interact with a legal aid organization or an employment attorney.

When does the sealing clock actually start

This is the part most people get wrong, and it's exactly the mechanics we cover in full in The Clean Slate Act: When Does Your NY Record Actually Get Sealed?. In short: 3 years for a misdemeanor and 8 years for a felony, both measured from release from incarceration, or from sentencing if there was no jail time. Not from the arrest, and not from the end of parole. Being under supervision does not pause that clock. What it does block is the sealing itself, which cannot happen while someone is still on probation or parole for that same conviction. Two kinds of convictions never seal under Clean Slate, no matter how much time passes: sex offenses and sexually violent offenses as Correction Law 168-a defines them, and class A felonies other than class A drug felonies. Other violent felonies are not on that list. They seal on the eight-year felony schedule like any other eligible conviction.

What about a violation?

When a case ends in a conviction for a violation or a traffic infraction, CPL 160.55 seals the records "on file with the division of criminal justice services, police agency, or prosecutor's office," and it does so right away. That holds no matter what the original charge was, so a misdemeanor arrest that ends in a plea to disorderly conduct is covered. Sealed records are "not made available to any person or public or private agency," with exceptions that include you, gun licensing officials, and police with a court order.

A violation, such as disorderly conduct, is not a crime in New York. Penal Law § 10.00 defines a crime as a misdemeanor or a felony, and a violation as an offense that can't carry more than 15 days in jail.

Two limits. It doesn't cover driving while ability impaired. And the prosecutor can ask the court to block the sealing, or the court can block it on its own, if "the interests of justice require otherwise."

What to do if a job application is on the line right now

Confirm the exact status of the specific record with the county clerk's office or a legal aid organization before assuming either the best or the worst case. If the record should already be sealed and is still surfacing on a background check, that's worth raising directly, since it may point to an error in how the record was processed rather than an actual sealing exclusion. See why that gap exists in the first place: New York gave its own court system until November 2027 to finish building the automated sealing system, so a record that qualifies on paper isn't always sealed yet in practice. See also what the agencies actually told lawmakers about funding and staffing that system, and whether the state is on track to hit that deadline.

If the record isn't sealed yet and a specific job or license is genuinely on the line, sealing isn't the only tool available. A Certificate of Relief from Disabilities removes the same kind of employment and licensing bars without waiting on the sealing clock. See Certificate of Relief from Disabilities in New York for who qualifies and how to request one.

A background check for an apartment works differently than one for a job. If a landlord is the one asking, see the Fair Chance for Housing Act explained for the specific window that decides what a New York City housing provider can legally hold against an applicant.

General information, not legal advice

Sealing rules and background check practices involve specific facts a general article can't fully resolve. Confirm the status of a specific record with the applicable county clerk's office or a licensed attorney.

The exact question to ask

If a specific job or apartment is on the line, ask the employer or landlord directly: "What exactly did the background check report, and which company ran it?" Under federal law you are entitled to a copy of the report and to dispute what is in it. If a sealed conviction is still showing, that is an error in the report, not a reason you were disqualified, and it can be corrected. Ask for it in writing before you accept that the record is why you were turned down.
