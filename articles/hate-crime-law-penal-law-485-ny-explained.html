How New York's Hate Crime Law Turns One Attack Into Multiple Enhanced Felonies: Penal Law Article 485 Explained

On September 8, 2026, the Manhattan District Attorney's Office announced a grand jury indictment against Raul Morales, 51, on ten counts stemming from two stabbings on the Upper West Side. According to the DA's own press release, every count carries a hate crime designation: four counts of second-degree attempted murder as a hate crime, two counts of first-degree assault as a hate crime, two counts of attempted first-degree assault as a hate crime, and two counts of second-degree assault as a hate crime. District Attorney Alvin Bragg said Morales "was allegedly motivated by antisemitism when he attacked two New Yorkers in broad daylight near two Upper West Side synagogues."

As alleged in the DA's release, on July 23, 2026, Morales approached a 57-year-old Asian man from behind on West 84th Street and stabbed him in the back with a knife, unprovoked, then fled while allegedly yelling "Allahu Akbar." Roughly twelve minutes later, near a second synagogue on West 86th Street, he allegedly approached a 50-year-old Jewish man wearing a kippah and stabbed him in the chest with a screwdriver. Both men survived. The DA's office says a later search of Morales's phone turned up text messages it characterizes as containing antisemitic sentiments, which is what moved the charges from a standard assault case to the expanded hate crime indictment. Morales has not entered a plea in the reporting reviewed for this piece. Nothing here is a comment on whether he did what he's accused of; that determination belongs to a jury, not to a general explainer, and every fact above comes directly from the DA's own release or reporting on it, not from any outside characterization of Morales himself.

What this case actually illustrates, regardless of how it resolves, is something a lot of New Yorkers never think about until they're facing it: a hate crime charge in New York isn't its own separate crime. It's a label that attaches to an existing charge and changes what that charge is worth in court.

What actually makes something a "hate crime" in New York

Under Penal Law § 485.05, a person commits a hate crime when they commit one of a specific list of underlying offenses (assault, menacing, robbery, arson, criminal mischief, and dozens of others are on that list) and either target the victim, in whole or substantial part, because of a belief about the victim's race, religion, national origin, gender, disability, sexual orientation, or several other protected categories, or commit the act itself for that reason. The statute is explicit that it doesn't matter whether the belief is even correct. If someone is attacked because the person committing the crime believed, wrongly or not, that the victim belonged to a particular group, the hate crime statute can still apply. That detail matters here: several outlets, including Gothamist and West Side Rag, reported that the first victim was an Asian man, not a member of the religious group prosecutors say Morales believed he was targeting. Under the statute, that distinction doesn't get the charge thrown out.

The law also requires more than the defendant's own background or the victim's. Under the same section, proof of the race, religion, or other protected characteristic of the defendant, the victim, or both, is not by itself legally sufficient to prove the hate crime element. Prosecutors still have to show the belief or motive actually drove the act, which is why the DA's office pointed to a specific piece of evidence, the recovered text messages, as what let it add the hate crime designation to every count.

The designation doesn't add a new charge. It upgrades the one that already exists.

This is the part most people miss. Under Penal Law § 485.10, when someone is convicted of a hate crime and the underlying offense is a misdemeanor or a class C, D, or E felony, the hate crime is automatically bumped one full category higher than the original charge. A misdemeanor becomes a felony. A class D felony becomes a class C. There's no separate hate crime sentence added on top; the underlying charge itself gets more serious.

When the underlying offense is already a violent felony, like the second-degree attempted murder and first-degree assault counts in this indictment, the statute works differently and, in some ways, more severely. The hate crime designation is deemed a violent felony offense in its own right, and for a class B violent felony specifically, the law sets hard floors on the sentence a judge can impose: a determinate term of at least eight years if sentenced under the standard violent felony provision, or at least twelve years if the defendant qualifies as a second violent felony offender. Those aren't maximums. They're the minimum the statute allows, regardless of what a judge might otherwise think the facts call for. On top of the sentence itself, the law also requires the court to make the defendant complete a hate crime prevention and education program as part of whatever sentence is imposed, where the court finds one is available and appropriate.

Why the label changes more than the paperwork

A hate crime designation doesn't just add a scarier-sounding word to an indictment. It resets the floor on what a plea can even look like. When the underlying charge already carries a mandatory minimum, adding the hate crime label can raise that minimum by years, which is exactly the kind of fact that changes what a defense attorney is negotiating toward from day one. Anyone facing an enhanced charge like this needs a lawyer who understands the enhancement itself, not just the underlying offense.

Does a diversion program or restorative justice option ever apply here?

For a case built on class B and C violent felony counts, realistically, no. Programs like Project Reset are built for low-level misdemeanors resolved through a Desk Appearance Ticket, and judicial diversion under CPL Article 216 is limited by statute to specific felony drug offenses. Neither reaches an attempted murder or first-degree assault count, hate crime designation or not. That's consistent with what Case Analytica has explained about diversion eligibility in violent felony cases: the more serious the underlying charge, the narrower the real path toward anything other than standard prosecution, and a hate crime enhancement only narrows that path further by raising the mandatory floor on a plea.

That's exactly why the question "is this case really as locked-in as the DA's press release makes it sound" still matters, even here. A hate crime designation depends entirely on proving motive, not just the underlying act, and motive is the kind of fact a defense attorney can contest at every stage, from a pretrial hearing on the evidence to the trial itself. It's also why Case Analytica has written about what a plea offer actually represents: a bet on what a jury would do with the DA's actual proof, not a verdict already reached. A hate crime enhancement changes the number the DA is betting with. It doesn't change the burden of proof.

General information, not legal advice

Penal Law Article 485 has real mechanics that apply the same way in every New York hate crime case, but how those mechanics interact with a specific set of facts, a specific charge, and a specific defendant's record is something only a licensed criminal defense attorney reviewing the actual file can evaluate.
