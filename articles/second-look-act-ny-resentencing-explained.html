New York's Second Look Act Clears First Committee Vote in 2026

On May 18, 2026, the New York State Senate's Codes Committee voted 9 to 4 to advance a bill that would let someone who has served at least ten years in state prison ask a judge, a different judge than the one who originally sentenced them, to take a second look at that sentence. The bill is called the Second Look Act. It's been introduced in some form since 2021 and never made it this far before.

New York's Chief Judge, Rowan Wilson, has publicly urged the Legislature to pass it. A May 2026 symposium at Brooklyn Law School put him in a room with the bill's own sponsors to talk about it directly. None of that means it becomes law. It does mean this is the most real movement the bill has had in five years, and it's worth understanding exactly what it would do, not just that people are talking about it.

What the bill actually authorizes

Senate Bill S158, sponsored by Senator Julia Salazar, and its Assembly companion A1283, sponsored by Assembly Member Latrice Walker, would add a new section, CPL 440.45, to New York's criminal procedure law. Anyone confined by the Department of Corrections and Community Supervision who has served ten years, or half of an indeterminate or determinate sentence of ten years or more, whichever is less, could apply for a sentence reduction. The application goes to a different judge than the one who handed down the original sentence, not the same one, and the incarcerated person has a right to counsel for it.

The bill doesn't hand out automatic reductions. A judge has to weigh a specific list of factors: the applicant's history and characteristics, including any documented rehabilitation and their record while incarcerated; the circumstances of the original offense and the applicant's actual role in it; any relevant physical or mental health evaluation; input from the victim or the victim's family if the victim has died; whether the original sentence was inflated because the person exercised their right to a trial instead of taking a plea; and the financial cost of continued incarceration to the state. There's a rebuttable presumption in favor of a reduction for applicants 55 or older, or for anyone who was 25 or younger at the time of the offense. The court cannot increase anyone's sentence through this process, and a prosecutor can also initiate an application on an incarcerated person's behalf.

One factor worth naming directly

Among the factors a judge must weigh is the applicant's own effort to participate in educational, therapeutic, and vocational programming while incarcerated, to the extent that programming was actually available to them. The bill's text is explicit that a person who wanted to participate but couldn't, because the programming didn't exist inside their facility, can't be penalized for that gap. That single provision is the clearest legislative acknowledgment on this bill of something Case Analytica has said repeatedly: a person's readiness to hold down a job and stable housing after release is treated as evidence that continued incarceration has stopped serving a purpose, not as an afterthought.

Why it's moving now, and why it stalled before

This isn't Salazar's first attempt. Versions of the bill were introduced and referred to committee, without further action, in the 2021-2022 session as S7872, and again in 2023-2024 as S321. This year's version, S158, was prefiled January 8, 2025, and didn't get a committee vote until May 18, 2026, a year and a half later. That vote broke down along fairly predictable lines: nine Democrats in favor, four Republicans opposed. It's currently sitting in the Senate's Crime Victims, Crime and Correction Committee, one step further than it has ever gotten, but still short of a floor vote in either chamber.

The bill's own sponsor memo, filed with the Legislature, cites New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services data from 2019 showing that while Black people made up 15 percent of the state's population that year, they accounted for 48 percent of people sentenced to prison; Latinx people made up 18 percent of the population and accounted for 23 percent of prison sentences. The memo also cites a 2021 Sentencing Project report finding Black people are sentenced to prison in New York at 7.9 times the rate of white people. That's the sponsors' own stated justification for the bill, not an outside claim Case Analytica is making on their behalf, and it's the reason the bill's supporters argue this is a racial disparity problem as much as a sentencing-length problem.

The bill also has support from people who don't have an obvious political incentive to back it: a former DOCCS commissioner, a former New York City corrections and probation commissioner, the American Bar Association, which passed a resolution favoring second look resentencing in 2022, and, according to a Vera Institute of Justice poll cited by Brooklyn Law School's Center for Criminal Justice, 68 percent of New Yorkers generally.

The money question the bill answers itself

Subdivision 10 of the bill requires the state Comptroller to study, three years after the law takes effect, how much money New York saves by reducing sentences under this section, and to recommend redirecting those savings into prison-based and community-based programs specifically designed to counter recidivism: education, therapeutic intervention, keeping families and social networks intact, and post-custodial reentry support. In other words, the bill's own text assumes that money currently spent on years of incarceration that a judge later finds no longer serves a purpose could instead fund exactly the kind of employment and housing support that keeps someone from coming back. That's not Case Analytica's inference. It's written into subdivision 10 itself.

It's worth putting the Second Look Act next to the other major New York reform working on this same problem from the opposite end. The Clean Slate Act seals eligible conviction records after someone has already served their time and stayed clean for years. The Second Look Act is aimed at people still inside, years or decades into a sentence a judge might now find no longer serves a purpose. Different mechanisms, same underlying bet: that clearing a barrier to a stable job or a stable home does more for public safety than another year of custody does.

If someone you know might qualify

This bill is not law yet. It has passed one Senate committee and remains in a second. Nobody should rely on CPL 440.45 existing until it's actually signed. If you're trying to understand what options already exist for someone serving a long sentence right now, see Case Analytica's coverage of how to prepare for an actual NY parole hearing and what the Less Is More Act changed about reincarceration for technical parole violations.

A ten-year floor and a different judge than the one who first imposed the sentence are both deliberate design choices in this bill, not accidents. They're built to keep this from becoming a rubber stamp and to put someone other than the original decision-maker in the room. Whether that's enough to get S158 and A1283 past a floor vote this session is a genuinely open question. What isn't open to argument is what the bill actually says, and right now, what it says is further along than it's ever been before.
