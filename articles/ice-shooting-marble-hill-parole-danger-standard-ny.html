The NYS Parole Board Has to Explain in Writing Why Someone Is a Danger. DHS Hasn't Said Why Its ICE Officer Fired Seven Shots in Marble Hill.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-09

Reflects law as of October 9, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

At about 4 p.m. on Thursday, October 8, an Immigration and Customs Enforcement officer fired seven rounds at a car at West 225th Street and Marble Hill Avenue in Manhattan. One round hit the driver, Oscar Belgal, 28, "below his left ear," NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch said, according to NBC New York. His 5-year-old son was in the back seat. The boy was not hurt and is with relatives. Belgal was taken to NewYork-Presbyterian Allen Hospital and is in stable condition.

Witnesses told ABC7 that a nearby school had just let out. "There was hundreds of kids out here," one said.

New York has a legal standard for deciding that a person is too dangerous to go free, and it applies to people who have earned their way to a presumption of release. The federal government has a standard for when its officers may shoot. The two standards ask different questions. They also leave very different records behind.

What officials have said so far

  • The approach. Tisch said a federal officer in plainclothes walked up to the car "with a gun drawn," followed by a second officer who was also out of uniform (NBC New York). Tisch said police camera footage shows the two officers reaching the driver's window, and the car then moving forward out of the camera's view (ABC7).
  • DHS's account. The Department of Homeland Security said its officers were conducting "a targeted enforcement operation" to arrest a man it described as having a prior grand larceny conviction and multiple prior arrests (ABC7). DHS said Belgal "rammed" ICE vehicles as officers tried to box in his car (NBC New York). As of October 9, DHS had not said what led the officer to fire.
  • The weapon. Witnesses told ABC7 they believed it was an assault rifle. Officials have not said what gun was used.
  • The investigation. ICE said "the FBI and HSI are on the scene." HSI, Homeland Security Investigations, is a division of ICE. Governor Kathy Hochul called for the release of body camera footage (ABC7). Mayor Zohran Mamdani called the shooting "an unconscionable and incredibly serious situation" (amNY).

The danger standard for a certificate holder

Someone serving an indeterminate sentence with a minimum term of eight years or less can earn an Earned Eligibility Certificate by successfully taking part in assigned work and treatment programs. Under Correction Law § 805, that person "shall be granted parole release" at the end of the minimum term. The Parole Board can say no only if it determines "that there is a reasonable probability that, if such incarcerated individual is released, he or she will not live and remain at liberty without violating the law and that his or her release is not compatible with the welfare of society." The law joins those two findings with "and," so the Board has to make both.

The Board does say no. The prison system's own report for April through September 2025 counts 1,101 Board interviews of certificate holders. The Board approved 732 and denied 369. DOCCS counts Board appearances, not individual people.

The law requires each of those denials to come with reasons. Executive Law § 259-i(2)(a) requires the Board to give its reasons for a denial in writing, and says "such reasons shall be given in detail and not in conclusory terms." The person denied can file an administrative appeal, and under § 259-i(4) a Board member who took part in the decision "may not participate in the resolution of that appeal." For how certificate holders prepare for that interview, see Two Questions on the Record.

So the state's finding that a person is a danger is made by a Board panel, written down in detail, handed to the person it is about, and open to challenge.

The danger standard for a federal officer

DHS sets the floor for every ICE officer in Policy Statement 044-05, its department-wide use-of-force policy, last revised February 6, 2023 and still posted on its website. It says:

  • Deadly force is allowed "only when necessary, that is when the LEO has a reasonable belief that the subject of such force poses an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury to the LEO or to another person."
  • "Deadly force shall not be used solely to prevent the escape of a fleeing subject." It is allowed to stop an escape only where the officer reasonably believes the person poses "a significant threat of death or serious physical harm."
  • Officers "are prohibited from discharging firearms at the operator of a moving vehicle" unless deadly force against that driver is justified under the standard above. Before firing, the officer "must take into consideration the hazards that may be posed to law enforcement and innocent bystanders by an out-of-control conveyance."

Under that policy, a car that moves forward is not by itself a reason to shoot the driver. Whether the officer in Marble Hill reasonably believed someone faced an imminent threat of death or serious injury is the question an investigation has to answer. Nothing released so far answers it. In the footage Tisch described, the car moves out of the camera's view.

The record the policy requires is internal. Section VII says "uses of force shall be documented and investigated pursuant to Component policies," and that each component reports firearm discharges to the DHS policy office and to the FBI's national use-of-force data collection. Nothing in it requires the officer's reasons to be given in writing to the man who was shot or made public.

Who can review the shooting

  • Federal investigators. The FBI, and Homeland Security Investigations, which is part of the agency whose officer fired.
  • Not the Attorney General's special unit. The state Attorney General's Office of Special Investigation, created by Executive Law § 70-b, covers incidents "in which the death of a person" is caused by a police officer or certain peace officers. Belgal survived, so that law does not reach this shooting.
  • A local prosecutor, in principle. Marble Hill is part of Manhattan. A state prosecutor can charge a federal officer. Hennepin County, Minnesota, did it this year: it charged ICE officer Christian Castro with assault over a January 14 shooting in Minneapolis in which a man was wounded in the leg, MPR News reported.
  • With an immunity fight attached. A federal officer charged by a state can move the case to federal court and claim immunity under the Supremacy Clause. As CBS News laid out, the court then asks whether the officer was acting within the scope of federal duties, and whether he did so in a "necessary and proper" way. The doctrine goes back to Cunningham v. Neagle in 1890, where the Supreme Court held that a deputy U.S. marshal who killed a man while guarding a Supreme Court justice was "not liable to answer in the courts of California."

As of October 9, Case Analytica found no public statement from the Manhattan District Attorney or the Attorney General about reviewing the shooting.

Two judgments of danger

The Parole Board predicts whether someone will break the law in the future, working from a file and an interview. An officer at a car window decides in seconds whether a threat is imminent. DHS's own policy, drawing on the courts, says officers "are often forced to make split-second judgments" and that force is judged "from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene."

The bar is still "necessary." And the difference in what each side has to put on paper is stark. A person who earned a certificate, served the minimum and was still denied gets the Board's reasons in writing, in detail, with a right to appeal to members who did not decide it. A man shot in the neck with his son in the back seat has, as of October 9, a federal statement about his record and no account of why the officer fired.

The body camera footage Hochul asked for would start to close that gap. Case Analytica will update this article if it is released, if DHS explains the shooting, or if a New York prosecutor opens a case.
