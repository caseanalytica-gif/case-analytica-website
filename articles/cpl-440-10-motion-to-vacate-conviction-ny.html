Is the Case Really Over Once You're Convicted? What CPL 440.10 Actually Lets You Do in New York

People find out about it too late, and almost always the same way. Someone is already years into a sentence when they finally hear, from another incarcerated person or a jailhouse lawyer or a letter from a legal aid clinic, that there was a document they could have filed. Not an appeal. Something else, aimed at a fact that never made it in front of a judge the first time. That's the moment the frustration hits hardest: not that the system is slow, but that nobody told them this existed while it could still have changed anything.

That document is a motion under Criminal Procedure Law § 440.10. It is real, it is available at any time after a judgment is entered, and it is one of the only tools in New York criminal law built specifically for what happened off the record.

What CPL 440.10 actually is, and how it's different from an appeal

An appeal reviews what's already in the court record: the transcript, the exhibits, the rulings a judge made out loud. A CPL 440.10 motion goes to the trial court itself and attacks something that was never entered into that record in the first place. That distinction is the whole point of the statute, and it's the thing most people never have explained to them: an appeal cannot fix a problem that never got written down anywhere.

Under section 440.10(1), a court can vacate a judgment on a specific list of grounds, including that the court never had jurisdiction over the case or the defendant, that the judgment was procured by fraud or duress from the court or the prosecutor, that material evidence used at trial was false and the prosecutor or court knew it at the time, that evidence was obtained in violation of the defendant's constitutional rights, that the defendant was incapable of understanding or participating in the proceedings because of a mental disease or defect, or that newly discovered evidence exists that could not have been produced at trial and that would probably have changed the verdict. A separate ground covers forensic DNA testing performed after conviction. Another covers convictions where the defendant's participation in the offense was the result of being a trafficking victim.

The ground most people actually use

The broadest ground in the statute is paragraph (h): that the judgment was obtained in violation of a right of the defendant under the New York or federal constitution. Ineffective assistance of counsel claims are almost always brought this way. If your lawyer never investigated an alibi, never told you about a diversion program that was actually available, or pushed you into a plea without explaining what you were giving up, that can be the constitutional violation this ground is built for. It has to be proven with facts, not just frustration with the outcome.

The part nobody warns you about: procedural bars

This is where the statute stops being generous. Subdivisions 2 and 3 of section 440.10 lay out situations where a court must, or may, deny the motion outright, regardless of how strong the underlying claim is. A court must deny the motion if the same issue was already decided on a direct appeal, unless the law has changed since then. A court must also deny it if the judgment is still appealable or currently on appeal and the record already contains enough to review the issue there, with one specific carve-out: this bar does not apply to a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel. A court may deny the motion if facts that could have been raised before sentencing, with due diligence, were not raised, and were never ruled on afterward either. And a second CPL 440.10 motion can be denied if the defendant was in a position to raise the same issue on the first one and simply didn't.

Why this matters before you file anything

These bars exist to stop people from using a 440 motion as a second appeal. That means the motion has to be built around something genuinely outside the trial record, argued with specific facts, not general dissatisfaction with how the case went. A motion filed without understanding which of these bars applies to your situation can get denied on a technicality before a judge ever reaches whether the underlying claim is true.

What happens if the motion is granted

If a court grants the motion, it vacates the judgment and either dismisses the accusatory instrument, orders a new trial, or takes other appropriate action under section 440.10(4). Where a plea was involved, the case is generally restored to its pre-plea status, meaning the original charges the defendant faced before pleading guilty come back into play, not just the reduced charge from the plea deal. That's a real consequence to understand going in, and a lawyer should walk through it before a motion is filed, not after.

The exact question to ask

If you or someone you love is already convicted and something about the case never sat right, ask directly: "Is there a fact about my case that a judge never actually saw, something outside the trial record, like a threat, a withheld piece of evidence, or something my own lawyer never told me?" That's the question that determines whether CPL 440.10 even applies. Columbia Law School's Jailhouse Lawyer's Manual devotes a full chapter to Article 440 motions specifically because so many incarcerated people are trying to answer that question without a lawyer in the room to help them.

This connects back to the plea itself

A huge share of these motions trace back to the same root problem covered in why so many people plead guilty in the first place: pressure to settle fast, and a defense attorney who never had the time or the caseload capacity to build the case properly. If that sounds familiar, what New York law actually says about public defender caseloads is worth reading alongside this one, because the same overload that pushes someone toward a bad plea is often the same overload that becomes the ineffective assistance claim years later.

Be informed before you make any decision, whether that's the plea itself or the decision to challenge it later. Nobody hands you this information automatically. You have to ask for it.
