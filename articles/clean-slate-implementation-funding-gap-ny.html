New York Says It Doesn't Need More Money to Seal Your Clean Slate Record

A record qualifying for sealing under New York's Clean Slate Act and a record actually being sealed in the state's system are two different things right now, and the gap between them isn't just a matter of time passing. It's a matter of whether anyone builds the system fast enough to close it. In February and March 2025, the state agencies responsible for doing exactly that told lawmakers, in sworn budget testimony, that they weren't asking for extra money or extra staff to get it done.

That testimony is the clearest public accounting on record of how New York is actually resourcing the sealing process, not just the deadline it gave itself to finish it. It matters because the throughline here isn't abstract: a person can't put a sealed record on a job application until it's actually sealed, and a person who can't pass a background check has a harder time getting hired, which is exactly the condition the research ties to a higher chance of ending back up in the system.

Why the funding question is a jobs and housing question

New York's own governor made the connection explicit when she signed the law: steady work makes someone less likely to end up back in the system. The research behind that statement is real. The Council of State Governments Justice Center's national analysis of state recidivism data found that three-year reincarceration rates dropped from 35 percent of people released in 2008 to 27 percent of people released in 2019, a 23 percent national decline the report ties directly to expanded reentry employment and housing support built up since the 2008 Second Chance Act. Prison Policy Initiative's research library separately puts the unemployment rate among formerly incarcerated people at roughly 27 percent, about five times the general population rate, and names poverty itself as the single strongest predictor of recidivism.

A person waiting on a record that qualifies for sealing but isn't sealed yet is, in practical terms, still carrying that unemployment risk. Every dollar and every staff hour the state doesn't spend speeding up the sealing pipeline is a dollar and an hour that stays on the side of the ledger the recidivism research says makes someone less safe, not more. That's the throughline: this isn't a paperwork delay sitting off to the side of public safety. It's sitting in the middle of it.

What the agencies actually told lawmakers

New York's Clean Slate Law makes roughly 5 million criminal records eligible for sealing, automatically, by November 16, 2027. Reporting from Spectrum News on a state budget hearing that February found that the heads of the agencies building the sealing system, state Chief Administrative Judge Joseph Zayas among them, did not ask lawmakers to add funding for the effort in the upcoming budget. "The Clean Slate issue is easier because we're all over that," Zayas testified, saying the Office of Court Administration had what it needed to implement the law over the following two years and eight months.

The Office of Court Administration is supposed to work with the state Division of Criminal Justice Services to share the data that actually triggers a sealing. According to the same reporting, a DCJS spokesperson said, as of that Friday in March 2025, the court system had not yet sent the department a single record to be sealed. Assemblywoman Catalina Cruz, the bill's Assembly sponsor, said she was ready to push for more funding if the agencies came back and said they needed it. As of that public accounting, they hadn't.

Why "we're good" is worth watching closely

Cruz told Spectrum News several of her own legislative staffers said Clean Slate would directly affect them or someone they love. She's an advocate for the law working the inside track to get it funded if it needs it. But her own account of the testimony makes clear that, as of that hearing, the burden was on the agencies to come back and ask, not on anyone auditing whether "we're good" was actually true. Paul Zuber of the Business Council of New York State told the outlet his organization was still working to educate its 3,400 members, many of them small and mid-sized employers, on the law's basics. If the state's own business community that stands to gain workers from Clean Slate hasn't finished being briefed on it, that's not a sign the sealing machinery is running ahead of schedule.

The deadline gives the agencies room. It doesn't give people a record.

Clean Slate took effect November 16, 2024, but as Case Analytica has reported, the law gave the Unified Court System until November 16, 2027, three full years, to actually finish building the automated sealing system. The New York State Unified Court System's own Clean Slate page confirms that until that system exists, DCJS keeps producing criminal history records that include convictions the law says are supposed to eventually disappear. It also confirms the form that would let someone request a manual review for a record they believe should already be sealed and isn't won't be published until that same November 2027 date.

Put those two facts together with the March 2025 testimony, and the picture is less "the system is being built on schedule" and more "the state agrees it has until 2027, said it doesn't need help getting there, and hadn't started moving data between the two agencies that have to talk to each other to make sealing happen." None of that is a claim about bad faith. It's a claim about what the public record actually shows as of the most recent testimony available, and it's worth someone checking again before 2027, not waiting for it.

If you think your record already qualifies

Check your status through the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services record review process. See Case Analytica's full breakdown of the waiting periods and exclusions for the underlying eligibility rules, and how a conviction actually shows up on a background check in the meantime. If the waiting period doesn't apply to your record at all, a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities can remove many of the same employment barriers without waiting on the sealing system.

Funding and staffing aren't the only obstacle sitting between "we're good" and a record actually being sealed. See the drafting bug in the sealing statute itself that the court system's own tech division says is blocking automatic sealing for cases with more than one conviction on the same docket, and the fix bill that's been sitting in committee since March 2025.

The people who wrote Clean Slate were right that a sealed record changes what's possible on a job application. What the March 2025 testimony shows is that "right" on paper and "built" in practice aren't the same claim, and right now, the only people checking that gap closely are the advocates who pushed the bill through in the first place, not an independent audit of whether the agencies' "we're good" holds up.
