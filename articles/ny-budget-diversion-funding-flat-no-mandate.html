Four State Budgets, No Diversion Mandate. Alternatives-to-Incarceration Funding Is Where It Was in 2023.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-10

Reflects law as of October 10, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

New York's state budget put $31.4 million into alternatives-to-incarceration programs in 2023. The budget passed this May put in $31.42 million. Across those four budgets, the Legislature and Governor Hochul did not require any court to send any charge to treatment instead of jail. The one budget change to diversion law in that time was a single repealed subdivision about where a transferred mental health court case goes if the defendant fails the program.

The money

The budget enacted in May 2023 included "$31.4 million for alternatives to incarceration programs," "$20 million for pretrial services" and $11.5 million for reentry services. The Vera Institute of Justice described that as "doubling funding for alternatives to incarceration" and tripling reentry funding.

By the budget enacted in May 2026, the figure was essentially the same. The Fortune Society reported "steady, baselined funding for Alternative to Incarceration (ATI) programs at $31.42 million" and reentry services at $11.526 million, in a statement on the budget. In March, the Legislature's budget proposals had included "tens of millions of dollars more than Hochul did for violence prevention and alternatives to incarceration programs," New York Focus reported. The final number stayed where it was.

The 2024 budget, enacted in April, put more than $33 million toward people with mental illness in the criminal legal system, according to the governor's office, including:

  • $8.2 million "to place mental health navigators in courts to identify defendants" who could be referred to treatment;
  • $14.6 million for Forensic Assertive Community Treatment teams;
  • $4.3 million for transitional housing "for individuals referred through the court system."

The law

Each year's public protection budget bill is where criminal law changes ride. Here is what each of the four did, from the Senate's record of each bill.

  • 2023, Chapter 55 of 2023. Changed the bail statutes and extended existing program provisions, including those for alternatives to incarceration, to 2025.
  • 2024, Chapter 55 of 2024. Created the crime of assault on a retail worker and let the governor close up to five prisons.
  • 2025, Chapter 55 of 2025. Rolled back the discovery law, let the prison system designate new programs that earn merit time, and allowed up to three prison closures. A separate budget bill widened the involuntary commitment standard and Kendra's Law, both civil processes outside a criminal case.
  • 2026, Chapter 55 of 2026. Part GG: "Subdivision 3 of section 230.21 of the criminal procedure law is REPEALED."

None of the four bills' Part summaries lists a change to Criminal Procedure Law Article 216, the judicial diversion statute. Part GG is the only one that touches a diversion court. It removed a rule, added in February by Chapter 45 of 2026, that sent a case back to the original county's court if a defendant transferred to a neighboring county's mental health court failed to comply with or complete the program. The statute as it stands says the receiving court "may then conduct such action to judgment or other final disposition," and no longer says where a failed case goes.

The bill that would change who gets diverted

The change that would reach every county is outside the budget. The Treatment Court Expansion Act would open diversion to a defendant charged "with any offense" who "has a qualifying diagnosis," set up a diversion part in each county, and let most people in without a guilty plea, according to the text of the current Senate bill, S10706. It has never reached a floor vote. On September 23 the Senate struck the earlier version, S4547, after it had cleared committee twice, and refiled the bill the same day. Case Analytica's article on the bill covers what it would do and where it stands.

What isn't public

The state has not published where the court navigators funded in 2024 are working. A state job posting for the program, run by the Office of Mental Health's Diversion Center, says the navigators are placed in counties with high arrest rates for people with serious mental illness and that taking part is voluntary. Erie County sought a provider for one navigator team in April 2025. We found no statewide list of counties and no evaluation.

We also found no published count of cases moved to a neighboring county's mental health court since the transfer law took effect in December 2025. What is public is the gap the transfer rule is meant to patch: 30 counties have a mental health court and about a dozen more are in development, according to the Office of Court Administration, as reported by New York Focus, out of 62.
