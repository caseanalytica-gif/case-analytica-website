Post-Release Supervision in New York: The Prison Time Hidden Inside a "Determinate" Sentence

Someone pleads guilty to a determinate sentence of five years. They do the five years. They come home. Then, months or years later, a supervision officer writes them up for missing a check-in, and they're back in prison, no new charge filed. Most people never understood that the five-year number they agreed to was never the whole sentence. It came with a tail attached, and the tail can put them back inside on its own.

What post-release supervision actually is

Penal Law § 70.45 requires that when a court imposes a determinate sentence, it has to state not just the prison term but also a fixed period of post-release supervision (PRS) to follow it. For most determinate sentences, that period runs up to five years, with longer and even lifetime supervision periods possible for certain sex offenses. This isn't parole. Parole is a discretionary decision the Board of Parole makes about early release from an indeterminate sentence. PRS is mandatory, it's fixed by statute according to the offense, and it's supposed to be pronounced by the sentencing judge as part of the sentence itself, not decided afterward by anyone else.

That distinction matters because of what happens if it's violated. Under § 70.45, breaking any condition of post-release supervision "shall subject the defendant to a further period of imprisonment up to the balance of the remaining period of post-release supervision." No new crime has to be committed. A missed appointment, a positive drug test, an unapproved change of address can be enough, and someone can be sent back for however much of that supervision term is left.

The case that forced judges to actually say it

For years, DOCCS added PRS to people's sentences administratively, after the fact, when a judge hadn't mentioned it at sentencing. In Earley v. Murray, 451 F.3d 71 (2d Cir. 2006), the Second Circuit put a stop to that. Sean Earley had pleaded to six years in prison. Nobody told him a five-year term of post-release supervision came with it, because the sentencing judge never said so on the record. DOCCS tacked it on anyway, administratively, without a judge involved. The Second Circuit held that a sentence is a judicial act: only a judge, on the record, can impose one. DOCCS adding a supervision term after the fact was unconstitutional, full stop.

New York's own Court of Appeals agreed under state law two years later. The legislature responded by enacting Correction Law § 601-d, which set up a formal process for anyone who had PRS administratively imposed between September 1998 and 2008 to get a real resentencing hearing, with appointed counsel, in front of an actual judge.

Why this still matters now

If someone you know was sentenced to a determinate term anywhere in that window and isn't sure whether a judge actually stated a post-release supervision term on the record at sentencing, § 601-d's resentencing process may still be relevant to how that supervision term is being enforced today. This is exactly the kind of procedural detail that gets buried in a case file nobody reread after the plea was entered.

The exact question to ask

Before any plea to a determinate sentence, ask directly: "Does this sentence include post-release supervision, and for how long?" Make the judge state it on the record at the plea, not just in paperwork signed after the fact. Ask what specific conditions will apply during that period, and what the realistic consequences are for a technical violation that isn't a new crime. A five-year prison sentence with a five-year PRS tail is functionally a ten-year exposure to incarceration, and the decision to accept a plea should be made with that full number in view, not just the part that gets said out loud in the courtroom that day.

The violation process itself runs close to how parole violations are handled; see what happens if you violate parole in New York for how that hearing actually works. And if a plea is still in front of you, this is exactly the kind of number that belongs in the conversation covered in the question before you plead guilty: know the full exposure before you agree to anything.
