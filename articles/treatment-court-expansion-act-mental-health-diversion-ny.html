Half of New York's Counties Have No Mental Health Court. The Bill to Change That Was Refiled on September 23.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-10

Reflects law as of October 10, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

If someone you love is charged in a New York county that has no mental health court, one law can help, and it is narrow. Outside New York City, the case can be moved to a mental health court in a neighboring county, but only if the defendant asks and the district attorneys of both counties agree. The bill that would put a diversion court in every county has never had a floor vote. On September 23, 2026, the Senate struck its old version, S4547, and refiled it the same day as S10706.

Where the courts are, and how long they take

Thirty counties have a mental health court and about a dozen more are building one, according to the Office of Court Administration, as reported by New York Focus. That leaves about half of the state's 62 counties without one. Roughly 1,700 people were active, compliant participants statewide last year, according to court system data New York Focus obtained through a public records request. Unlike drug courts, New York Focus notes, mental health courts aren't required by state law.

Where they do exist, they are slow. At the Brooklyn Mental Health Court, the median time from arrest to a defendant's first contact with the court's clinical staff was 265 days last year, according to Center for Justice Innovation data in the same report. The typical participant then waited another 104 days to learn whether they were eligible, and 56 more to start the program.

Tracy Barber, a Manhattan resident, landed on Rikers Island with a felony assault charge in early 2019 and spent a couple of months in a jail psychiatric ward. Many months later, she was diverted into the Manhattan Mental Health Court. "I just was not in touch with reality," she told New York Focus. Of jail, she said: "I think it just breaks people."

What the law allows today: a transfer, with two DAs' consent

The transfer rule is Criminal Procedure Law section 230.21. It has changed three times in under six months.

  • December 5, 2025. Governor Hochul signed Chapter 587 of the Laws of 2025, which added mental health courts to a 2021 law that already let cases move to an adjoining county's veterans treatment court or human trafficking court. It passed the Senate 62 to 0.
  • February 13, 2026. A follow-up law, Chapter 45 of the Laws of 2026, required the receiving court to coordinate treatment with the local mental hygiene agency of the defendant's home county. It also added a rule that if the defendant failed to comply with or complete the program, the case would go back to the original court.
  • May 2026. The state budget repealed that send-back rule. Part GG of the public protection budget bill, Chapter 55 of the Laws of 2026, reads in full: "Subdivision 3 of section 230.21 of the criminal procedure law is REPEALED."

What the statute says now: "In any county outside a city having a population of one million or more," the court may move the case "upon motion of the defendant and with consent of the district attorney and the district attorney of the adjoining county." The receiving court "may then conduct such action to judgment or other final disposition." The statute no longer says what happens if a transferred defendant does not finish the program.

Three limits matter for anyone thinking about this route. It does not apply in New York City. The defendant has to ask for it. And either district attorney can say no, with no standard in the statute for when they may refuse.

What the Treatment Court Expansion Act would change

The bill has been introduced in some form since the 2019-2020 session. In this session the Senate version, S4547, sponsored by Senator Jessica Ramos, cleared its committee twice, in May 2025 and again on March 5, 2026, and both times was sent on to the Finance Committee. It went no further. On September 23, 2026, the Senate recorded it as "recommitted, enacting clause stricken," and the same day S10706, sponsored by Senator Nathalia Fernandez, was introduced and sent to the Rules Committee. The Assembly version, A4869, sponsored by Assemblymember Phara Souffrant Forrest, has sat in the Ways and Means Committee since January 7, 2026.

According to the text of S10706, the bill would:

  • open diversion to a defendant charged "with any offense" who "has a qualifying diagnosis," a definition that includes "substance use disorders and substance-induced disorders";
  • establish a diversion part in each county;
  • let most participants enter without pleading guilty, except people charged with certain violent felonies under Penal Law section 70.02;
  • keep class A felonies outside the drug law and class B sex offenses under Penal Law Article 130 out unless the prosecutor and the court consent;
  • bar a court from denying treatment "for inability to pay";
  • require a hearing before jail or termination for a violation, with the prosecution bearing the burden "by clear and convincing evidence";
  • make people who finish the program eligible to apply for sealing;
  • take effect one year after it becomes law.

Court of Appeals Chief Judge Rowan Wilson and Chief Administrative Judge Joseph Zayas support the bill, New York Focus reported, as does the New York City Bar Association.

The guilty plea, under current law

Mental health courts today generally take a guilty plea in exchange for treatment, New York Focus reports. Judicial diversion for felony drug cases works the same way by default. Under CPL 216.05(4), "the eligible defendant shall be required to enter a plea of guilty," with two exceptions: when "the people and the court consent" to diversion without a plea, or when the court finds "exceptional circumstances," which the statute ties to severe collateral consequences of a plea. If the participant fails the program, that plea is already on the record. See Case Analytica's explainer on judicial diversion for how the drug-case version works.

The exact question to ask

If mental illness is part of a pending case outside New York City, ask the defense attorney early: "Does this county have a mental health court, and if it doesn't, will you move under CPL 230.21 to transfer the case to the one next door, and have you asked both district attorneys whether they'll consent?" The transfer starts only on the defendant's motion, so nobody else has to raise it. "We'll see how the case goes" is not an answer to that question. Ask which county's court it would be and whether the DA has been asked.
