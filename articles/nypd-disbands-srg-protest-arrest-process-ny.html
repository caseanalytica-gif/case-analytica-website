The NYPD Is Disbanding the Unit That Polices Protests. What Happens to Someone It Arrests Does Not Change

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-11-16

Reflects law as of November 16, 2026

On Monday, Nov. 16, 2026, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch announced that the NYPD will disband its Strategic Response Group, the SRG, the unit the city's Department of Investigation wrote in 2020 had "been a primary resource for the NYPD's response to large-scale protests" since it was created in 2015. The announcement changes who shows up at a protest. It does not change what the law says about a person who is arrested at one. ABC7 reports that officers will still make protest arrests. The charges, the court ticket and the sealing rules all come from state statutes, and none of the reports says those are changing.

Case Analytica explains how New York's arrest and court process works, and a protest arrest is one way into it. This article follows that process step by step from the statute text and sets the new plan beside it.

What was announced

amNY, CBS New York and ABC7 all reported the plan on Nov. 16. A civilian-led Community Dialogue and De-escalation Division would sit inside the Mayor's Office of Community Safety, with no weapons and no arrest powers. A 30-officer First Amendment Support Team, called FAST, in the NYPD's Community Affairs Bureau would handle dialogue first. A new Patrol Support Division of vetted officers would make protest arrests "in rare instances, if necessary," ABC7 reports.

The plan bans kettling, tear gas and long guns at First Amendment events, says pepper spray will no longer be a general crowd-management tool, and caps long-range acoustic devices at 90 decibels, according to amNY and ABC7. Counterterrorism work, including heavy weapons, moves to the Intelligence and Counterterrorism Bureau. ABC7 puts the SRG at 500 uniformed officers, per Tisch, and says they will be reassigned, many to the department's Counterterrorism Bureau. The DOI described the unit as approximately 700 officers in 2020. amNY reports the NYPD's headcount stays the same and the change is made at no additional cost.

The reports differ on timing. ABC7 says the unit is to be dissolved in the spring. CBS says the reforms begin in January. amNY says City Hall has not announced a firm timetable. None of the three names the legal instrument that carries the change.

A City Council bill from Council Member Chi Ossé, Int. 0796-2026, would put part of it in law. It says "The department shall not deploy officers assigned to the strategic response group in response to any first amendment event," bans kettling, and requires a quarterly report of "the number of arrests made, in connection with such event disaggregated by the highest offense charged." It was introduced on March 26, 2026, and the Council's record on Nov. 16 shows it in the Public Safety Committee, with 27 sponsors.

Reaction is split. CBS reports that Patrick Hendry, president of the police union, said the mayor is "showing his true colors," and that Council Member Phil Wong said the mayor is "more interested in dismantling specialized police units to appease activists" and wants to keep the SRG.

What a protest arrest is charged as

The Department of Investigation (DOI) report on the 2020 George Floyd protests shows which charges people faced. In the DOI's data on arrests processed at the mass-arrest centers, a public-order charge "such as disorderly conduct or unlawful assembly" was the top charge for 42% of arrests on May 28, 85% on May 29 and 89% on May 30. Obstructing governmental administration was the top charge for 32% on May 28. From June 1 on, curfew violations led. Three of those charges are in the Penal Law:

  • Disorderly conduct, § 240.20, is a violation, not a crime. Seven separate acts qualify. Two concern streets and crowds: obstructing vehicular or pedestrian traffic, and congregating with others in a public place and refusing "a lawful order of the police to disperse." All seven require intent to cause "public inconvenience, annoyance or alarm," or recklessly creating a risk of it.
  • Unlawful assembly, § 240.10, is a class B misdemeanor. It applies to a person who assembles with four or more others "for the purpose of engaging or preparing to engage with them in tumultuous and violent conduct likely to cause public alarm."
  • Obstructing governmental administration in the second degree, § 195.05, is a class A misdemeanor. It covers intentionally obstructing "the administration of law" or preventing a public servant from performing an official function, "by means of intimidation, physical force or interference" or "by means of any independently unlawful act."

After the arrest

All three charges are below the felony level. For an offense other than a class A, B, C or D felony, Criminal Procedure Law § 150.20 says the officer "shall" instead issue an appearance ticket, a ticket with a court date, unless one of eleven listed exceptions applies. They include an outstanding warrant, a failure to appear in court in the last two years, and an identity the officer cannot verify. That is the statute's default. How a particular arrest is handled on the street is a separate question; the DOI's report is the record for 2020.

If a felony is charged instead, different rules apply, including the 120-hour limit in CPL 180.80, which we explain in a separate article.

What happens to the record

A conviction for a violation falls under CPL § 160.55. That section says "all official records and papers relating to the arrest or prosecution" shall be "sealed and not made available to any person or public or private agency." There are listed exceptions: the person, a prosecutor on certain motions, a law enforcement agency on an ex parte motion, a licensing officer reviewing a gun-license application, and state corrections or probation for people under supervision. On the district attorney's motion, or on its own, the court can also find that "the interests of justice require otherwise."

A misdemeanor conviction is outside that section, which covers traffic infractions and violations. It follows the separate sealing rules we cover in our Clean Slate and background check articles.

Ask when you get the ticket or at arraignment: "What is the top charge, and is it a violation or a misdemeanor?" Disorderly conduct is a violation. Unlawful assembly and obstructing governmental administration are misdemeanors. If the answer is only "it's a protest charge," that is not an answer. Ask for the section number, then ask your attorney what a plea to it would do to the record.
