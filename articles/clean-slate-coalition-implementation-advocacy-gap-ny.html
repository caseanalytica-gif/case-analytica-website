The Coalition That Won Clean Slate Isn't Publicly Fighting to Finish It

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-04

It took four years and a broad, unusual coalition to pass New York's Clean Slate Act: directly impacted organizers, the Center for Community Alternatives, the Legal Aid Society, Legal Action Center, Brooklyn Defender Services, Community Service Society, JPMorganChase, the Business Council of New York State, labor unions, and eventually four city district attorneys. That's according to a case study the Brennan Center for Justice published on October 30, 2025, based on interviews with roughly a dozen people who ran the campaign. The coalition won. Governor Hochul signed the bill in November 2023.

Almost three years later, the law still isn't doing what it was built to do for most of the people who qualify. New York gave itself until November 16, 2027, three years past the law's own effective date, to actually build the systems that seal a record automatically. As of the most recent public reporting on the subject, a March 2025 state budget hearing covered by Spectrum News, the Division of Criminal Justice Services said the Office of Court Administration had not yet sent it a single record to seal.

The same coalition that spent four years turning public pressure into a signed bill is exactly who has the standing, and the relationships in Albany, to turn that same pressure toward the 2027 deadline now. Based on what these organizations are telling the public right now, that pressure hasn't shown up yet as a named, headline priority, and this piece is less an accusation than an appeal. The people who got Clean Slate passed are the same people who can get it finished on time, or ahead of time. What's asked of them here is simple: stay as loud about the deadline as you were about the bill. Because every year that pressure doesn't show up is another year a qualifying record stays unsealed, another year someone gets turned down for a job or an apartment they were legally entitled to have a shot at, and the research this site keeps coming back to is clear about where that leads: blocked employment and blocked housing are what drive people back into the system, not away from it. That's the stake if implementation quietly slips past 2027, not just a missed date on a calendar.

What the coalition actually built

The Center for Community Alternatives, a Syracuse-founded reentry and advocacy organization, organized the Clean Slate NY coalition's steering committee alongside Community Service Society, the Bronx Defenders, Legal Action Center, and the Legal Aid Society. Its own Clean Slate campaign page describes it as a four-year fight that won "the nation's most expansive legislation to seal old conviction records." The Brennan Center's case study adds detail the campaign's public materials don't: business leaders joined out of concern about a shrinking pandemic-era workforce, organized labor brought in members with their own records, and even law enforcement, including then-Bronx DA Darcel Clark and NYPD Chief of Department Rodney Harrison, signaled early conditional support in exchange for keeping law enforcement access to sealed records intact. The compromise that resulted, a longer felony wait and a wider exclusion list than the coalition's own preferred version, is documented in Case Analytica's earlier reporting on exactly what changed before the bill passed. This piece isn't about that negotiation. It's about what happened after the coalition won.

Where the coalition's public energy is going now

Look at what these same organizations are telling the public they're working on in 2026, and Clean Slate implementation barely appears.

The Center for Community Alternatives' own campaign page, checked for this article, lists its most recent public actions as pushing for two entirely different bills: the Earned Time Act and the Second Look Act, both aimed at reducing prison sentences rather than speeding up record sealing. CCA's own June 2025 press statements framed it as "keep fighting" language aimed at lawmakers who left session without passing those two bills, not at accelerating Clean Slate's rollout.

The Fortune Society tells the same story. Its 2023 legislative priorities named Clean Slate NY specifically as a bill still moving through Albany that the organization was actively pushing to pass. Its 2026 legislative and policy priorities, published January 21, 2026, run long: closing Rikers Island, protecting Raise the Age, the Youth Justice Innovation Fund, the Reentry from the Inside Out bills, the Transitional Reentry Health Act, a $425-a-month reentry stipend bill, the Second Look and Earned Time Acts, Fair and Timely Parole, and Elder Parole. Clean Slate doesn't appear anywhere on that list, not as a bill to pass and not as an implementation timeline to watch.

What this doesn't prove

A bill missing from a public priorities list isn't proof that no one is working on it. Organizations do plenty of implementation-focused work, technical meetings with agency staff, data-sharing conversations, casework, that never shows up in a press release. What's fair to say is narrower: as of the most recent public statements these organizations have put their own names on, none of the groups that led the fight to pass Clean Slate are publicly campaigning, the way they did before 2023, to make sure the state hits or beats its own 2027 sealing deadline.

Meanwhile, the state's own account of its progress has gone quiet too

New York's Chief Administrative Judge, Joseph Zayas, delivers detailed public testimony to the Legislature every year about what the Unified Court System has accomplished and where it's headed. His February 12, 2026 testimony to the Joint Legislative Hearing on the FY 2027 Judiciary Budget runs several pages: nonjudicial staffing levels finally returning to their 2009 peak, a 20 percent increase in Family Court staffing, a 25 percent drop in pending Family Court matters in New York City, a 10 percent citywide rise in felony case dispositions, and a detailed e-filing rollout. Clean Slate, the single largest records project the court system is currently required to build, isn't mentioned once. A year earlier, at the March 2025 hearing, Zayas had told legislators OCA had what it needed to implement the law over the following two years and eight months, according to Spectrum News' report on that testimony. Whether that's still on track wasn't part of what he chose to report on a year later.

See Case Analytica's original Clean Slate Act explainer for the waiting periods and exclusion list, and Does a Conviction Actually Show Up on a Background Check in New York? for what that means on an actual job application right now.

Why the silence matters more than the compromise did

This site has said before that the negotiated compromise in Clean Slate's text, the longer felony wait, the wider exclusion list, cost real people real years. That's still true. But there's a second cost stacking on top of it, and it's less visible precisely because nobody's making noise about it. A bill that passed with business leaders, labor unions, and four district attorneys standing behind it had enormous public pressure behind it in 2023. That pressure is what got the waiting periods written into a specific bill and got a governor to sign it in front of cameras. Implementation, the unglamorous work of actually building the system that seals a record, doesn't have anyone applying that same pressure right now, at least not in public.

That matters because of what's actually at stake for the people waiting. Every year a qualifying record sits unsealed is a year that record can still surface on a background check and cost someone a job or a lease. The research behind this site's throughline hasn't changed: stable employment and stable housing after release are what actually keep someone from cycling back into the system, and a sealing law that takes years to execute delays exactly that stability during the window when it matters most. A coalition that fought hard enough to win a first-of-its-kind sealing law is capable of fighting just as hard to make sure the state doesn't quietly let the 2027 deadline become 2028. Right now, based on what these organizations are telling the public, that fight hasn't started yet.

If your record should already qualify

Don't wait passively for the 2027 deadline. Check your status through the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services record review process, and see whether a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities or the older, motion-based sealing route under CPL 160.59 can remove the same barriers without waiting on the automatic system to catch up.

If Clean Slate's implementation is going to move faster than the state's own worst-case timeline, it will likely take the same thing that got the bill passed in the first place: organizations willing to make it a loud, public, named priority again, not just a line item in a technical meeting. Until one of them does, the honest thing to tell someone waiting on a record is that 2027 is the deadline, not a guarantee, and nobody with the standing to speed it up is currently saying they're trying to.
