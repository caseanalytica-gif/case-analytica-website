Closing Rikers Island: How Root-Cause Prevention and Community Reinvestment Create Real Safety

The Case for Closing New York's Most Notorious Jail

For decades, Rikers Island has been synonymous with the failures of American carceral justice. The sprawling jail complex, which houses over 10,000 people at capacity across multiple facilities, has become a symbol of systemic dysfunction—rife with violence, inadequate medical care, and a revolving door that cycles people back into the system rather than offering genuine pathways to safety.

The good news: New York is moving to close it. And the better news is that this shift represents a fundamental reimagining of what public safety actually means.

Real Safety Starts with Root-Cause Prevention

Public safety doesn't come from bigger jails or longer sentences. It comes from understanding why people enter the justice system in the first place—and addressing those underlying conditions.

The evidence is clear: most people cycling through criminal legal systems are experiencing poverty, untreated mental illness, substance use disorders, housing instability, or some combination of these. Rikers Island doesn't treat these conditions. It warehouses people experiencing them, often making their situations worse.

Research from the Vera Institute of Justice and The Sentencing Project shows that supportive housing reduces recidivism by up to 50%. When people have stable housing, access to mental health treatment, and therapeutic support, rearrest rates plummet. A person with a home, medication management, and peer support is far less likely to reoffend than a person released from jail with a criminal record and no resources.

New York's plan to close Rikers is built on this evidence. By expanding:

  • Supportive housing with wraparound services
  • Community-based mental health resources
  • Therapeutic programs addressing substance use and trauma
  • Job training and placement initiatives

...the city shifts from punishment to actual rehabilitation. This isn't soft-on-crime sentimentality. It's mathematics. Prevention is cheaper and more effective than incarceration.

The Community Reinvestment Piece

Here's where the economic argument gets powerful. Rikers Island sits on 415 acres of valuable waterfront real estate in the East River. Currently, those acres house a jail that costs roughly $500,000 per person per year to operate—among the most expensive incarceration systems in the world.

When New York closes Rikers, it has an opportunity to repurpose that land entirely. The plan includes:

  • Green energy infrastructure: solar farms, renewable energy facilities that could power parts of the city while creating union jobs
  • Community gardens and food production: addressing food insecurity in neighboring communities
  • Parks and public space: expanding recreational access in neighborhoods that have historically lacked investment
  • Affordable housing: directly addressing the housing crisis that feeds into the criminal legal system

This isn't abstract symbolism. Every dollar not spent on Rikers is a dollar available for communities.

Consider: the city spends roughly $14 billion annually on the entire jail system. If even a fraction of that shifts to housing, mental health, and community infrastructure in neighborhoods most impacted by over-policing and mass incarceration, the compounding effects are massive. A person with stable housing and mental health support doesn't end up in jail. Their children don't grow up with a parent in custody. Schools and communities stabilize.

The Data: Prevention Works Better Than Punishment

The numbers back this up. According to the Council of Criminal Justice, jurisdictions that have reduced incarceration while investing in community services have seen:

  • Stable or declining crime rates (contrary to "tough on crime" predictions)
  • Lower recidivism when people receive supportive services versus jail time
  • Better mental health outcomes for people with mental illness
  • Improved employment and housing stability after release

New York City's own data supports this. Neighborhoods that have received increased investment in mental health services and housing programs have seen decreases in both crime and incarceration—simultaneously.

What Closing Rikers Actually Means

This isn't about being "soft on crime." It's about being smart on crime.

  • For public safety: Addressing root causes prevents crime more effectively than jail
  • For taxpayers: Prevention is far cheaper than incarceration
  • For communities: Reinvesting jail budgets into housing, health, and jobs directly improves neighborhoods
  • For people caught in the system: A pathway to stability beats a record and no resources

Rikers Island closing represents a choice: Do we believe people are irredeemable, or do we believe that when we address the conditions that drive criminalized behavior, people can stabilize and contribute?

The evidence says the latter. New York is betting on it. And for the first time in decades, public safety policy is following the science instead of fear.
