Root-Cause Prevention Is the Part of Closing Rikers Nobody Budgets For

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-08-20

The case for closing Rikers that gets made in public has three legs to it: the jails are old and violent, the land is worth more to the city as something else, and the money would do more good spent on the housing and mental health care that keep people out of the system in the first place. That third one is the one worth slowing down on, because it is the only one of the three that has to be true before the jail closes rather than after. And it is the one with the least money attached to it.

Start with what the city already spends

New York City spent $507,000 per incarcerated person in 2023, or roughly $1,389 a day, per the Comptroller's office. As of July 2025 the Department of Correction was holding 7,602 people, which puts the annual cost of running the city's jails at close to $3.85 billion. Eighty-seven percent of that population, as of the most recent breakdown available, had not been convicted of anything.

That last number is what makes the prevention argument more than a slogan. The majority of people at Rikers on any given day are not serving a sentence. They are waiting. Which means the question of what would keep them out of a cell is not a question about punishment at all. It is a question about what happens in the weeks between an arraignment and a disposition.

What the evidence actually supports

There is real research behind the claim that people come back to court without being held, and it is worth being precise about what it says, because the precise version is more useful than the broad one.

Court date reminders. A randomized trial covering 5,709 public defender clients, published in Science Advances and covered by NYU Steinhardt, found that reminders sent seven, three, and one day before a court date cut bench warrants by about 20 percent and pretrial jail stays by about 21 percent. One of the researchers put the cost at a few cents per case.

Supervised release. New York City already runs a program of court-mandated check-ins with a case manager instead of cash bail. An MDRC evaluation found it reduces the use of cash bail and pretrial detention while maintaining court appearance rates.

Why people miss court at all. A Pew Charitable Trusts brief found most states still underuse the cheapest fix available, a text message, in favor of tools built to punish a failure that a two-cent reminder often prevents. Most missed court dates are logistics, not defiance: a forgotten date, an unclear notice, a job that will not survive another day off.

Notice what is and is not in that list. The evidence that people return to court without being jailed is strong and specific. The evidence that a given housing or treatment program produces a given reduction in rearrest is a different literature, with different numbers for different populations and program designs, and it does not reduce to a single percentage. If you see a clean headline figure for what supportive housing does to recidivism, ask which study, which population, and over what window before repeating it.

The part that has to come first

The city's legal commitment to close Rikers was set in 2019, with an original deadline of August 2027, and that deadline has already slipped repeatedly. The four borough-based jails meant to replace it are projected to finish between 2029 and 2032, and even when they do, they are designed for a population smaller than the one the city is currently holding.

That gap is the whole argument. If the replacement jails have fewer beds than the current population, then the alternatives to holding someone are not a nice-to-have that runs alongside the closure plan. They are the mechanism the closure plan depends on. Every person who is at Rikers because a reminder was never sent, or because supervised release was never offered, or because a treatment slot did not exist, is a person the borough jails have to have a bed for.

Which is why "reinvest the money in communities" is a real argument and also an incomplete one. The reinvestment has to happen on a schedule that runs ahead of the closure, not after it. Land repurposing, green energy on the island, community projects on the site: those are downstream of a jail that has already closed. The prevention spending is upstream of it.

New York City took a step toward funding that upstream side directly in 2026: see how Mayor Mamdani's Office of Community Safety is coordinating community-based violence prevention, and what real evidence exists that the model behind it actually works.

What to actually ask

If this is a live question for someone in your family right now rather than a policy debate, the useful version is narrower. Detention itself changes what happens next in a case, including how much leverage a prosecutor has over a plea. So the questions worth asking a defense attorney are concrete ones: has supervised release been requested, has a treatment-based alternative been raised, and if bail was set at an amount the family could pay, is a bail source hearing the reason they are still inside.

Those are answerable in a specific case. "Close Rikers" is not.

Where these numbers come from

Cost and population figures are from the NYC Comptroller's Office and Department of Correction reporting as cited in our earlier coverage, current as of the dates noted. Research findings are from NYU Steinhardt, MDRC, and the Pew Charitable Trusts as linked above. Population, cost, and construction-timeline figures change; confirm current numbers before citing them elsewhere.
