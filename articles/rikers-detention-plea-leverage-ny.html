How Holding Someone at Rikers Becomes Leverage for a Plea

Closing Rikers Island gets argued as a humanitarian question, and a fiscal one: half a million dollars a person, per year, for a jail that's held people in conditions that killed fifteen of them last year alone. Both of those arguments are correct and covered elsewhere. There's a third dimension that gets discussed less, because it doesn't show up in a budget line: pretrial detention is also a tool, whether or not any individual prosecutor thinks of it that way, that makes it more likely someone pleads guilty regardless of what actually happened. If the population held at Rikers comes down, that specific piece of leverage comes down with it. It's worth understanding exactly how it works before assuming what replaces it.

What the research actually shows

Two separate bodies of evidence point the same direction. A Stanford Law Review study of misdemeanor cases in Harris County, Texas found that defendants held in pretrial detention were 25 percent more likely to plead guilty than similarly situated defendants who were released, with the same facts and the same charge. The only variable was custody status. Separately, a review of the plea-bargaining research collected in the Vera Institute of Justice's "In the Shadows" report found pretrial detention increases the likelihood someone pleads guilty by as much as 46 percent. The exact number moves depending on the jurisdiction and the study design, but the direction never does: sitting in a cell before trial makes a person more likely to plead, independent of guilt.

This sits on top of a system where pleading guilty is already the default outcome, not the exception. Nationally, 97 percent of felony convictions in large urban courts come from a guilty plea, not a trial, per the Bureau of Justice Statistics. Detention doesn't create that dynamic from scratch. It amplifies it, for the specific group of people who can't post bail or aren't bail-eligible at all.

This doesn't require anyone to be acting in bad faith

None of this needs a corrupt prosecutor or a coerced confession in the classic sense. It requires a system where every day someone spends at Rikers, at $1,389 a day to the city and an unmeasurable cost to the person inside, makes "time served, go home today" look like the rational choice compared to waiting months for a trial date, regardless of whether the underlying case is strong. The pressure is structural. It doesn't need a villain to work.

How the leverage actually plays out

The mechanism is straightforward once it's named. A person held pretrial faces every cost of incarceration in real time, lost income, an apartment that may not survive a missed month of rent, a job that can't hold the position open, a child care arrangement that collapses, on top of the physical risk of being inside a jail the city's own comptroller and the Vera Institute have documented as dangerous. An offer to plead to a lesser charge, or to time served, ends all of that immediately. A decision to fight the case at trial means staying inside for months longer, with an uncertain outcome at the end of it. That comparison isn't about the strength of the evidence. It's about which option makes the pain stop sooner, and pretrial detention is what makes that comparison exist at all. Someone facing the identical charge from outside a cell is evaluating the case on its merits in a way someone facing it from inside Rikers structurally cannot.

What happens to that leverage if the jail population shrinks

This is the part worth thinking through honestly, rather than assuming closing Rikers automatically fixes the underlying dynamic. If fewer people are held pretrial, because of supervised release, GPS monitoring, text-message court reminders, or simply because the city runs out of beds before it runs out of people to charge, then fewer people face this specific form of pressure. That's a real, measurable improvement, and it's consistent with what the research above predicts: take away the detention, and the guilty-plea rate for that group should, in theory, move closer to what it would be if the case were actually decided on the facts.

But the leverage doesn't have only one source. A prosecutor's charging decision, whether the same conduct gets filed as a single count or five, as a felony or a misdemeanor, happens before bail is ever set, and it's the tool already doing most of the work in jurisdictions with lower detention rates than New York. If the population at Rikers drops substantially, it's reasonable to expect more of the pressure that currently comes from custody status to shift toward charge severity and toward whatever supervision conditions replace jail, restrictive GPS monitoring, curfews, frequent check-ins, which carry their own weight even without a cell attached to them. The research on supervised release specifically found it does not increase new arrests and does not reduce court appearance rates, which is a meaningfully different profile from detention. That's the case for building the alternatives well, not just building them cheaply.

What this means in practice

Closing Rikers removes a particularly severe, well-documented form of leverage. It does not, by itself, remove the incentive structure that makes plea bargaining the default resolution for the overwhelming majority of cases. Anyone facing a decision about a plea, whether they're sitting in Rikers or checking in with a supervised release case manager instead, should still ask the same question: does this offer look the same if I evaluate the case on its facts, separate from how badly I want out of my current situation?
