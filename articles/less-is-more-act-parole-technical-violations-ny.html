New York Led the Nation in Reincarcerating People for Breaking Parole Rules. What Less Is More Actually Changed

In 2018, New York sent more than 5,780 people back to prison for a technical parole violation, not a new crime, 66 percent of everyone the state reincarcerated off parole that year. A missed appointment with a parole officer, a late curfew, a positive drug test: none of those are crimes on their own, but under the old system they could end a person's freedom anyway. The #LessIsMoreNY campaign, run by the Katal Center and Unchained, put it plainly in its own organizing materials: New York imprisoned more people for non-criminal technical parole violations than any other state in the country. This article looks at the pattern behind that, what New York actually changed in 2021, and what the evidence since shows about whether it worked.

This is a different question than the mechanics of a parole violation hearing itself. If you want the statute-by-statute walkthrough of how a violation gets charged and heard today, see Case Analytica's explainer on Executive Law § 259-i. This piece is about the pattern behind those hearings: how often they used to end in reincarceration, why, and what changed.

What "technical violation" actually covers

A technical violation is a rule violation, not a new arrest. Missing a curfew, failing to report an address change, testing positive for alcohol or drugs, or fraternizing with someone who has a record can all count. Before reform, New York treated many of these the same way it treated a new crime: as grounds for reincarceration, often with no cap on how long someone could be held while the case worked through the system. The 5,780 figure and the 66 percent share cited above come from a 2020 Bureau of Justice Statistics report, via the Katal Center and Unchained's #LessIsMoreNY campaign. A separate 2019 report from the Council of State Governments Justice Center, cited by the same campaign, found that nearly a third of all new admissions to New York's state prisons in 2017 were people being reincarcerated for a technical violation, not sentenced for a new offense.

The racial pattern was stark. A 2020 Columbia University Justice Lab report found that in 2019, Black people were incarcerated in New York City jails for technical parole violations at more than twelve times the rate of white people.

The scale of it

At any given time, roughly 35,000 people are under active parole supervision in New York State. Under the old system, nearly any one of them could see a job, an apartment, and a family reunification disrupted by reincarceration over a curfew violation, with no new criminal charge involved at all.

What the Less Is More Act actually changed

Governor Hochul signed the Less Is More: Community Supervision Revocation Reform Act on September 17, 2021, with most provisions effective March 1, 2022. The law did four specific things: it eliminated incarceration as an automatic response to most technical violations, capping remaining jail exposure at 30 days for the most serious repeat cases; it replaced automatic detention with a written notice to appear in court for most violations; it required a speedy hearing, generally within 30 days instead of up to 105; and it created earned-time credits that shorten a person's supervision period for every 30 days without a violation. Today's actual reincarceration schedule for most technical violations, as published in DOCCS's own Community Supervision Handbook, imposes no reincarceration at all for a first or second substantiated violation, capping out at 30 days only after a fifth.

Did it work?

Partially, and the state's own numbers show the limits of that partial success. A report from the Columbia Justice Lab Probation and Parole Project, covered by City & State in November 2023, found that 1,300 fewer people were held in state custody for a technical violation in 2022 than the year before. Separately, the #LessIsMoreNY campaign reported nearly 2,000 people held solely on technical violations were released in the law's first ten months. Those are real reductions.

But two years after the law took effect, the state still could not say how much money, if any, it had actually saved. A DOCCS spokesperson told City & State the reform added new costs of its own in the short term, including additional staff to handle the new recognizance hearings and the higher evidentiary bar at revocation hearings. New York's own 2021 fiscal estimate for the bill had put the annual cost of incarcerating people for technical violations at roughly $680 million before the reform. As of that November 2023 reporting, no state agency had confirmed how much of that figure had actually been recovered.

Where the reduction goes matters as much as the number

The Columbia Justice Lab report that documented the 1,300-person reduction also surveyed 231 New Yorkers across five communities with historically high reincarceration rates for parole violations, asking how they'd want any savings from the reform spent. Affordable housing topped the list, followed by behavioral health care and employment support. Community members who lived through the old system, in other words, identified the exact two levers, housing and employment, that the research says actually keep people from cycling back through it. See Case Analytica's companion piece on the Clean Slate Act's implementation gap for how that same employment-and-housing throughline plays out on the record-sealing side of the system.

What this means if someone you love is on parole right now

The reform changed the default, but it didn't eliminate the underlying tension. A technical violation can still result in reincarceration under the right circumstances, particularly for repeat violations or where a hearing officer finds the conduct closer to a new crime than a rule slip. If a loved one is facing a violation, the exact legal standard that applies, and the deadlines that protect them, are covered in full in Case Analytica's explainer on Executive Law § 259-i. And if the underlying goal is staying off parole's radar entirely going forward, how to actually prepare for a New York parole hearing covers what the Board evaluates at the release stage, before supervision even begins.

The numbers tell a specific story: New York built a system that, for years, treated a missed appointment the same as a new crime, at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars a year and a wildly disproportionate impact on Black New Yorkers. Less Is More narrowed that. It didn't close it, and the state still can't tell you exactly what the old system cost versus what the new one saves. Until it can, the honest answer is that reincarceration for a rule violation, not a new offense, is still a live risk for the roughly 35,000 people on parole in this state today.
