The Bill Fixing Clean Slate's Sealing Bug Has Sat in Committee Since March 2025

A New York State Senator introduced a bill on March 14, 2025, to fix a drafting problem in the Clean Slate Act. It's still sitting in the Senate Codes Committee. Its Assembly companion wasn't even introduced until April 14, 2026, more than a year later, and it hasn't moved either. Neither bill has gotten a floor vote. Meanwhile, the deadline for New York to finish automatically sealing millions of eligible records, November 16, 2027, keeps getting closer.

Case Analytica has already reported on two other gaps in how Clean Slate is actually being implemented: the waiting periods and exclusions negotiated into the bill before it passed, and the state agencies telling lawmakers in 2025 they didn't need extra money or staff to build the sealing system. This is a third gap, and it's different from the other two. It isn't about how long someone has to wait or how the program is funded. It's about a specific piece of the law's own language that the court system itself says it cannot execute as written, for a category of cases that shows up constantly in real dockets.

What "multicategory conviction docket" actually means

Most people who get arrested and convicted of something in New York don't walk away with exactly one, clean, standalone conviction. A single arrest often produces a single case, one docket or accusatory instrument, that carries more than one count. Someone might be convicted of a top charge and a lesser included charge from the same incident. Someone else might plead to one count while a second count on the same docket gets resolved a different way. New York's court system refers to a docket carrying more than one conviction as a "multicategory conviction docket."

The Clean Slate Act, codified at CPL 160.57, was written to seal records on a conviction-by-conviction basis, sometimes called offense-specific sealing. Each individual conviction gets its own waiting period, three years from release or sentencing for a misdemeanor, eight years for a felony, and its own eligibility check. That sounds precise on paper. In practice, according to the sponsor memo for the bill meant to fix it, the state's Unified Court System, through its Division of Technology and Court Research, says that exact offense-specific structure is what's preventing the automated sealing system from being able to process multicategory dockets at all. The system can't cleanly seal one conviction on a docket while leaving another unresolved, and the current statute doesn't tell it how to handle that.

In plain terms

If your case had more than one conviction attached to the same docket, the sealing system New York is building right now may not know what to do with your record, even after every individual conviction on it becomes eligible. That's not a hypothetical edge case. Multi-count cases are common.

What the fix bill actually does

State Senator Zellnor Myrie's Senate Bill S6492, and Assembly Member Cruz's companion measure, A10951, would add new conditions to CPL 160.57. Instead of only checking whether an individual conviction has cleared its own waiting period, the amended law would also require that any infractions or noncriminal violations on a multicategory docket wait until every other criminal conviction on that same docket is independently eligible for sealing, and that those other convictions are eligible too, before any of it seals. It's a coordination rule: get every conviction on the docket lined up, then seal the docket together.

The sponsor memo is direct about why this matters right now, not eventually: "Due to the technological infrastructure necessary to meet the State's goal of automatically sealing all eligible convictions by November 16, 2027, certain implementation issues have arisen due to the use of the term, 'conviction'... these amendments will ensure that these hindrances are bypassed allowing for the timely completion of the statutory mandate." That's the Legislature's own sponsor, in the Legislature's own bill file, saying the current law's language is a bottleneck against its own deadline.

A year and counting

S6492 was introduced March 14, 2025. Its most recent action, as of this writing, was being referred back to the Codes Committee on January 7, 2026, nearly ten months after it was first filed. A10951, the Assembly companion, wasn't introduced until April 14, 2026, over a year after the Senate version. Neither bill has a floor vote scheduled. Both are, technically, still active for the 2025-2026 legislative session. Neither has moved.

Compare that timeline to the deadline it's supposed to protect. The Unified Court System has until November 16, 2027, roughly fourteen months from now, to finish building the system that automatically seals every eligible record in the state. If the agencies building that system are right that the current statutory language is a genuine technical obstacle, then every month S6492 and A10951 sit in committee is a month the deadline gets closer without the fix that's supposed to make hitting it possible for multicategory cases.

Why this is a jobs and housing question, not a drafting footnote

New York City's own Comptroller's office estimated over half a million NYC residents would become eligible to have a record automatically sealed under Clean Slate, and put the potential wage gain at $2.4 billion a year once people with sealed records could compete for jobs that currently screen them out. Nearly 80 percent of that eligible population is Black or Latinx.

That's the number that makes a technical drafting bug worth writing about. A person whose case involved more than one count on the same docket, which describes a large share of New York's actual conviction records, isn't just waiting on the calendar to hit their eligibility date anymore. They're waiting on a coordination rule that doesn't exist in the statute yet, one the state's own court technology division says it needs before the system can process their record automatically at all. The Council of State Governments Justice Center's national recidivism analysis ties the drop in three-year reincarceration rates directly to expanded post-release employment and housing support. Every month a qualifying record stays unsealed because of a fixable statutory gap is a month that person is still carrying the exact risk factor the research says makes someone more likely to end up back in the system, not less.

If your case had more than one count

Don't assume a multi-count docket seals the same way a single conviction does. Check your record's actual status through the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services record review process rather than relying on when the waiting period alone should have expired. See Case Analytica's full breakdown of Clean Slate's waiting periods and exclusions for the underlying eligibility rules.

Nobody in this story is accused of acting in bad faith. A large piece of legislation like Clean Slate almost always needs a technical corrections bill behind it; that's normal. What isn't normal, or at least shouldn't be treated as routine, is a fix the court system itself flagged as necessary sitting in committee for over a year while the clock it's supposed to protect keeps running. If S6492 and A10951 are still sitting in committee a year from now, the November 2027 deadline will have arrived with the underlying bug still unfixed. That's worth watching, and worth asking your own state senator and assembly member about directly.
