Do You Have the Right to Testify Before the Grand Jury in New York? CPL 190.50 Explained

Most people learn what an indictment is at the exact moment it lands on them, and by then they've already missed the one window where they could have gotten in front of the grand jury themselves. Nobody explains that window in advance. It closes the moment an indictment is filed, and unlike most of what happens after arraignment, this piece is not something your attorney can revive once it's gone.

This right is not in the Constitution. It's in the statute.

New York is one of the few states that lets a defendant appear as a witness in their own grand jury proceeding at all. That right does not come from the U.S. Constitution or the New York State Constitution. It exists because the legislature wrote it into Criminal Procedure Law § 190.50, and subdivision 1 of that same section makes clear that outside the circumstances it lists, "no person has a right to call a witness or appear as a witness in a grand jury proceeding" at all. This is a legislative choice, not a constitutional guarantee, which means it works exactly the way the statute says and no further.

How the right actually gets triggered

Under CPL 190.50(5)(a), once you've been arraigned in a local criminal court on a felony complaint charging an offense that a grand jury is about to consider, the district attorney is required to notify you or your attorney of the proceeding and give you "a reasonable time" to exercise the right to appear. Before that arraignment, the DA has no obligation to tell you a grand jury is even meeting. The way you actually invoke the right is by serving the district attorney with written notice, before any indictment is filed, requesting to appear and stating an address for communications. Miss that window and the opportunity is gone, indictment or not.

The trade nobody explains up front

To actually testify, CPL 190.50(5)(b) requires you to first sign a waiver of immunity under CPL 190.45. Grand jury witnesses ordinarily testify under transactional immunity, meaning what they say can't later be used to prosecute them. Waiving that immunity is the price of admission for a defendant who wants to testify in their own case, and it means your own words in that room can be used against you afterward. That is not a decision to make without understanding exactly what you're giving up, and it is exactly the kind of fork in the road covered in the bet every plea deal is making: a choice that looks simple on the surface and isn't.

What happens if the DA gets this wrong

CPL 190.50(5)(c) makes an indictment obtained in violation of the notice requirement invalid, and it can be dismissed on motion. But that motion has to be made within five days of your arraignment on the indictment itself, or the objection is waived permanently. Five days. Not five business days, not five weeks. This deadline runs whether or not you or your attorney ever realized the notice requirement had been violated in the first place, which is exactly why the pace of a case in its earliest days matters as much as it does in how New York's speedy trial clock under CPL 30.30 actually runs: both statutes reward whoever is paying close attention in the first days after arraignment, and punish whoever isn't.

There's a second, more limited option too: under subdivision 6, a defendant can ask the grand jury, orally or in writing, to call a specific witness on their behalf. That one is not a right. It's a request the grand jury can grant or refuse entirely at its own discretion.

The exact question to ask, and when to ask it

The moment you are arraigned on a felony complaint: "Has the DA filed notice of an intent to present this case to a grand jury, and have we served written notice that I want to testify?" Ask it that day, not the week after. And before signing anything, ask what specifically you're waiving under CPL 190.45 by testifying, and what your attorney thinks that testimony realistically buys you.

The overwhelming majority of felony defendants never end up testifying, in part because the tactical case against it is often real; a defendant can be cross-examined by the same prosecutor building the case against them, in a room where their own lawyer cannot object or speak. But that judgment call should be made deliberately, by you and your attorney, inside the window the statute actually gives you, not defaulted into by nobody raising it in time. That default is exactly the failure covered in why a public defender carrying 100-plus cases might never get to this conversation at all: it isn't that anyone decided this right didn't matter. It's that in a system built to settle, it's the first thing to fall through the cracks.
