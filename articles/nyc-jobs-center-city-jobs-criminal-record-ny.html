The Line for City Jobs Went Down the Block. What Chance Does Someone With a Record Have?

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-06

Reflects law as of October 6, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

On Monday, October 5, New York City's new Jobs NYC Center at 2 Lafayette Street opened its doors to walk-ins. The city had 14,391 vacant positions across its agencies as of August 31, according to amNY. People came. Videos posted to TikTok that day showed a line stretching down the block under the scaffolding. One creator, @Erikfnyc, put it at about 2,500 people. A person who waited posted that staff started turning people away after noon. We found no news coverage of the line as of October 6. Case Analytica has asked the city for its own count of how many people came, how many were seen, and whether anyone was hired.

A line like that is a picture of how many people are looking for work. The city's own economic development agency reported in September that private employers in New York City had added just 3,900 jobs so far in 2026, and that outside health care and social assistance, private employment was down 12,900 jobs. It called the market "cooling" with "relatively little churn." Against that, city government was carrying more than 14,000 open positions.

Now put someone with a criminal record in that same crowd. They are competing for the same jobs as everyone else, and nationally they start further behind: the Prison Policy Initiative found that formerly incarcerated people were unemployed at a rate of over 27 percent, nearly five times the general rate, in its 2018 analysis of 2008 data. For a city job, their record doesn't come up in line or on the application. It comes up after the offer, when the city runs their fingerprints. Here is what the city sees at that point, what it can hold against an applicant, and why Clean Slate hasn't changed that yet for most people.

What the Center does, and what it doesn't

The Mayor's Office announcement describes the Center as help finding and applying for public-sector jobs, including guidance on civil service exams, with a testing center upstairs in the same building. It also hosts agency hiring halls, where interviews and on-the-spot hiring happen on scheduled dates. amNY reported that walk-in hours run Monday through Thursday, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., with generally three staff members available.

So a walk-in visit gets someone help with an application. The post shared from the line said the same thing in plainer words: staff weren't taking résumés or hiring on the spot, and were helping people apply on the city's jobs website. That matters for anyone with a record, because nothing about a criminal history comes up at the application stage for most city titles. It comes up at the end.

Every new city hire is fingerprinted

The Department of Citywide Administrative Services, which acts as the city's civil service commission, requires everyone newly appointed by an agency under its jurisdiction to submit fingerprints. The new hire pays the fee. DCAS sends the prints to the state Division of Criminal Justice Services and, through it, to the FBI, and gets back a report of the person's arrests and prosecutions. The DCAS fingerprinting FAQ puts the total cost for a civilian title at $104.50.

The authority for that check comes from Civil Service Law § 50(4). It lets a civil service commission refuse to certify someone who "has been guilty of a crime," and says the commission "may require" a state and national criminal history check. The same subdivision says no one can be disqualified under it without a written statement of the reasons and a chance to explain and submit facts against it.

When the city can ask, and what it can hold against you

The city's own rules for this are in DCAS Personnel Services Bulletin 100-13. Here is what they say.

  • No questions before a conditional offer for most titles. That is the city's Fair Chance Act, and it applies to city agencies.
  • The exceptions ask up front. Police and peace officer jobs, positions at law enforcement agencies including NYPD, FDNY, Correction, Probation and the district attorneys' offices, and a list of "Investigation Before Appointment" titles that DCAS designates and publishes.
  • Only felonies and unsealed misdemeanors count. The agency can't consider arrests that didn't end in a conviction unless a statute requires or permits it. It can't consider youthful offender adjudications at all.
  • A conviction is not an automatic no. The agency has to apply Correction Law Article 23-A. It can pull an offer only if the conviction bears a direct relationship to the job or the hire would pose an unreasonable risk, after weighing eight factors that include how long ago the offense was, the person's age at the time, and any evidence of rehabilitation.
  • You get it in writing, and you get time. Before pulling an offer, the agency has to give the applicant a written copy of its Article 23-A analysis and allow time to respond, holding the job open in the meantime. How much time depends on which city document you read. DCAS's bulletin, last updated in December 2021, says not less than three days. The Commission on Human Rights, which enforces the Fair Chance Act, says at least five business days.

A Certificate of Relief from Disabilities or a Certificate of Good Conduct changes that analysis. Under Correction Law § 753(2), either one "shall create a presumption of rehabilitation" for the offenses it covers.

Where Clean Slate actually stands

Under CPL 160.57, a misdemeanor conviction becomes eligible to seal three years after sentencing or release from incarceration, and a felony after eight. Sex offenses and Class A felonies other than drug offenses never seal. Nothing seals while the person is on probation or parole for that conviction, or while a new charge is pending in New York.

Eligible and sealed are not the same thing yet. The law took effect November 16, 2024, nearly two years ago, and the court system's Clean Slate page says it has until November 16, 2027 to seal all eligible convictions. The form to ask for a manual review of a record that should have sealed won't exist until that same date. Case Analytica has asked DCJS for a current count of convictions sealed so far. We have not found a public one. We have reported on two things slowing it down: the agencies building the system told lawmakers they didn't need more money or staff, and a drafting bug the court system says it can't execute as written is still waiting on a fix bill stuck in committee.

Put that next to the city's rules. A misdemeanor that qualifies for sealing but hasn't been sealed yet is, on the day the fingerprints come back, an unsealed misdemeanor, which is exactly what Bulletin 100-13 lets an agency consider. For a job seeker with an older conviction, that means a record can qualify under Clean Slate and still count against them in October 2026.

The question nobody has answered in public

Sealing doesn't make a record invisible to everyone. CPL 160.57 keeps sealed convictions available to a list of exceptions, and three of them touch hiring:

  • Individuals or entities "required by" a local, state or federal law to request a fingerprint-based check.
  • Entities authorized to run a fingerprint check for work with children, older adults, people with disabilities or other vulnerable people.
  • Any prospective employer of a police officer or peace officer.

A second law decides what an employer can do with a sealed record once it has it. Executive Law § 296(16) makes it unlawful for any employer, including the state and any political subdivision, to ask about or act against someone because of a conviction sealed under CPL 160.57. The exceptions are records reached through the first two carve-outs above, the required check and the vulnerable-population check, or through a separate one for State Education Department professional licensing. Someone asked about a protected sealed conviction "may respond as if" it "did not occur." Police and peace officer hiring is outside that protection entirely.

Here is the gap. Civil Service Law § 50(4) says the city "may require" a criminal history check. DCAS made it a requirement for every new hire through its own bulletin. Whether that counts as a check "required by" law under CPL 160.57 decides two things for an ordinary civilian city job: whether a sealed conviction comes back with the fingerprints, and whether the city can lawfully use it. We could not answer that from the statutes or from DCAS's published rules. Neither bulletin mentions Clean Slate. On October 6 Case Analytica put the question to DCAS and to DCJS directly. We will update this article with what they say.

For a deeper look at how the sealing exceptions work, see Does a Conviction Actually Show Up on a Background Check in New York?

If you're applying with a record

  • Check the title first. If it's a law enforcement position or on the DCAS list of Investigation Before Appointment titles, expect the record question before any offer. Most other titles can't ask until after one.
  • Know your own record before the city does. You can request your New York rap sheet through the DCJS record review process. It shows the New York criminal history DCJS holds on you.
  • Build the rehabilitation file now. Article 23-A requires the agency to weigh any information you produce about your rehabilitation and good conduct, and a certificate carries a legal presumption of rehabilitation.
  • If an offer is pulled, use the response window. The agency owes you its written analysis. Answer it in writing, point by point, against the eight factors, and ask in writing how many days you have.
  • If you were asked about your record before an offer for a title that isn't an exception, that's a Fair Chance Act issue. The NYC Commission on Human Rights enforces it.

The exact question to ask

Ask the agency's HR office in writing as soon as you get a conditional offer: "Is this an Investigation Before Appointment title, and will the fingerprint report you receive for this position include convictions sealed under CPL 160.57?" Keep the answer. "We follow all applicable laws" doesn't answer either half of that question.
