New York City Landlords Can't Screen You Out for a Criminal Record First Anymore: The Fair Chance for Housing Act Explained

A conviction from eight years ago shouldn't be the reason an apartment application gets thrown out before anyone even looks at your income or your references. As of January 1, 2025, in most cases across New York City, it isn't allowed to be. Most tenants have never heard of the law that changed it, and most landlords are still figuring out how to comply with it.

What actually changed

New York City's Fair Chance for Housing Act, Local Law 24 of 2024, took effect January 1, 2025. It amended the New York City Human Rights Law, codified at Administrative Code § 8-107, to bar most housing providers, landlords, co-op and condo boards, brokers, agents, and property managers, from considering an applicant's criminal history before making a conditional offer. Source: NYC Commission on Human Rights.

Before this law, a landlord could reject an application over a decades-old conviction before ever reviewing income, references, or credit. In most cases, that's now illegal.

The exact order it has to happen in

A housing provider has to evaluate an applicant's general eligibility first, income, references, credit, and extend a conditional offer, before it can even start the process of looking into criminal history. Only after that conditional offer can a background check begin, and only after giving the applicant the Commission's official Fair Chance Housing Notice and disclosing that a check is coming. Source: Holland & Knight.

Even once a check is run, the law narrows what can actually be held against someone. Only what the law defines as "Reviewable Criminal History" can be considered: a registered sex offense conviction, with no time limit, a misdemeanor conviction within three years of release from incarceration or sentencing, or a felony conviction within five years of release or sentencing. Anything older than that window, even a serious conviction, legally cannot be used to deny the application.

What a landlord still can't do

Advertise or state that applicants with a criminal history are excluded. Ask about or require disclosure of criminal history before a conditional offer. Revoke an offer without following the full process below. Reject an applicant over any conviction that falls outside the Reviewable Criminal History window, no matter how serious it was. Source: NYC Commission on Human Rights.

If a background check turns something up

The applicant has to be given a written copy of everything the check found, even information the housing provider isn't relying on. From there, the applicant has five business days to respond with corrections or mitigating evidence before any final decision. The housing provider then has to conduct what the law calls an individualized assessment and, if it still rejects the applicant, put the reasoning in writing, specifically connecting the conviction to a legitimate business interest. A vague objection like "the residents don't want criminals in the building" is explicitly not enough on its own.

What happens if a landlord skips these steps

An applicant who believes a housing provider ignored this process can file a complaint with the NYC Commission on Human Rights, which enforces the law and can issue fines up to $250,000. An applicant can also pursue a separate civil action. This isn't a courtesy a landlord can decide to skip. It's a legal process with real consequences attached to it.

A record that's already been dealt with in the legal system doesn't have to keep costing someone new opportunities years later. If a conviction is old enough, a Clean Slate Act sealing or a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities can remove the barrier on the employment side, the way this law now limits it on the housing side. And before assuming a record will even show up the way someone expects, see what actually shows up on a background check in New York.

The exact question to ask

"Did you offer me this apartment or unit conditionally before you ran a background check, and if you're pulling that offer back now, can I see the written Fair Chance Housing Notice and the specific reason tied to your legitimate business interest?" If a housing provider can't produce that, the denial itself may be the violation, not the record it's pointing to.

A criminal record follows people into every room they try to walk into after the courtroom: the job interview, the apartment showing, the co-op board meeting. This is one door New York actually narrowed how far a landlord can slam it. Know the timeline the law requires. Ask for the notice. Don't let a rejection stand just because nobody told you the law changed.
