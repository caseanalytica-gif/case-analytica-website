What Is the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act in New York? Penal Law 60.12 Explained

A domestic violence survivor gets arrested for something that grew directly out of surviving the abuse that put them in that position in the first place. A prosecutor puts a plea deal on the table, and buried inside it is a condition: sign away the hearing that could cut the sentence down to a fraction of what's being threatened, or walk away from the deal entirely. In April 2026, New York's highest court said that trade is not legal. Not just because it's unfair to ask, though it is. Because the hearing was never a chip prosecutors were allowed to bargain with.

What the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act actually does

The Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act, passed in 2019, amended Penal Law § 60.12 to give judges a path around the standard sentencing scheme for a specific group of defendants: people convicted of an eligible offense whose criminal conduct was significantly shaped by domestic abuse. To get there, the statute requires a hearing, and at that hearing the defendant has to establish three things: that at the time of the offense they were a victim of domestic violence, subjected to substantial physical, sexual, or psychological abuse by a member of the same family or household as defined in CPL 530.11(1); that the abuse was a significant contributing factor to the criminal behavior; and that a standard sentence would be unduly harsh given the nature of the crime and the defendant's history and character.

If a judge finds all three, the reduction is not symbolic. For a class B felony that would otherwise carry a lengthy indeterminate term, the court can instead impose a determinate sentence of as little as one year and no more than five. For a class A felony that would otherwise require a minimum of fifteen to twenty-five years, the statute allows a determinate term as low as five years. Some categories allow probation or a sentence of one year or less with no state prison time at all. This is the difference between a decade or more inside and a sentence measured in months, decided entirely by whether the abuse and its effect on the case get heard.

This is not automatic

Nobody is required to raise a 60.12 hearing on a survivor's behalf. The defendant has to request it, the request has to be supported with real evidence of the abuse and its connection to the offense, and a judge still has to be persuaded. Like every other diversion or alternative-sentencing option, the system does not surface this. Someone has to ask.

The case that decided you can't sign this hearing away

In People v N.H., 2026 NY Slip Op 02437, decided April 23, 2026, New York's Court of Appeals took up exactly this question. N.H. had fled to her car during a confrontation involving her abusive ex-boyfriend, and while trying to escape, struck and permanently injured a bystander. She was charged with attempted murder in the second degree, facing a maximum exposure of twenty-five years. A psychologist who evaluated her found her account of years of physical and psychological abuse consistent with a diagnosis of PTSD, and both a forensic social worker and psychologist concluded a standard sentence would be unduly harsh given her history. N.H. requested a 60.12 hearing to make that case to a judge.

Instead, the prosecution offered a plea on one condition: waive the hearing. N.H. took the deal, pleaded guilty to first-degree reckless assault, and was sentenced to five years in prison followed by five years of post-release supervision, the exact outcome a 60.12 hearing exists to prevent for someone in her position. She appealed the waiver itself, not the plea. The Court of Appeals sided with her, holding that a 60.12 hearing is "too valuable, both to the survivor and to the community, to be sacrificed in plea bargaining," and that allowing prosecutors to condition a plea on giving it up would gut the statute for the overwhelming majority of survivors, since almost everyone in the system pleads rather than goes to trial.

The exact question to ask

If you or someone you know is a domestic violence survivor facing charges connected to that abuse, ask directly, and ask before any plea is on the table: "Am I eligible to request a hearing under Penal Law 60.12, and has that been raised with the DA yet?" After People v N.H., a prosecutor cannot lawfully condition a plea offer on giving that hearing up. If a deal comes with that condition attached anyway, that is now the exact thing to push back on.

This sits in the same category as every other alternative-sentencing and diversion option covered here: judicial diversion under CPL Article 216 works the same way for eligible felony drug cases, decided by a judge weighing real evidence, not handed out by default. And the pressure to give up a real hearing in exchange for a "certain" outcome is the same pressure described in what every plea deal is actually betting on. The family and household definition that determines who counts as an abuser under 60.12 is the identical standard used to decide who a criminal court order of protection can be issued against, covered in how a criminal court order of protection actually works in New York.

Know this before anything is signed

A hearing you're entitled to is not a bargaining chip someone else gets to trade away on your behalf. Get the full picture of every option on the table, including this one, before agreeing to anything with a prosecutor. Sit with your family and make the decision there, not in a hallway outside the courtroom.
