What Happens If You Violate Parole in New York? Executive Law § 259-i Explained

Probation and parole get treated like the same word by people who've never had to sit through either hearing. They are not the same system. Violate probation and a judge who never left the courthouse decides your fate under CPL 410.70. Violate parole and it's the New York State Board of Parole moving under a completely different law: Executive Law § 259-i. Different agency, different hearings, different clock. Confuse the two and you'll walk into the wrong process not knowing what's actually being decided that day.

Two kinds of violation, and who moves first

Under Executive Law § 259-i(3), a parole officer who has probable cause to believe someone on presumptive release, parole, conditional release, or post-release supervision has committed a violation reports it to a member of the Board or a designated officer. From there, the law splits into two tracks. A technical violation gets a written notice of violation, and the person is generally directed to appear rather than arrested outright. A non-technical violation, meaning conduct closer to a new crime, can result in a notice of violation or a warrant for the person's arrest and temporary detention. The statute is specific that fraternizing with someone who has a criminal record, or conduct involving cannabis that is otherwise lawful under New York law, cannot by itself be the basis for a notice of violation or a warrant.

The first 24 hours: a recognizance hearing

If a warrant is executed for a violation alleged to be "in an important respect," the law requires the officer to bring the person before a court, in New York City the criminal court or supreme court criminal term, elsewhere the county, district, or city court, for a recognizance hearing within 24 hours of the warrant's execution. If no court is available within that window, the hearing happens the next day a court is open for any business. At that hearing, the court can only order continued detention if it finds the person currently presents a substantial risk of willfully failing to appear, and that no non-monetary condition would reasonably assure their appearance. Otherwise, the law directs release on the least restrictive non-monetary conditions available, with a presumption of release on recognizance. There is a right to counsel at this hearing, assigned if the person can't afford one.

Detention isn't automatic just because a warrant issued

A warrant gets someone in front of a judge fast. It does not, by itself, guarantee they stay in custody. The statute puts the burden on the state to justify continued detention at the recognizance hearing, not on the person to justify their release. That distinction gets lost when a warrant is treated like a done deal.

The preliminary hearing: 10 days, a lower bar

Next comes a preliminary revocation hearing, held before a hearing officer who has had no prior supervisory involvement over the case, and held at a courthouse rather than a correctional facility. For most technical violations, this has to happen within 10 days of the notice of violation or the recognizance release order. If a court ordered the person detained, that window shortens to 5 days. The standard here is a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not, applied only to the narrower question of probable cause. A conviction for a new crime committed while under supervision counts as prima facie evidence on its own. If the hearing officer finds no probable cause, the notice of violation is dismissed and the person is restored to supervision immediately. If probable cause is found, the case moves forward and the person gets written notice of a final hearing, along with the right to counsel, the right to confront witnesses, and the right to present evidence.

The final revocation hearing: 45 days, a much higher bar

The final hearing has to happen within 45 days of the original notice of violation or recognizance release order (30 days from the preliminary hearing's finding, for cases where a court ordered detention). This is where the burden shifts hard against the state. The standard of proof rises from a preponderance to clear and convincing evidence, a materially higher bar than what got the case to this stage. The person can plead not guilty, guilty, or guilty with explanation, cross-examine every witness against them, and present their own evidence and witnesses. One protection worth knowing by name: the statute bars the same conduct from becoming the basis of a sustained parole violation if a criminal court has already acquitted the person of it, dismissed it, or resolved it through an ACD. The Board doesn't get a second bite at conduct a court already cleared.

The exact question to ask

If you or someone you love is facing a violation, ask directly: "Is this being charged as a technical or non-technical violation, and what specifically is the evidence supporting probable cause?" The technical versus non-technical distinction isn't a formality. It decides whether incarceration is even legally on the table if the violation is sustained.

What actually happens if the violation is sustained

Incarceration is not automatically imposed for a technical violation. The statute reserves reincarceration for specific circumstances and otherwise directs the presiding officer toward restoring the person to supervision, or ordering community-based re-entry services through a qualified nonprofit agency as an alternative to reincarceration. For a sustained non-technical violation, the presiding officer can order reincarceration and set a date for the Board to reconsider release, or, for someone on post-release supervision, order reincarceration for up to the remaining balance of that supervision period. None of this is decided by a jury. All of it runs on a lower overall burden of proof than the case that sent someone to prison in the first place.
