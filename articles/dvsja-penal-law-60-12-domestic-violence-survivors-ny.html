The Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act in New York: What Penal Law 60.12 Actually Does

A woman in New York was facing a maximum of 25 years on an attempted murder charge after she drove her car through a chaotic scene while fleeing an abusive ex. Her lawyer asked for a hearing under a law built for exactly her situation. The prosecution's answer was a plea deal on the condition that she give that hearing up. She took it. Three years and two appeals later, the state's highest court said that trade should never have been on the table. That is not a hypothetical. That is People v N.H., decided by the New York Court of Appeals on April 23, 2026, and it is the clearest proof yet of what happens when a survivor never gets the chance to make her case to a judge before a prosecutor makes the decision for her.

What the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act actually does

Signed into law in May 2019, the Domestic Violence Survivors Justice Act, known as the DVSJA, amended Penal Law § 60.12 to give judges a path around the standard mandatory sentencing ranges for certain survivors of domestic violence. It is not automatic, and it is not a defense to the underlying charge. It is a request for an alternative, shorter sentence, and it only becomes available after a hearing.

To qualify, a defendant has to establish three things at that hearing: that at the time of the offense they were a victim of domestic violence subjected to substantial physical, sexual, or psychological abuse by a member of the same family or household (the same definition used for a criminal court order of protection); that the abuse was a significant contributing factor to the criminal behavior; and that a standard sentence would be unduly harsh given the nature of the crime and the defendant's history and character. If a judge finds all three, the reduction can be substantial: the statute allows determinate sentences well below what the standard scheme would otherwise require, and in some cases a non-incarceratory disposition instead of prison. Some of the most serious homicide and terrorism-related offenses are carved out by the statute itself, so this is not a universal opt-out. It is a targeted correction for one category of case the legislature decided the standard rules got wrong.

This can also reach people already serving time

Under CPL § 440.47, someone currently incarcerated on a sentence of eight years or more, for an offense committed before the DVSJA's effective date, can move for resentencing under the same framework. The application has to include at least two pieces of corroborating evidence of the abuse, and at least one has to come from a specific list: a court record, a presentence report, a hospital record, a sworn witness statement, a law enforcement record, a domestic incident report, or an order of protection. A 2023 analysis by the Sentencing Project and the Survivors Justice Project found that, as of that point, 40 survivors had been resentenced under this retroactive provision, while at least 32 applications had been denied. This is discretionary relief, not a formality, and it depends entirely on someone knowing to apply for it.

Why People v N.H. matters to every case that hasn't been decided yet

N.H. was a survivor of years of documented abuse. While her application for a DVSJA hearing was pending, backed by a psychologist's PTSD diagnosis and a forensic social worker's assessment, the prosecution offered her a plea: a determinate sentence on a single reduced count, in exchange for waiving the hearing entirely. She accepted. The trial court itself flagged the problem on the record, noting there was no case law settling whether that kind of waiver was even allowed, then let it stand anyway. The Appellate Division agreed the waiver was valid. The Court of Appeals did not.

The Court held that a Penal Law 60.12 hearing is "too valuable, both to the [survivor defendant] and to the community, to be sacrificed in plea bargaining," language the opinion borrows directly from its own precedent on rights that cannot be traded away in a deal. The reasoning is worth sitting with: because the overwhelming majority of cases in New York end in a plea rather than a trial, letting prosecutors condition a deal on giving up this hearing would gut the law for almost everyone it was written to help. The decision does not force anyone to request a DVSJA hearing who doesn't want one. It just makes sure that if you do want one, a prosecutor cannot make you trade it away as the cost of a plea. That is the exact same coercive dynamic Case Analytica keeps coming back to in every plea offer being a bet on what you won't fight for: the system runs on deals precisely because it cannot afford trials, and a right that only exists on paper protects nobody standing in a courtroom hallway with a plea sheet in front of them.

The exact question to ask

Before anyone signs anything: "Does anything about my history of abuse make me eligible for a DVSJA hearing under Penal Law 60.12, and does this plea offer ask me to give that hearing up?" As of April 2026, the answer to whether that trade is legal is no. A judge, not the district attorney, is supposed to be the one deciding whether the abuse in your history changes what a fair sentence looks like. That decision belongs to you and the court, not to whoever is offering the deal.

This sits alongside the same pattern covered in judicial diversion under CPL Article 216: another instance where the legislature deliberately put a judge, not a prosecutor, in the seat of final decision-maker, precisely because leaving it to the DA alone had already failed a class of defendants once.
