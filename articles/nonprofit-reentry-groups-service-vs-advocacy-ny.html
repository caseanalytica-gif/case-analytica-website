Which New York Reentry Groups Push to Change the Law, and Which Just Work Inside It

New York has dozens of nonprofits doing reentry work, and it's easy to assume they're all pulling in the same direction: helping someone find a job, find housing, and stay out of the system. Some of that assumption is right. But not every organization that provides reentry services is also fighting to fix the laws and funding gaps that make reentry so hard in the first place, and not every program with a well-funded mission has independent evidence behind it that it actually reduces recidivism. Both of those things matter if you're deciding where to send a donation, where to refer someone who just came home, or which group's name to trust when it shows up in a press release.

This isn't a ranking. It's a look at what four organizations doing reentry work in New York actually say about themselves, in their own published materials, sorted into two categories that matter: groups mainly delivering services within the system as it currently exists, and groups actively campaigning to change the underlying law or funding. Then it's a look at what independent evidence, where it exists, actually says about whether any of this moves the needle on recidivism.

Center for Community Alternatives: both, and the advocacy is real

The Center for Community Alternatives runs direct reentry, housing, and employment programs, but it's also the organization that organized and led the steering committee for the Clean Slate NY coalition, a four-year campaign documented in detail by the Brennan Center for Justice's October 2025 case study of the bill's passage. CCA didn't stop campaigning once Clean Slate passed. Its own campaign pages, checked for this article, show it has since moved its public legislative energy to the Earned Time Act and the Second Look Act, both aimed at shortening excessive sentences, with press statements from June 2025 vowing to "keep fighting" after lawmakers left session without passing either bill. Whatever else is true about CCA, it isn't an organization that only delivers services inside the current rules. It's one that has repeatedly tried to change the rules themselves.

The Fortune Society: extensive advocacy, and an honest account of what the evidence shows

The Fortune Society runs one of the largest reentry service operations in New York City, and its David Rothenberg Center for Public Policy publishes a detailed legislative priorities list every year. The 2026 list, published January 21, 2026, runs to more than a dozen active bills and campaigns: closing Rikers Island, protecting Raise the Age, expanding parole review under Fair and Timely Parole and Elder Parole, and several reentry-funding bills. This is not an organization sitting quietly inside the system.

But "does extensive advocacy" and "has independently verified outcomes" are two different questions, and Fortune Society's own history shows why that distinction matters. A federally funded evaluation of Fortune's reentry services, conducted by Abt Associates researchers Douglas McDonald, Christina Dyous, and Kenneth Carlson for the U.S. Department of Justice's National Institute of Justice and archived through the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research, compared thousands of Fortune clients against a matched group of people released from New York State prisons and New York City jails who never used Fortune's services. The study found a real, positive effect on avoiding homelessness for people released from city jails who used Fortune's services. It found no equivalent effect for people released from state prison, and no evidence of a positive effect on recidivism for either group.

Fortune Society hasn't hidden from that kind of finding. Ronald F. Day, the organization's own vice president of programs and research, served on the National Academies of Sciences committee that produced the 2022 report "The Limits of Recidivism: Measuring Success After Prison," which argued that recidivism, a simple yes-or-no measure of rearrest, is itself an incomplete and sometimes misleading way to judge whether a reentry program is working, since it ignores progress in employment, housing, and health, and can misclassify a technical parole violation as a new crime. That's a genuinely different posture than simply claiming success: it's an organization whose own research leadership is on record saying the standard metric used to judge programs like Fortune's own doesn't capture what actually matters. It's honest, and it also means nobody, including Fortune itself, can currently point to a clean, independently verified recidivism number for its programs specifically.

Why the "limits of recidivism" finding cuts both ways

If recidivism is a flawed measure, that's a reason to be skeptical of any program, on any side of the ideological spectrum, that claims a specific recidivism-reduction percentage without independent verification. It's not a reason to assume a program works anyway. Better employment and housing outcomes are also measurable, and worth asking about directly.

JustLeadershipUSA: organizing and advocacy, not direct service delivery

JustLeadershipUSA operates differently from CCA and Fortune Society. Its own stated mission centers on amplifying the power of directly impacted people through organizing, leadership training, and self-advocacy, not running case-managed service programs. It publicly supported Clean Slate's passage and has focused its recent policy work on parole and probation, specifically pushing back on the use of community supervision as a driver of reincarceration rather than a tool for stability. That focus lines up with the throughline this site keeps returning to: a technical parole violation that sends someone back to custody doesn't just interrupt a sentence, it can cost a job and a lease that took months to secure. See Case Analytica's reporting on the Less Is More Act for what New York actually changed about that pattern, and how far the reform did and didn't go.

Exodus Transitional Community: primarily direct service, with a distinct model

Exodus Transitional Community, founded by Julio Medina after his own 12 years in prison, runs mentoring, trauma counseling, substance use treatment, and employment services out of East Harlem, staffed largely by people who have lived through incarceration themselves. Its own public materials describe a service-delivery model built around lived experience, not a legislative campaign operation. That's not a criticism. A staff that has personally navigated reentry is a real form of expertise, and direct service is genuinely necessary work regardless of whether an organization also runs a lobbying operation. But based on what's publicly documented, Exodus isn't leading statewide legislative campaigns the way CCA led Clean Slate or JustLeadershipUSA leads parole reform organizing, and it would be inaccurate to describe it as doing that kind of work without evidence that it is.

What this means if you're choosing where to put your support

None of this is an argument that service-only organizations are doing something wrong, or that advocacy-heavy organizations are automatically more effective. It's an argument for asking two specific questions before assuming either one: does this organization have independent, not self-reported, evidence that its programs improve employment, housing, or recidivism outcomes, and is it also working to fix the laws and funding structures that make reentry harder than it needs to be. Some organizations, like CCA, can answer yes to the second question with a documented track record. Some, like Fortune Society, can point to real advocacy wins alongside an honest acknowledgment that the outcome evidence is more complicated than a press release usually suggests. And some organizations do essential, values-driven direct service without claiming to be something else.

The throughline holds regardless of which bucket an organization falls into: stable employment and stable housing after release are what actually keep someone out of the system, and any program, or any campaign, is worth judging against whether it moves those two things forward, not against how good its mission statement sounds. For more on the specific legal mechanisms these organizations are pushing to change, see Case Analytica's Clean Slate Act explainer and what a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities actually removes for someone whose record doesn't qualify for sealing yet.
