A Queens robbery case made public this week offers a real-world window into a process most New Yorkers never see up close: what happens between the moment police take someone into custody and the moment a judge first hears the case, and whether that case is even headed toward prosecution in the traditional sense at all.

On August 20, 2026, the Queens District Attorney's Office announced that a 16-year-old from Jamaica has been charged in connection with a string of chain-snatching incidents that targeted women in Jamaica, St. Albans, and Elmhurst in June and July. According to the DA's office, the teenager, not publicly named, consistent with how prosecutors typically handle 16- and 17-year-old defendants, faces two counts of second-degree robbery, two counts of second-degree assault, three counts of third-degree robbery, three counts of fourth-degree grand larceny, two counts of third-degree assault, and petit larceny. Queens District Attorney Melinda Katz said in the release that "this defendant approached unsuspecting pedestrians in broad daylight and forcibly grabbed jewelry from their necks." QNS also reported the case the same day, citing the DA's announcement.

The defendant was arraigned in Queens Criminal Court on August 13, and bail was later set at $2,000 cash or a $4,000 bond by a New York Supreme Court justice, with the case returning September 15. The DA's release doesn't specify when the defendant was physically taken into custody, so that detail is unclear from the public record. What the case does illustrate is the sequence that follows an arrest in New York State, a few wrinkles that apply when the accused is a minor, and where a diversion path realistically does and doesn't fit into that sequence.

An arrest and a set of charges are allegations, not findings of guilt, and nothing here should be read as a comment on whether this defendant actually did what they're accused of. Anyone facing a specific case should talk to a licensed criminal defense attorney rather than rely on a general explainer like this one.

Booking: the first stop after custody

Once someone is taken into police custody in New York City, they're typically brought to a precinct for fingerprinting, photographing, and a records check. What happens next depends largely on the charge's severity.

For many lower-level, nonviolent offenses, NYPD policy allows officers to issue a Desk Appearance Ticket, or DAT, releasing the person the same day with a written instruction to appear in court later. Some DAT recipients never end up prosecuted at all: the city's Project Reset program lets people holding a DAT for a qualifying misdemeanor complete a short program before their court date and avoid a criminal record, if they find out about it in time. Eligibility isn't guaranteed even for qualifying charges, either; an inability to produce ID, an open warrant, or certain priors can push a case toward full custodial processing instead.

For more serious charges, including violent felonies like second-degree robbery, among the charges here, a DAT generally isn't an option, and neither is Project Reset, which is built for misdemeanors, not violent felony counts. The person instead moves through central booking: transport to a borough court complex serving Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, or Staten Island, a stage that can take many hours depending on how backed up that borough's system is at that moment.

Arraignment: the legal clock

New York law generally requires that a person held in custody be brought before a judge for arraignment without "unnecessary delay," which courts have interpreted to mean roughly 24 hours, though actual wait times vary by borough and by how many arrests are moving through the system at once. Arraignment is the first formal court appearance: charges are read, the defendant is advised of the right to counsel, and a judge decides whether to release the person, set bail, or in eligible cases order remand.

In the Queens case, arraignment occurred August 13 in Queens Criminal Court (see Case Analytica's Queens Criminal Court logistics guide for the building's address, transit options, and its 11 p.m. cutoff for same-day arraignments), with bail addressed afterward at the Supreme Court level, a structure typical when a violent felony requires grand jury action or a youth-specific track applies. There's also a statutory backstop on how long someone can be held before that first appearance: under CPL 180.80, a person held on a felony complaint generally must be released on recognizance after 120 hours, 144 across a weekend or holiday, if no indictment or hearing has occurred, though only if someone actually applies for it.

Right to counsel, and what family can (and can't) do

Everyone arraigned in New York has a right to counsel at that appearance, whether through a private attorney or a public defender such as Legal Aid, regardless of ability to pay. What that right doesn't guarantee is a lawyer before arraignment: as Case Analytica has reported, the counsel guarantee attaches at the courthouse, not the precinct, leaving a gap that a 2014 settlement, Hurrell-Harring v. State of New York, narrowed but didn't close.

In the first hours after an arrest, family members generally cannot visit the person at a precinct or during central-booking transport, cannot post bail before a judge has set it, and often can't get real-time updates on where a loved one is in the system. Contacting a defense attorney, checking the city's online arraignment lookup, or reaching a public defender's office are the practical steps that matter most in those first 24 to 48 hours, precisely because direct contact is so limited until arraignment.

Bail, ROR, and supervised release

Since New York's 2019 bail reform law took effect and was later amended in 2020 and 2022, most misdemeanors and nonviolent felonies aren't eligible for cash bail at all, and defendants are released on their own recognizance (ROR) or under supervised release instead. Violent felonies, including robbery charges like those in the Queens case, remain bail-eligible, which is why a judge here set a specific cash-or-bond amount rather than releasing the defendant outright. Case Analytica's explainer on how bail gets set post-reform walks through what changed and what got added back in those later amendments.

A low, seemingly affordable bail number also doesn't always mean release. As Case Analytica has explained, a defendant can be held for a week or more on bail as low as a few hundred dollars if a judge orders a bail source hearing to question where the money is actually coming from, a wrinkle unrelated to the dollar amount itself.

The Raise the Age wrinkle

Because the Queens defendant is 16, the case also sits inside New York's "Raise the Age" framework, in effect since 2018 and 2019. Most 16- and 17-year-olds charged with felonies are classified as "Adolescent Offenders," heard in a specialized Youth Part of the criminal court system, staffed by judges with Family Court training, rather than in Family Court itself or treated identically to adult defendants. Nonviolent felonies are presumptively moved to Family Court; violent felonies, the category that includes second-degree robbery, generally stay in the Youth Part unless a judge orders removal. The public record here doesn't specify whether removal was requested or decided.

That's a separate question from what happens to the record afterward. Raise the Age governs which court hears the case; a distinct mechanism, Youthful Offender status under CPL Article 720, governs whether a conviction later gets sealed. As Case Analytica has explained, the two are frequently confused, and failing to request YO status before sentencing can cost a young person a sealed record they were otherwise entitled to.

Is a diversion path realistically available here?

It's worth asking, for any case like this one, whether the arrestee's situation could route toward a diversion program instead of standard prosecution, and what would actually have to be true for that to happen. New York has several real diversion mechanisms, but none of them fit every case, and the charges in the Queens complaint sit closer to the edge of what most of them are built for.

Project Reset, discussed above, is off the table here since it's built for DAT-eligible misdemeanors, not violent felony counts. Judicial diversion under CPL Article 216, which lets a judge send an eligible felony defendant to treatment instead of prison, is limited by statute to specific felony drug offenses, so it wouldn't apply to a robbery and assault case regardless of age. An Adjournment in Contemplation of Dismissal, or ACD, functions like an informal diversion: complete a clean period and the case is dismissed and sealed, but it's typically reserved for lower-level charges, and a prosecutor has to agree to offer it.

That leaves two more realistic angles specific to this case. The first is the Raise the Age removal question already discussed: because second-degree robbery is a violent felony, this case doesn't automatically move to Family Court the way a nonviolent felony would, but a judge can still order removal after reviewing the specifics, and the public record doesn't say whether that was requested here. The second is restorative justice or alternative-to-incarceration programming, which some NY county DA's offices, including in Queens, have expanded in recent years. Case Analytica's coverage of NYC's restorative justice funding notes that teens arrested on serious charges, including gun cases, have been routed into circle-based programs rather than standard prosecution in some instances, and a broader overview of restorative alternatives lays out how those programs typically get offered.

Whether any of that applies to this particular defendant isn't something the public record answers. The complaint describes three separate incidents against three different people, which is the kind of pattern that tends to make prosecutors and judges more cautious about diversion, but multiple charges alone don't rule it out, and a lot depends on facts that aren't in the DA's press release: the defendant's prior record, if any, the strength of the evidence, and what Queens' DA's office and defense counsel negotiate as the case moves toward its September 15 return date. That's a genuinely open question here, not a settled one, and it's exactly the kind of thing a defense attorney would be pursuing from day one rather than something a general explainer can answer.

General information, not legal advice

New York's arrest-to-arraignment process has real timelines and real rights attached to it, but how those rules, and any diversion options, apply to a specific case is something only a licensed attorney reviewing the actual file can answer.
