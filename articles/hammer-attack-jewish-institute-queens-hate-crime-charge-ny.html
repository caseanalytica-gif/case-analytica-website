A Man Chased a Woman With a Hammer Onto a Queens Jewish School's Grounds. Why Wasn't It Charged as a Hate Crime?

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-11

On July 28, 2026, a man chased a woman down Queens Boulevard in Elmhurst while swinging a hammer. She ran into traffic to get away from him, then took refuge inside a fenced schoolyard at the Jewish Institute of Queens on Woodhaven Boulevard. He followed her onto the property, pushed an eight-year-old boy into a metal gate hard enough to require six stitches, and bit a staff member who helped restrain him. According to the Queens District Attorney's Office, a grand jury has since indicted Shaleek Brunson, 42, of Brooklyn, on an eleven-count indictment: five counts of second-degree assault, one count of third-degree criminal possession of a weapon, two counts of third-degree assault, menacing, endangering the welfare of a child, and criminal trespass. He faces up to fourteen years in prison if convicted.

Nowhere in that list is a hate crime charge. The attack happened on the grounds of a religious school, during a children's summer camp, and the case still made news specifically because of where it ended up. That's a reasonable thing to notice, and a reasonable thing to ask about. It's also a good real-world example of a legal standard most people have never actually read: New York's hate crime law doesn't ask where an attack happened. It asks why the defendant went after the specific person he went after.

This isn't a claim that Brunson committed, or didn't commit, a hate crime. Everything here comes from the Queens DA's own charging documents and public statements. Brunson is presumed innocent unless and until a jury says otherwise, and nothing below should be read as a comment on his guilt. What follows is what New York's hate crime statute actually requires, and why the facts as charged in this case explain why prosecutors didn't reach for it here.

What the DA's office actually charged

According to the Queens District Attorney's press release, Brunson approached a woman walking with two other people near the Queens Center Mall at around 11 a.m. and chased them while swinging a hammer. At the intersection of Woodhaven Boulevard and the Long Island Expressway, the woman ran across the road in traffic to escape him. She found a fenced-in area at the Jewish Institute and went inside. Brunson followed, pulled on the locked front doors of the school building, then went into the schoolyard, still holding the hammer, where children were playing. A camp counselor and a bystander got the hammer away from him. As he ran toward the yard's metal gate, he pushed several children out of his way and shoved an eight-year-old boy into the gate, opening a laceration on the boy's forehead. Brunson then ran into the school itself, where staff restrained him, and bit one of them on the hand. DA Melinda Katz said in the release: "This daytime assault shattered the sense of safety and security for everyone connected to this school and neighborhood." Gothamist and QNS both reported the same sequence based on the DA's office. Supreme Court Justice Germaine Auguste ordered Brunson to return to court October 7.

What isn't in the indictment

Eleven counts, and not one of them is a hate crime enhancement under Penal Law Article 485. That's not a gap in reporting. It's the charging decision the Queens DA's office actually made, based on the facts its own press release lays out.

What Penal Law 485.05 actually requires

New York's hate crime statute, Penal Law § 485.05, doesn't criminalize attacking someone near a place tied to a particular group. It requires that the defendant selected the specific person he targeted, in whole or substantial part, because of a belief or perception about that person's race, color, national origin, ancestry, gender, gender identity or expression, religion, religious practice, age, disability, or sexual orientation. That's an intent element aimed at the victim, not the location. A person can commit an assault a few feet from a synagogue, a mosque, or a school without it being a hate crime, if nothing shows he picked his target because of who that person is or what group she belongs to.

Case Analytica has already written a full explainer on how that enhancement works and what it adds to a sentence once it applies; see How New York's Hate Crime Law Turns One Attack Into Multiple Enhanced Felonies. The short version: the underlying offense gets bumped up a class, sometimes two, once a hate crime count attaches, and that upgrade turns on proving the defendant's motive, not on where the incident occurred.

Based on the facts the DA's office has made public, the woman Brunson is accused of chasing was first approached on a public street, not at the school. The school became part of the story because she ran there to escape him, not because he was targeting the building or the people inside it before she arrived. Nothing in the DA's account describes Brunson saying anything about the victim's religion, or selecting her, the eight-year-old, or the staff member because of any protected characteristic. Absent that kind of evidence, the statute's intent element isn't satisfied no matter how significant the location turns out to be to the community around it.

Why this distinction matters beyond one case

If proximity to a religious or ethnic institution were enough on its own, prosecutors would be adding hate crime counts constantly, since violence happens near all kinds of buildings all the time. The law is written narrower than that on purpose: it targets why a specific person was chosen, not where the chase happened to end.

The charges that are actually doing the work here

What Brunson is charged with, assault in the second degree, weapon possession, endangering the welfare of a child, menacing, and criminal trespass, already carries serious exposure. Five separate second-degree assault counts plus a weapon possession charge is how New York law captures repeated, escalating violence against multiple people in a single incident, without needing a hate crime theory to make the case reflect its real severity. A conviction on even a portion of an eleven-count indictment like this one can carry years in state prison; the DA's office cites a maximum of fourteen years.

Because second-degree assault is a violent felony, this case moved into New York's post-bail-reform framework the way a nonviolent case wouldn't. Case Analytica's explainer on how bail actually gets set after reform covers why violent felony charges like these remain bail-eligible, and why a judge in a case like this one can order a defendant held pending trial rather than released.

What to actually ask, if a case near you raises the same question

It's a fair instinct to ask "why wasn't this a hate crime" whenever an attack happens near a school, a house of worship, or a community clearly tied to one identity. The honest answer is usually the same one that applies here: the statute isn't about the map, it's about the motive, and prosecutors need actual evidence of that motive before they can charge it and prove it to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Charging a hate crime that the evidence doesn't support doesn't protect anyone; it just hands the defense an appeal issue and risks the whole case. If new evidence about Brunson's intent surfaces as this case proceeds toward its October 7 return date, the Queens DA's office can still add or amend charges. As of the indictment, it hasn't.

General information, not legal advice

Whether any specific incident meets the legal definition of a hate crime depends on evidence a general explainer like this one can't evaluate. If you or someone you know was targeted because of who they are, an attorney and, where relevant, the NYPD Hate Crime Task Force are the right first calls.
