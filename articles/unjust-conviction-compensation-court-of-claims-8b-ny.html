Can You Get Compensated for a Wrongful Conviction in New York? Court of Claims Act 8-b Explained

Getting a conviction thrown out is not the same thing as getting paid for the years it took from you. Those are two separate fights, filed in two separate places, on two separate clocks. Most people only find out there's a second fight after the first one is already over, which is exactly when a filing deadline can quietly close the door on it.

What Court of Claims Act 8-b actually is

New York created a specific path for this in 1984: Court of Claims Act § 8-b, the state's Unjust Conviction and Imprisonment Act. It lets someone who was convicted of a felony or misdemeanor, sentenced, and imprisoned for it, sue New York State directly for damages, but only if they can prove they didn't do it. The legislature wrote its own reasoning into the statute: people who were actually innocent had been "frustrated in seeking legal redress due to a variety of substantive and technical obstacles in the law," and deserved an avenue built specifically for them, separate from an ordinary lawsuit.

That word "frustrated" is doing real work. Ordinary tort law was never built for this. Section 8-b was.

The burden is not "not guilty." It's "clear and convincing evidence."

To win under § 8-b, you have to prove, by clear and convincing evidence, three separate things: that you were convicted and served all or part of a sentence, that the conviction was later reversed, vacated, or pardoned on innocence grounds tied to specific legal categories, and that you did not commit the acts charged and did not cause your own conviction through your own conduct. This is a higher bar than the reasonable-doubt standard the state had to meet to convict you in the first place, and it's a separate proceeding with its own evidence rules.

The dismissal has to be the right kind of dismissal

Not every case that ends in your favor qualifies. The statute requires the reversal or vacatur to rest on specific grounds: certain subdivisions of Criminal Procedure Law § 440.10, the same statute that lets a New York court vacate a judgment after the fact for reasons that never made it onto the trial record, or comparable grounds under CPL § 470.20, or a constitutional violation in the underlying statute itself. If your case was dismissed on a technicality unrelated to innocence, § 8-b doesn't apply. The dismissal has to actually say, in effect, that you didn't do it.

The clock: two years, not the deadline you've heard about elsewhere

Anyone who has spent time around New York's court system has heard about tight notice deadlines for suing the state or a city. Section 8-b runs on its own separate clock: a claim has to be filed within two years of the pardon or the dismissal of the accusatory instrument, under subdivision seven of the statute. Two years sounds like a long time until you remember what's usually happening in it: reentry, finding housing, finding work with a record that hasn't been sealed yet, and, very often, pursuing a completely different lawsuit at the same time.

This claim and a civil rights lawsuit are not the same thing

A federal civil rights suit against a city, a county, or individual officers for misconduct that led to the wrongful conviction runs on entirely different law, in a different court, against a different defendant. Alan Newton, a Bronx man who spent 21 years in prison after an eyewitness misidentification, was exonerated in 2006 once DNA testing the Bronx DA's office had spent over a decade claiming was lost turned out to be sitting in the exact evidence bin it was supposed to be in. He later won $18.5 million from New York City in a federal civil rights case, one of the largest wrongful conviction awards on record. That case ran against the city. A § 8-b claim runs against the state. Pursuing one does not automatically preserve or extend the other, and an attorney focused entirely on the bigger federal case can let the state law claim's two-year window close without meaning to.

The exact question to ask

If a conviction was just vacated, reversed, or you were pardoned on innocence grounds, ask directly: "Does this dismissal qualify under Court of Claims Act 8-b, and when does the two-year clock start running?" Ask it of whoever handled the vacatur, not months later. If they don't handle Court of Claims practice themselves, ask for a referral to someone who does. This is not a claim that gets raised automatically just because a conviction was overturned. Like every diversion program and every post-conviction remedy in this state, it depends on someone actually raising it.

Once a case is dismissed, what happens to the arrest record itself is a separate question with its own automatic process. See what CPL 160.50 actually seals after a dismissal for what gets sealed without a petition, and what doesn't.
