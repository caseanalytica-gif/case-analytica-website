At the Brooklyn Museum on November 16, 2023, a woman named Melinda Agnew stood next to Governor Hochul as she signed the Clean Slate Act into law and told the room what a criminal record had actually cost her. "In October of 1999, I was sentenced to three years' probation," she said. "I completed my sentence, and since then, I have become a mom and a grandma, returned to school, and obtained both my bachelor's degree and my master's. Yet 25 years later, despite all that I have done to give back to my community, I am still turned away from jobs, denied promotions and rejected from housing programs because of my two-decade-old conviction." Her statement is published directly on the website of the Center for Community Alternatives (CCA), the organization she's a member of and one of the lead groups that spent four years fighting to pass the bill.

Agnew's case is the human version of a question researchers have actually studied: does sealing a record change what happens to a person's job and their paycheck, or is that just the pitch lawmakers use to get a bill passed. It turns out there's a real answer, and it's the reason Clean Slate's own sponsors kept using the word "economy" as much as they used the word "fairness."

The study Clean Slate was actually built on

The single most cited piece of evidence behind Clean Slate-style laws nationally is a Michigan study published as "Expungement of Criminal Convictions: An Empirical Study," 133 Harvard Law Review 2460 (2020), by University of Michigan law professors J.J. Prescott and Sonja Starr. Using a data-sharing agreement with Michigan state agencies, they tracked what actually happened to people after their records were cleared under that state's "set-aside" law, the closest legal cousin to New York's sealing statute. Three things stood out. First, uptake was low: only 6.5 percent of people who were legally eligible for record-clearing actually obtained it within five years, mostly because the old process required a petition, a fee, and a lawyer, exactly the kind of friction New York tried to remove by making Clean Slate automatic. Second, the people who did get their records cleared had extremely low subsequent crime rates, comparing favorably to the general population, not just to other people with records. Third, and most directly relevant here: those same people saw wages rise by an average of 25 percent within two years compared to their pre-clearing trajectory, an effect driven mostly by unemployed people actually finding jobs, not already-employed people getting raises.

That 25 percent wage figure, along with a companion 11 percent employment increase, is the exact number the Center for Community Alternatives cites on its own Clean Slate campaign page when it makes the economic case for New York's law. It isn't a talking point invented for a press release. It's a peer-reviewed empirical finding about what happens when the exact mechanism New York just built, an old record disappearing from view, actually gets used.

What New York's own numbers add to that picture

The Brennan Center's analysis of poverty and criminal records in New York State found that New Yorkers with a criminal record collectively lose an estimated $12.6 billion in earnings every single year. A felony conviction alone cuts lifetime earnings by roughly $100,000. Time actually served in prison cuts a person's subsequent annual earnings by about 50 percent, adding up to an average lifetime loss near $500,000. Even a misdemeanor, the kind of record that seals fastest under Clean Slate, slashes annual earnings by more than 15 percent. More than 2.3 million New Yorkers carry a record like this, and 80 percent of them are Black or Latinx, a figure that tracks directly with decades of unequal policing and prosecution, not unequal offending.

Why this is the throughline, not a side point

A person who can't pass a background check can't get hired. A person who can't get hired can't qualify for a lease. Once both of those doors are closed, the research is blunt about what tends to happen next: instability, not a second arrest by choice, is what actually pulls people back toward the system. That's the same logic Hochul herself invoked at the signing, and it's the same logic behind every piece of research cited above. Clean Slate was never really a records bill. It's an employment and housing bill that happens to work by editing a record.

New York's own implementation timeline is where that promise runs into a wall it hasn't fully cleared. Automatic sealing only began taking effect starting November 16, 2024, and the state gave its own court system until November 16, 2027 to finish building the systems that actually do the sealing. Case Analytica has covered that gap in detail elsewhere: see what actually changed in the bill before Hochul signed it, and what every year of the implementation delay is costing, and what state agencies told lawmakers about funding and staffing for the sealing system itself. Melinda Agnew spoke about her conviction at the bill's signing, a full year before automatic sealing even began. Her case is the reason the research above mattered enough to build a law around it in the first place, not a claim about where any specific record stands today.

If you think your own record already qualifies

Start with Case Analytica's full breakdown of Clean Slate's waiting periods and exclusions to see whether your conviction is the kind of record the law covers, then check your status directly through the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services record review process. If your record doesn't qualify yet, or you can't afford to wait out the state's 2027 deadline, a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities can remove many of the same employment and licensing barriers without waiting on the sealing clock. See who qualifies and how to request one.

What actually shows up on a background check right now

The research above measures what happens once a record is actually gone. Whether your record is actually gone yet, versus just old enough to qualify on paper, are two different questions, and New York's own agencies have acknowledged the gap between them. See Case Analytica's explainer on what actually shows up on a background check in New York today for how that plays out on an actual job application, and what Correction Law Article 23-A already requires of an employer even before a record seals.

Melinda Agnew didn't need a Harvard Law Review citation to know what a two-decade-old conviction was costing her. She'd been living the answer for 25 years by the time she said so out loud, standing next to the governor who'd just signed the bill meant to fix it. The research exists now because people like her told legislators what the cost actually was, long before anyone ran the numbers. Know exactly where your own record stands, and don't let a job application or a lease be the first place you find out it hasn't caught up yet.
