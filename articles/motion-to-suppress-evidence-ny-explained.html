Can Evidence Get Thrown Out Before Trial? What a Suppression Hearing in New York Actually Does

Ask most people what happens between an arrest and a plea, and they'll describe the offer. Almost nobody describes the fight that's supposed to happen first. Before a case ever reaches a plea, New York law lets a defendant challenge how the evidence against them was actually obtained. If a search was illegal, if a confession was coerced, if a lineup was staged without a lawyer present, that evidence can be kept out of the case entirely, before a jury ever sees it. This is what a suppression hearing does, and skipping it is one of the quietest ways a case gets settled on the state's terms instead of the facts.

What a suppression motion actually lets you challenge

The authority for this sits in Criminal Procedure Law § 710.20. It allows a defendant to move to suppress evidence on several grounds: physical evidence obtained through an unlawful search or seizure, a statement or confession that was involuntary, testimony from an eyewitness identification procedure that was improperly conducted, and certain chemical test results. If the motion succeeds, that evidence cannot be used against the defendant at trial. Depending on how central that evidence is to the case, a successful suppression motion can leave the prosecution with little or nothing left to prove the charge.

The hearings, and what each one actually tests

These motions go by the names of the cases that created them, and each one tests something different.

A Mapp hearing challenges physical evidence obtained through a search, testing whether the search itself was legal. It comes from Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961), the U.S. Supreme Court decision that made the exclusionary rule binding on state courts: evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment cannot be used against a defendant, period.

A Huntley hearing challenges a confession or statement, testing whether it was made voluntarily. It comes from People v. Huntley, 15 N.Y.2d 72 (1965), where New York's Court of Appeals set the rule that still governs: the burden is on the prosecution, not the defense, to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a statement was voluntary before a jury ever hears it.

A Dunaway hearing challenges the arrest itself, testing whether police had probable cause before taking someone into custody. It comes from Dunaway v. New York, 442 U.S. 200 (1979), a case that started in Rochester: police brought a man in for questioning without probable cause for a full arrest, and the Supreme Court held that anything obtained from that questioning, including a confession, had to be suppressed as the fruit of an illegal seizure.

A Wade hearing challenges an identification procedure, like a lineup, testing whether it was conducted fairly and with counsel present when required. It comes from United States v. Wade, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), which held that a lineup held after a defendant has been formally charged is a critical stage of the case, meaning the defendant has a right to have a lawyer there.

These four motions can be, and often are, filed together in a single combined motion. Which ones apply depends entirely on the specific facts of the arrest: what was searched, what was said, how the arrest itself came about, and whether an identification procedure was used.

Why this matters before any plea gets discussed

A plea offer is priced on the strength of the case against you. If the evidence that case depends on can be thrown out, the strength of that case changes, sometimes completely. But that only happens if the motion actually gets filed and litigated. See why innocent people plead guilty for the pressure that pushes people to accept a deal before that fight ever takes place.

It doesn't happen on its own

A suppression motion is not automatic. Nothing gets reviewed by a judge unless the defense actually files the motion, in writing, laying out the specific grounds under CPL 710.20 and the facts supporting them. If it's not filed, or filed too late, the issue can be waived entirely and the evidence simply comes in. This is exactly the kind of decision that has to happen early, with someone who has actually looked closely at how the evidence in your specific case was gathered, not after a plea is already on the table.

Some of these motions start with a stop that should never have happened in the first place. See Can NYPD Stop and Frisk You Without a Reason? for what reasonable suspicion actually requires under CPL 140.50, and what a stop without it can mean for anything found afterward.

The exact question to ask

"Has a suppression motion been filed in my case, and if not, why not?" If your attorney hasn't reviewed how the search, the statement, or the arrest itself happened, ask directly. This is not a technicality. It can be the difference between a case with real evidence behind it and one without.
