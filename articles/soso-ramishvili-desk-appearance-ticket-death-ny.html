Soso Ramishvili, 32, was arrested on March 18 at the 63rd Precinct in Brooklyn on charges of petit larceny, criminal possession of stolen property, and criminal possession of a controlled substance, after he allegedly shoplifted from a Home Depot in Mill Basin, according to reporting by Brooklyn Paper. He was scheduled to be arraigned the next morning. That hearing got rescheduled. So did the ones after it. Three days later, on March 21 at about 8:25 a.m., a member of the courthouse medical team found him slumped over in a holding cell at Kings County Criminal Court, at 120 Schermerhorn Street in Downtown Brooklyn. EMS pronounced him dead at the scene. He was never arraigned. Nobody, including the Brooklyn District Attorney's Office, had even reached the point of hearing the case against him.

This article is about what should have happened to Ramishvili under New York law, and why his case sits inside a pattern the city's own public defenders have been sounding an alarm about for over a year.

What CPL 150.20 actually requires

New York law is specific about this. Under Criminal Procedure Law § 150.20, a police officer authorized to arrest someone for most low-level offenses, meaning anything short of a violent or serious felony, is required to issue a written appearance ticket and release that person, not take them into custody, unless a specific exception applies: an outstanding warrant, a documented history of failing to appear in court, or an inability to verify the person's identity after a reasonable chance to do so. Self-identification alone, the statute says, can be enough. Petit larceny, the top charge against Ramishvili, is exactly the kind of offense the law was written to keep out of a holding cell.

In a joint statement after his death, the Legal Aid Society and Brooklyn Defenders said Ramishvili "should have been granted a desk appearance ticket and released based on the offense," and that instead he was "allowed to languish in pain in custody for three days" while "deprived of medical care despite repeated pleas from defense lawyers and other personnel to secure them needed care." Police sources gave a different account to Brooklyn Paper, saying Ramishvili was taken to the hospital multiple times over those three days and released back into custody each time by treating doctors. The medical examiner's office had not determined a cause of death at the time of that reporting, and the NYPD said its Force Investigation Division was handling the case. What isn't in dispute, on either account, is that a man accused of a low-level, non-violent offense spent three days in a courthouse holding cell without ever seeing the judge who was supposed to decide his case within roughly a day.

This wasn't the only case that week

Months later, according to reporting from the Queens Daily Eagle, a man named Christopher Nieves died in a Brooklyn courthouse holding cell in August while awaiting arraignment on a charge of stealing food from a Williamsburg grocery store. His attorney at the Legal Aid Society said he appeared to be in visible medical distress, including a bandaged foot and yellowed skin, and asked to go to a hospital. He was never taken. He died a few hours later.

A pattern, not an isolated tragedy

Ramishvili's and Nieves's deaths came during a documented spike in how often NYPD holds people in custody instead of issuing the ticket the law calls for. According to a letter the Legal Aid Society sent the New York City Department of Investigation, obtained and reported on by the New York Daily News, the number of people arrested for misdemeanor drug possession who were put through full custodial processing rose 59 percent in the first two months of 2025 compared to the same period the year before, 2,086 arrests versus 1,312. People held to arraignment on noncriminal violations rose 188 percent over the same window, from 86 to 248. Legal Aid also found that 75 percent of petit larceny suspects, Ramishvili's charge, were put through the system in that window, compared to an average of 45 percent in 2021. An NYPD spokesperson told the Daily News the numbers reflect a legislative change that expanded the criteria for a custodial arrest, not a policy of ignoring the appearance-ticket law.

The city's Department of Investigation and the NYPD's own Office of the Inspector General confirmed at a City Council hearing that they were investigating the broader rise in in-custody deaths, which by that point had topped 50 since the start of 2023, including nine in a single year. And the pattern hasn't stopped. On July 24, 2026, a 29-year-old named Julian Pagan was found unresponsive in a Bronx courthouse holding cell hours after his arraignment, shortly after learning he'd be sent back to Rikers Island, according to Gothamist. His attorney said he'd shown no signs of medical distress hours earlier. He is at least the fifth person to die in, or shortly after leaving, city Department of Correction custody in 2026 alone.

What public defenders are asking the city to do

The Legal Aid Society, Brooklyn Defenders, the Bronx Defenders, New York County Defender Services, and the Neighborhood Defender Service of Harlem jointly released a 10-point plan after the string of deaths, calling on the city to end broken-windows-style low-level arrests, station medics in arraignment courtrooms, and give councilmembers oversight power to inspect NYPD holding cells directly. A City Council bill backed by Councilmember Nantasha Williams would require the city to open health clinics inside its criminal courthouses.

Why the ticket, not just the custody, is the point

A desk appearance ticket exists precisely so a low-level charge doesn't cost someone their job, their childcare arrangement, or their housing while the system works out whether the charge even sticks. Case Analytica has covered how that mechanism is supposed to work, and how narrow the window actually is once someone is held instead: see CPL 180.80's 120-hour rule for the statutory backstop on felony holds, and how Project Reset uses a desk appearance ticket to route eligible misdemeanors away from prosecution entirely, assuming the ticket gets issued in the first place. Ramishvili's case shows what happens when it doesn't: not a diversion opportunity missed, but three days that should never have happened at all.

If you or someone you love is arrested in New York City for a low-level offense and isn't issued an appearance ticket, that's worth raising directly with a defense attorney immediately, not after the fact. The statute is specific about when custody is actually allowed. Knowing that distinction, and saying it out loud to whoever is holding the case file, is not a small thing.

General information, not legal advice

This article describes what CPL 150.20 requires and how several documented cases unfolded, based on public reporting. It is not a statement about the cause of Soso Ramishvili's death, which remains under investigation, and it is not legal advice for any specific case. Anyone facing an arrest or representing someone who is should speak with a licensed attorney.
