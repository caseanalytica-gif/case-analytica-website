Why Rikers Island Should Close, and What Could Actually Replace It

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-08-13

Earlier today, the NYC Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice shared a video series from the Independent Rikers Commission and Echoes of Incarceration, Inc., titled "Reasons to Close Rikers." The post frames closure as the Mamdani administration's cornerstone for a safer, fairer city, and lists three arguments: aging, isolated jails compound trauma without reducing crime; the money is better spent on the supportive housing and mental health care that actually address why people end up in the system; and the land itself is worth more to the city repurposed than as a jail. All three are reasonable arguments. None of them are the most urgent one. The most urgent one is that the plan to close Rikers is years behind schedule, billions over its original budget, and still won't have anywhere close to enough beds for the people the city is currently jailing, which means the alternatives to holding someone at Rikers aren't a nice-to-have anymore. They're the thing the entire plan depends on.

What Rikers already costs, restated

We've laid this out in detail before, so the short version: New York City spent $507,000 per incarcerated person in 2023, or roughly $1,389 a day, per the NYC Comptroller's office. As of July 2025, the Department of Correction was holding 7,602 people, which puts the annual cost of running the city's jails at close to $3.85 billion. Eighty-seven percent of that population, as of the most recent breakdown available, hadn't been convicted of anything. Fifteen people died in city jail custody in 2025, according to the Vera Institute of Justice's tracking. None of that has changed since we published those numbers; if anything, it's the baseline every closure argument should start from.

The replacement plan is years late, and still too small

The city's legal commitment to close Rikers was set in 2019, with an original deadline of August 2027. That deadline has already slipped repeatedly. As of the most recent public reporting, the Brooklyn facility is projected to finish in 2029, Queens and Bronx by 2031, and the Manhattan site possibly not until mid-2032, roughly five years past the original date. The initial cost estimate for the four borough-based jails was $8.7 billion in 2019; construction costs have grown substantially since. In January 2026, a federal judge overseeing conditions at Rikers appointed a remediation manager to take direct control of fixing the jail's violence and safety problems, after the city itself failed to, which is its own signal about how the current facility is functioning while everyone waits for its replacement.

The number that matters most

The four borough-based jails are being built for a combined 4,160 beds. As of December 2025, the city was jailing roughly 7,000 people. Even if every facility opens exactly on its newest, already-delayed timeline, there is no version of this plan that closes Rikers without the pretrial population also coming down substantially between now and then. That's not a political preference. It's arithmetic.

So the alternatives aren't optional. What actually works, and what it costs

The city and the reform groups it's amplifying today are right that this shouldn't just be about swapping one jail for four smaller ones. The research on what keeps people showing up for court without holding them in a cell is further along than most of the public conversation acknowledges, and the cost differences aren't subtle.

Text message reminders. A randomized trial covering 5,709 public defender clients, published in Science Advances and covered by NYU Steinhardt, found that sending reminders seven, three, and one day before a court date cut bench warrants by about 20 percent and pretrial jail stays by about 21 percent, across both misdemeanor and felony cases. One of the study's researchers put the cost at "a few cents per case." Against $1,389 a day at Rikers, this is close to free.

Supervised release. New York City already runs its own program: court-mandated check-ins with a case manager instead of cash bail. An MDRC evaluation found it maintains court appearance rates and did not increase new arrests over a nine-month follow-up period, compared with people who would otherwise have been held. The city expanded its funding by $7 million in 2019 and added another $37 million in 2023 as caseloads grew after bail reform. The city hasn't published a clean per-person daily cost, but even the larger of those two figures, spread across a full year of participants, isn't in the same universe as $3.85 billion.

Phone-based check-in apps. The clearest cost comparison available isn't from New York, and it's worth naming that plainly: ICE's SmartLINK app, used as an alternative to immigration detention, runs at roughly $4 per person per day, against roughly $150 a day for detention in that system, according to CBS News' reporting. That's a different population under a different legal framework, so treat it as an illustration of what app-based check-ins cost relative to custody, not a direct stand-in for a New York criminal court program. The gap between "an app on someone's phone" and "a bed and a guard shift" is the same shape either way.

GPS ankle monitors. This is the most expensive of the four, and the most contested. Monitoring is frequently run through private vendors who bill the fee, often in the range of $10 to $15 a day, directly to the person wearing the device, a practice researchers have flagged for effectively taxing people for their own surveillance while they're still presumed innocent. It's still a small fraction of $1,389 a day, but it's also the alternative with the weakest evidence that the monitoring itself, rather than just the fact of release, is what's keeping people out of a jail cell.

The cost stack, in one place

Rikers Island: roughly $1,389 a day, per person. GPS ankle monitor: roughly $10–$15 a day, often billed to the defendant. Supervised release: funded in the tens of millions annually citywide, no clean per-person daily figure published, but a rounding error against the jail budget. Text message court reminders: a few cents per case. The order of magnitude doesn't shift no matter which alternative you pick.

What "ensuring people show up" actually requires

The research consistently points the same direction: most missed court dates aren't defiance, they're logistics, forgotten dates, unclear notices, an inability to take another day off work without losing the job that makes bail or a fine payable at all. A Pew Charitable Trusts brief found most states still underuse the cheapest fix available, a text message, in favor of tools built to punish a failure that a $0.02 reminder often prevents in the first place. That's the actual test any Rikers replacement plan should be measured against: not whether it sounds tough, but whether it gets people to court, and closing Rikers depends on getting that answer right for thousands of people currently held pretrial, not just the ones a video series happens to highlight.

Not everyone held at Rikers is there because bail was unaffordable; some are held on cases where a separate mechanism, a bail source hearing, keeps someone in custody on money they could otherwise pay. See how a bail source hearing works for one of the reasons pretrial detention doesn't always track ability to pay in the first place. And detention itself changes what happens next in a case, including whether it ends in a plea rather than a trial; that mechanism is worth its own explanation, in how holding someone at Rikers becomes leverage for a plea.
