Is the DA Watching When You Open Your Case File? Discovery Platforms and Work Product in NY

Most of what gets handed over in discovery today isn't a manila folder, it's a login. Body camera footage, 911 calls, lab reports, and photographs all sit on a digital evidence platform, and the defense gets an account to view them. What almost nobody flags for a client is that the platform is also keeping a second file nobody asked for: a log of exactly when that account opened each item, how many times, and for how long. That log isn't evidence in the case. It's a record of how the defense is working the case, and there's a real legal question about who gets to see it.

Why an access log is not a neutral technical detail

An access log can say more than it looks like it says. If a defense file has been opened three times on the same lab report and never once on the eyewitness statement, that pattern points at where the defense sees weakness and where it doesn't. It doesn't hand over an argument word for word, but it narrows the field considerably, in the same direction a legal strategy memo would. Vendors that manage these platforms for prosecutors' offices have described exactly this kind of use case: pulling up access records to see how many times, and how recently, a piece of evidence was actually viewed. That capability exists because it's useful for chain-of-custody questions. It's the same capability that, pointed at the defense side of an account instead of the prosecution's own file, starts to look like a window into legal strategy.

What New York law actually protects

Under CPL 245.65, "attorneys' work product," meaning legal research, opinions, theories, and conclusions generated by a lawyer or their staff, along with a defendant's own statements to counsel, is excluded from discovery entirely. The statute protects the thinking behind a case, not just its paperwork. An access log doesn't hand over that thinking directly. But a pattern of which evidence gets revisited, and when, is a shadow of that same thinking, and the work-product doctrine has never been about protecting only documents with the word "strategy" written on them. It's about protecting the mental process of building a defense, which is exactly what a detailed access log can reveal without a single page of it ever being read.

How this sits next to New York's discovery reform

New York's 2020 discovery overhaul, CPL Article 245, was built to fix a real problem: prosecutors sitting on evidence until the last possible moment. It made New York an automatic-discovery state, meaning the DA has to turn over the case file without the defense filing a motion for it. That reform assumed the flow of information only needed to run one direction, prosecution to defense, faster and more completely than before. Nobody was legislating, at the time, for what happens when the platform used to deliver that discovery also happens to track what the defense does with it afterward. The statute closed one gap and left a newer one that didn't exist yet when it was written.

That's not a small oversight. The whole reason notice deadlines and discovery timelines matter so much in a New York case is that the early moves, what gets reviewed, what gets requested, when, are where a defense actually gets built. If the mechanics of reviewing evidence themselves generate a record the other side can request or infer from, the "automatic discovery" reform ends up automatically discovering something it was never supposed to touch.

The exact question to ask, if evidence review happens on a shared platform

"Does this platform log my review activity in a way that's visible to the prosecution or the vendor, and has our office ever confirmed that in writing?" Ask it of the assigned attorney or public defender's office before treating a discovery portal like a private file room, because right now it may not be one.

None of this means digital evidence platforms are the problem. They've made discovery faster and more complete than the old system of paper boxes ever was, which is its own kind of progress. The problem is a legal framework built for physical files being applied to a system that generates metadata nobody accounted for, on a right, work product, that New York decided decades ago was worth protecting absolutely.

General information, not legal advice

This is general information about how digital discovery platforms and New York's work-product rule interact, not legal advice for a specific case. Confirm with a licensed attorney how any particular platform in use on your case actually handles access records.
