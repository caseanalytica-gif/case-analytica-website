A Queens Postal Clerk Faces 89 Counts Over $21,400 in Stolen Money Orders

QUEENS COUNTY · WHITE-COLLAR INDICTMENT

One scheme, 89 separate counts, and a sentencing exposure that runs on how a prosecutor decides to count it.

Alleged theft

$21,400

from 10 people, Feb. 2025 to May 2026

Indictment

89 counts

forgery, identity theft, grand larceny and more

Next date

Sept. 30

Queens Supreme Court

CaseAnalytica caseanalytica.com

On August 31, 2026, Queens District Attorney Melinda Katz announced that Jere Campbell, 44, of the Bronx, had been indicted on 89 counts for allegedly stealing mail and cashing forged money orders while working as a clerk at the Whitestone Post Office. According to the DA's office, Campbell entered the building's distribution room, opened envelopes that arrived at the facility, pocketed money orders inside, and resealed and mailed the now-empty envelopes so nothing looked missing. She then allegedly forged the necessary signatures and cashed the money orders at her own postal window.

The amounts involved were, in isolation, not enormous. One victim mailed three $1,000 money orders to a landlord's postal box; they were cashed but the landlord never received them. Another mailed $3,000 in money orders to Florida to cover homeowners association fees; the envelope arrived, empty. A third victim, according to the DA's office, bought a money order to cover a deposit on a memorial plaque for her parents' grave. Investigators say the pattern ran from February 2025 through May 2026, ten victims, $21,400 total. Campbell was arraigned on the indictment August 27 and is due back in Queens Supreme Court September 30. If convicted, prosecutors say she faces five to fifteen years.

These are allegations. Campbell has not been convicted of anything, and the Queens DA's own release states plainly that criminal complaints and indictments are accusations, with a defendant presumed innocent until proven guilty. Nothing here should be read as a comment on whether Campbell did what she's accused of. What the case does illustrate, clearly, is something most people never think about until they're facing it themselves: how New York actually counts a criminal charge, and why one alleged scheme can turn into dozens of separate counts on paper.

What the indictment actually charges

According to the Queens DA's office, the 89-count indictment breaks down as 15 counts of forgery in the first degree, two counts of scheme to defraud in the first degree, one count of criminal possession of stolen property in the third degree, eight counts of criminal possession of a forged instrument in the first degree, 23 counts of falsifying business records in the first degree, 15 counts of identity theft in the first degree, two counts of grand larceny in the third degree, 15 counts of identity theft in the second degree, five counts of petit larceny, and three counts of grand larceny in the fourth degree. Forgery in the first degree, the top charge by count, is a class C felony under Penal Law § 170.15. Grand larceny in the third degree, which applies to thefts over $3,000, is a class D felony under Penal Law § 155.35.

Why one scheme becomes 89 counts

It looks, on first read, like prosecutorial overkill: 89 separate charges for what's described as a single ongoing scheme against ten people. It isn't unusual, and it isn't arbitrary. New York generally charges financial crimes by the individual act, not the scheme as a whole. Each forged signature can support its own forgery count. Each falsified transaction record at the postal window can support its own falsifying-business-records count. Each victim's stolen money order can support its own larceny or identity-theft count, with the degree of that count turning on the dollar amount involved in that specific instance, which is why the indictment includes both grand larceny counts, for the larger thefts, and petit larceny counts, for the smaller ones.

That structure isn't unique to this case. It's how New York prosecutes any repeated financial offense, from a single skimming scheme to a payroll fraud. The practical effect is that a defendant can be technically exposed to what sounds like a life sentence in count terms, while the actual sentencing exposure is governed by concurrent and consecutive sentencing rules a judge applies at the end of the case, not by simple multiplication. It also hands the DA's office real leverage at the plea table: a defendant weighing 89 counts against a plea to a handful of them is weighing a very different number than the actual time a judge would likely impose after trial. That gap, between what an indictment displays and what a sentence would actually run, is exactly the kind of thing Case Analytica has written about before in The Bet Every Plea Deal Is Making: a plea offer is priced on a guess about what a defendant and their attorney won't risk at trial, and a stack of counts is one of the clearest ways that bet gets built.

The question to ask

If you or someone you know is facing an indictment with a large count number, the count total by itself tells you very little. The question worth asking a defense attorney directly is which of those counts would actually run consecutively if convicted at trial, versus concurrently, and how that real number compares to what's being offered in any plea discussion. The count on the indictment and the time on the table are not the same thing, and conflating them is exactly what makes a large indictment feel more frightening, and more coercive, than the underlying exposure may actually be.

What a conviction here would mean beyond prison time

Campbell's job is part of what makes this case unusual. She's accused of using a position of public trust, as a U.S. Postal Service clerk, to steal from the people she was supposed to be serving. USPIS Inspector in Charge Ketty Larco-Ward said as much in the DA's release, calling out offenders "who abuse their position of trust within the United States Postal Service." A federal position like that one is generally not coming back after a conviction like this, separate from and in addition to whatever sentence a state court imposes.

What happens after that, for any future job outside the federal government, runs into New York's own protections against being permanently locked out of work by an old conviction. Correction Law Article 23-A requires a New York employer to run an individualized analysis before rejecting an applicant over a conviction, rather than issuing a blanket denial. It's a real protection, but it's also a narrower one than people assume: it governs how a future private or public employer in New York has to evaluate an old conviction, not whether the federal government reinstates a position tied directly to the offense itself. Depending on how this case resolves, a conviction here would still follow Campbell into whatever comes next, long after this particular case is closed.
