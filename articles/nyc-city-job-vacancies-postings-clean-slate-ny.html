The City Counted 15,530 Empty Jobs. Its Job Board Listed 3,821.

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-09

Reflects law as of October 8, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

This article is free, and always will be. If it helped you or someone you love, Case Analytica runs on reader support.

♥ Chip In

In April, the New York City Comptroller counted 15,530 vacant full-time positions in city government, a 5.1 percent vacancy rate against the headcount the city's budget authorized. On October 6, the city's own job board listed 1,384 postings covering 3,821 positions, according to the Jobs NYC Postings dataset the city publishes on its open data portal.

For someone with a conviction record, that job board is the door they can see, and the law meant to help them through it isn't finished. The Clean Slate Act took effect November 16, 2024. The courts have until November 16, 2027 to set up automatic sealing, and the state has not published how many convictions have been sealed so far.

The two numbers measure different things, and the gap between them proves nothing on its own. A vacancy is a budgeted seat with nobody in it. A posting is a job the city is advertising right now. Some vacancies are never meant to be filled. The Comptroller estimates that, measured against the positions the city has actually funded, the vacancy rate drops to 2.6 percent. The city also fills many jobs from civil service exam lists rather than open postings.

What the city is advertising

The city refreshes the postings dataset itself. The snapshot Case Analytica pulled was processed on October 6. Of the 1,384 postings open to the public that day:

  • 787 were competitive-class titles, the civil service category generally filled through exams.
  • 148 were marked entry-level. Most, 1,051, were for experienced workers who don't manage anyone.
  • Five agencies posted the most: Health and Mental Hygiene (233), Environmental Protection (159), Design and Construction (128), Social Services (116) and Transportation (105).

Exams are the main door. New York Focus reported in February that 80 percent of city jobs require one, with more than 100 different tests to choose from. The state created NY HELPS, "a state program that lets agencies fill many positions without exams," and 52 of the 57 counties outside the city opted in. The city proposed exempting 51 titles in September 2024. After several city unions sued to block the program, the city told them it "would no longer proceed with its proposal," according to a court filing the outlet cited.

The private market is barely growing

Private employers in New York City had 4,171,000 jobs in August 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That is 27,600 more than in August 2025, an increase of less than 1 percent, and the August figure is preliminary. Across January through August, private employment averaged 0.3 percent above the same months of 2025. These figures are not seasonally adjusted, so each month is compared with the same month a year earlier.

The city's economic development agency measured it a different way in September, and as we reported on October 6, found 3,900 private jobs added so far in 2026, with private employment down 12,900 outside health care and social assistance. Both measures describe a private market adding few jobs.

Hiring statewide did pick up. New York employers, public and private, hired an average of 311,000 people a month in 2025, up from 272,000 in 2024, while job openings held about level at 473,000 a month against 467,000, according to the BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey. Those are statewide figures, seasonally adjusted, and the BLS data service had no 2026 state figures as of October 8.

Who is looking with a record

No public dataset counts how many New York City residents have a felony conviction. These are the closest measures the state publishes:

  • 3,122 people were released from state prison in 2024 after being sentenced by a court in one of the five boroughs, according to the DOCCS prison releases data. That counts where they were sentenced, not where they went home.
  • 5,561 people under DOCCS community supervision lived in the five boroughs on March 31, 2026, according to the state's supervision data. Another 3,526 people statewide had no county recorded.
  • 14,047 people were under felony probation supervision in New York City in 2025, according to the DCJS probation data.

Statewide, the Legal Aid Society said when the Clean Slate Act was signed that it "will automatically seal conviction records for more than 2.3 million New Yorkers." Nationally, the Prison Policy Initiative found formerly incarcerated people unemployed at over 27 percent, nearly five times the general rate, in its 2018 analysis of 2008 data. We found no current figure for New York City.

The law meant to help isn't finished

The Clean Slate Act took effect November 16, 2024. The court system's Clean Slate page says the law gives the courts until November 16, 2027 "to set up the required processes to automatically seal eligible conviction records," and that "once that work is complete," eligible convictions will be sealed for certain civil background check purposes. The systems that decide who is eligible, the page says, "are still being developed."

So a person whose conviction has already passed its waiting period under CPL 160.57, three years for a misdemeanor and eight for a felony, can still be carrying it into a background check today. The court system has not published a count of convictions sealed so far. Case Analytica asked DCJS for one on October 6. We have reported on two things slowing the work down: the agencies building the system told lawmakers they didn't need more money or staff, and a drafting bug the court system says it can't execute as written is still waiting on a fix bill stuck in committee.

What we will keep tracking

These numbers move, so Case Analytica is keeping them in one place on a new jobs and records data page, each with its source and the date it was read: the city's vacancies and postings, private employment, state hiring, the supervision counts, and the count of sealed convictions, which today reads "not published." We plan to ask the court system and DCJS for that count every month and record the answer, including when there isn't one.
