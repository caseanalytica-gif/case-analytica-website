Mental Health Court Diversion in New York: Why It Depends on Which County You're Arrested In

Two people get arrested in New York on the same day, facing the same kind of low-level felony, both living with a serious mental illness that had a lot to do with why they were arrested at all. One is in a county with a mental health court. The other isn't. Same illness, same conduct, completely different outcome, decided entirely by a county line neither of them chose to be standing on.

That is not a hypothetical. It is how New York's mental health court system actually works right now.

Why this isn't the same as judicial diversion for drug cases

New York already has one form of diversion written directly into state law: judicial diversion for eligible felony drug cases under CPL Article 216, which a judge can order in any county, over a prosecutor's objection if necessary, because the legislature made it a statutory right for people who qualify. Mental health court diversion has no equivalent statute. It exists because individual courts, county by county, chose to build one, coordinated administratively through the state court system's problem-solving courts framework. More than 40 mental health courts now operate across the state. New York has 62 counties. Do that math and you land on the actual problem: roughly half the state has no mental health court to divert anyone into at all.

"You walk over an invisible line that divides two counties, and the legal outcomes are completely different."

That's Leanne Lapp, the public defender in Ontario County, describing the disparity directly to New York Focus, the nonprofit newsroom that obtained the state's own participation data through a public records request. In December 2025, Governor Hochul signed a stopgap law letting a case get transferred from a county without a mental health court to a neighboring county that has one. It's a patch, not a fix, and it depends on someone in that first county knowing to ask for the transfer.

What the numbers actually show

According to data the Office of Court Administration released to New York Focus, roughly 1,700 people were active, compliant participants in mental health courts statewide last year. Brooklyn's court, widely treated as the model other counties look to, opened in 2002; by the end of 2025 it had graduated 1,560 people total in over two decades of operation. Recent graduation rates there and in comparable courts have run between 73 and 87 percent, and participants who complete the program typically see their charges reduced or dismissed.

The tradeoff is speed. In Brooklyn, the median time from arrest to a person's first contact with mental health court clinical staff was 265 days last year, nearly nine months, before eligibility is even determined. Add roughly another 104 days to find out if someone actually qualifies, then another 56 days before the program itself starts, and a person can spend well over a year in the system before treatment, the thing that was supposed to be the alternative to incarceration, actually begins.

What's pending, and what it would change

A bill called the Treatment Court Expansion Act would take mental health courts out of the county-by-county patchwork and put them into state law: standardizing eligibility, widening who can be diverted, and reducing how often a guilty plea has to be entered just to access treatment. It has stalled in the legislature more than once despite support from the Court of Appeals and the state's Chief Administrative Judge. Until it passes, or doesn't, whether treatment is even on the table after an arrest still comes down to geography.

The exact question to ask

"Does this county have a mental health court, and if not, can the case be transferred to a neighboring county that does under the law signed in December 2025?" Ask it early, and ask it directly. A public defender carrying a heavy caseload, or a private attorney unfamiliar with problem-solving courts, will not always think to raise this on their own, and the county where an arrest happens to occur should not be the thing that decides whether someone gets treatment or a cell.
