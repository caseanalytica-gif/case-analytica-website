How Mental Health Court Works in New York, and Why It Depends on Which County You're Arrested In

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-10-10

Reflects law as of October 10, 2026

Share X Facebook LinkedIn

In a New York mental health court, a defendant usually pleads guilty in exchange for treatment in the community, and if they finish the program, "their charges are generally reduced or dismissed," New York Focus reported in August. If they don't finish, some "are ultimately sentenced to incarceration." Whether the option exists at all depends on the county. Thirty counties have a mental health court, according to the Office of Court Administration, and about half of the state's 62 counties have none.

Two people arrested the same day, on the same kind of felony, both living with a serious mental illness that had a lot to do with the arrest, can end up on different tracks because of which side of a county line they were on. "You walk over an invisible line that divides two counties, and the legal outcomes are completely different," Ontario County public defender Leanne Lapp told New York Focus.

Who ends up in mental health court

Unlike drug courts, New York Focus notes, mental health courts aren't required by state law. At the Brooklyn Mental Health Court, the defendants "typically live with serious mental illnesses like schizophrenia or bipolar disorder," and most face "felony charges related to assault, robbery, or burglary," according to the same report. A diagnosis alone doesn't get someone in. "Many incarcerated people who have been diagnosed with a mental illness don't meet the criteria for diversion," New York Focus reported, and diversion "typically involves a risk assessment and the victim's consent." The prosecutor's view of the case matters too: one defense attorney told New York Focus that her client's release "hinged on how a prosecutor evaluated her case."

How it differs from judicial diversion for drug cases

New York does have a diversion statute, but it covers a different group. CPL Article 216 reaches defendants charged with class B through E felonies under the drug laws and a short list of other offenses, and it is written into state law. For most of them the decision belongs to the judge, after a hearing if either side asks for one. Like mental health court, it generally takes a plea first: under CPL 216.05(4), "the eligible defendant shall be required to enter a plea of guilty," unless "the people and the court consent" to diversion without one or the court finds "exceptional circumstances." See Case Analytica's explainer on judicial diversion for how that version works.

What the numbers show

Roughly 1,700 people were active, compliant participants in mental health courts statewide last year, according to court data New York Focus obtained through a public records request. The Brooklyn court opened in 2002 and had graduated 1,560 people through the end of 2025, the same report says. Its recent graduation rates have run from 73 to 87 percent, according to Center for Justice Innovation data cited by New York Focus, and participants there "often spend a year or more in the program." New York Focus cites an evaluation of the court that found "significant reductions in re-arrest" among participants, though it calls the broader research "limited."

Getting in takes time. In Brooklyn last year, according to the same data, the median time from arrest to first contact with the court's clinical staff was 265 days. The typical participant then waited 104 more days to learn whether they were eligible, and 56 more to start.

If the county has no mental health court

Outside New York City, a case can be moved to a mental health court in a neighboring county under CPL 230.21, but only "upon motion of the defendant and with consent of the district attorney and the district attorney of the adjoining county." The law took effect in December 2025 and has been amended twice since. Case Analytica's article on the transfer law and the Treatment Court Expansion Act covers both changes, and the bill that would put a diversion court in every county.

The exact question to ask

Before any plea that comes with a mental health court offer, ask the defense attorney: "What exactly am I pleading guilty to, what happens to the charge if I finish the program, and what sentence am I facing if I don't?" The plea comes first and the treatment comes after, so the answer to the last part is the one that matters most if things go wrong. "Don't worry about that, you'll finish" is not an answer. Ask the attorney to spell it out before the plea is entered.
