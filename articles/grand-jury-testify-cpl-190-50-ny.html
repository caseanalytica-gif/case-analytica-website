Can You Testify Before Your Own Grand Jury in New York? What CPL 190.50 Actually Requires

Ask a room full of people who've been arrested in New York whether they knew they had a right to walk into the grand jury room and tell their own side of the story before ever being indicted. Almost nobody says yes. That's not because the right doesn't exist. It's because Criminal Procedure Law § 190.50 hands you a real right and then hands you a deadline to claim it, and nobody is under any obligation to walk you through both at the same time.

What CPL 190.50 actually gives you

The default rule is the opposite of what most people assume: nobody has a general right to appear or call witnesses before a grand jury. The exception that matters is a defendant's own case. Under CPL 190.50(5)(a), when a criminal charge against you is being, is about to be, or has been submitted to a grand jury, you have a right to appear as a witness in your own behalf, but only if, before any indictment is filed, you serve the district attorney with a written notice requesting it and stating an address where you can be reached.

Once that notice is served, the district attorney has to notify the grand jury foreman of the request, and then notify you of the time and place you'll be heard. Show up, sign a waiver of immunity under CPL 190.45, and you have to be permitted to testify and give any relevant, competent evidence about your case, though you can then be questioned by the prosecution.

The trap in this rule

The district attorney has no obligation to tell you a grand jury proceeding is happening at all, unless you've already been arraigned in a local criminal court on a still-pending felony complaint charging the same offense the grand jury is considering. Only in that specific situation does the law require the DA to notify you or your attorney and give you a reasonable time to exercise the right. Outside of that, waiting to be told is the same as giving the right up.

What happens if the DA gets it wrong, and the deadline that erases it

If an indictment is obtained without the district attorney honoring a proper request to testify, the indictment is invalid under CPL 190.50(5)(c). But that protection comes with its own clock: a motion to dismiss on that ground has to be made within five days of being arraigned on the indictment. Miss that window, and the violation is waived. It cannot be raised later, even if it was real.

The exact question to ask

The moment you're arraigned on a felony complaint, ask your attorney directly: "Has a grand jury proceeding started or been scheduled on this charge, and did we serve notice that I want to testify?" If an indictment already came down and you were never given the chance to testify, the challenge has to be filed within five days of your arraignment on that indictment, not five days after you find out something went wrong.

Testifying is also not a decision to make lightly. Signing the waiver of immunity required under section 190.45 means giving up certain protections your testimony would otherwise carry, so this has to be weighed with your attorney against the strength of the case, not treated as a free option. If your case is also sitting on a clock for how long the DA has to declare readiness for trial, see how CPL 30.30's speedy trial rules actually work, since both deadlines can be running at the same time. And if you're still in the earliest hours after an arrest and trying to understand what comes next procedurally, our FAQ on being just arrested in New York covers the other questions that come up first.

Know this before the window closes

A right that expires the moment you're not told about it is not a right you can afford to learn about after the fact. Ask early, ask in writing, and get confirmation that notice was actually served, not just discussed.
