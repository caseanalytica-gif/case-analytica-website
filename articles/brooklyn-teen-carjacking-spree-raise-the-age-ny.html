Two teenagers, both 16, allegedly carjacked two people at gunpoint across three Brooklyn neighborhoods in under an hour, and the spree ended with an NYPD officer firing his weapon at a stolen car that police say was driven straight at him. Both boys are now in custody. One faces two counts of robbery. Whether either of them ends up prosecuted as an adult or routed toward Family Court doesn't turn on how the case reads in a headline. It turns on a specific New York statute, and on one alleged fact in particular: whether a gun was displayed.

According to CBS News New York, the spree began around 7:40 p.m. on September 8, 2026, in Midwood, when a man told police two suspects approached him and one pulled a gun and tried to rob him. Minutes later, on a nearby block, two suspects approached a second man sitting in his parked car, one displaying a gun while the other pulled him from the driver's seat, then drove off in his car. Police say they tracked that stolen vehicle to Marine Park by 8:30 p.m., where it crashed; one suspect ran, the other was taken into custody at the scene. The suspect who fled then allegedly shoved a woman aside on a nearby street and stole her parked car. Officers tracked that second stolen vehicle to a roadblock in Canarsie, where police say the driver steered onto a sidewalk toward an officer on foot; the officer fired a single shot, the car broke through the roadblock, and it was finally stopped after crashing into a pole in East New York. ABC7 New York and amNewYork also reported on the spree. As CBS reported it, the suspect apprehended at the Marine Park crash has been charged with criminal possession of stolen property and obstructing governmental administration; the driver stopped in East New York faces two counts of robbery. Neither teenager has been publicly named by police, consistent with how New York generally handles 16- and 17-year-old defendants.

Everything above is an allegation, not a finding of guilt, and nothing here is a comment on what actually happened that night. Anyone connected to a case like this needs a licensed criminal defense attorney reviewing the actual file, not a general explainer.

Raise the Age doesn't have one door. It has two.

New York's "Raise the Age" law, phased in during 2018 and 2019, moved most 16- and 17-year-olds charged with crimes out of adult criminal court and into a hybrid track. Case Analytica has explained how that's different from Youthful Offender status, which governs what happens to the record afterward, not which court hears the case. What decides where the case is heard is CPL 722.23, and the statute actually splits into two separate tracks depending on the charge.

Under subdivision 1, a defendant charged with a felony that is not a Class A felony and not a "violent felony" as defined in Penal Law 70.02 gets a presumption: the court orders the case removed to Family Court after arraignment, unless the district attorney files a motion within 30 days arguing that "extraordinary circumstances" justify keeping it in the criminal court's Youth Part. The statute doesn't define what counts as extraordinary. New York's Court of Appeals addressed that gap directly this year. In People v. Guerrero, decided in a divided ruling reported by amNewYork, the Court held 4-3 that a Youth Part judge did not abuse discretion in finding extraordinary circumstances for a 17-year-old with five years of Family Court involvement who took part in an armed home robbery, even though the three dissenting judges warned the ruling risked turning "extraordinary" into "ordinary" for any violent felony case. A coalition of legal service providers and reform advocates, the Coalition to Protect Raise the Age, said afterward that the ruling "applies solely to the facts of this case" and doesn't lower the bar for every future one.

Subdivision 2 is a different, faster track, and it's the one that matters most for a case like the Brooklyn spree. When the top charge is a Class A felony or a violent felony under Penal Law 70.02, which covers armed robbery, the court doesn't start with a presumption toward Family Court at all. Instead, within six calendar days of arraignment, the court holds an appearance to review the accusatory instrument, and the district attorney can keep the case in the adult Youth Part by proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, one of three things: that the defendant caused significant physical injury to someone other than a participant in the offense, that the defendant "displayed a firearm, shotgun, rifle or deadly weapon... in furtherance of such offense," or that the offense involved specific sexual conduct. A preponderance standard means "more likely than not," a far lower bar than the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard a trial requires.

Second-degree robbery, the charge reported against the driver in the Brooklyn case, is a violent felony under Penal Law 70.02. And a gun was allegedly displayed, more than once, during the incidents that led to it. That combination is exactly what subdivision 2 was written for: it's the provision that decides, within days rather than a month, whether a case like this one proceeds toward Family Court at all, or whether it stays in the Youth Part on the strength of the weapon allegation alone.

Why the six-day track and the thirty-day track aren't the same fight

The distinction matters because the two tracks put the burden in different places. Under subdivision 1's 30-day "extraordinary circumstances" process, the presumption favors the young defendant; the district attorney has to affirmatively move and prove why this case is unusual enough to stay in adult court, and the Guerrero fight shows how contested that standard still is. Under subdivision 2, for the most serious charges, there's no presumption to overcome in the same way. The question is narrower and moves faster: did the DA prove, by the lower civil standard, that one of three specific aggravating facts is true. A displayed gun during a robbery is one of only three facts in the entire statute that can do that work.

None of this means either Brooklyn teenager's case is settled. The public reporting here doesn't say whether an accusatory-instrument review has been scheduled, what a judge decided, or whether removal to Family Court has been requested or waived by either side. Those are exactly the questions defense counsel would be pursuing immediately, not something a news report or a general explainer resolves.

General information, not legal advice

Which court hears a 16- or 17-year-old's case in New York depends on specific facts a judge has to find, under a specific statute, within a specific number of days. How that applies to any individual case is something only a licensed attorney reviewing the actual charging documents can answer.
