A person serving an indeterminate sentence whom the Parole Board keeps turning down goes home on their conditional release date, and New York law puts that date at about twice the time served before a first parole hearing. Those extra years come out of the time that would have been spent at home on supervision, which is the only time the Less Is More Act's earned time can cut. That is why Case Analytica is documenting what the Board writes when it denies people who hold an Earned Eligibility Certificate.

In July 2026 the Correctional Association of New York released a dashboard on state prison releases showing that conditional release has passed the Parole Board as the most common way out of state prison. In 2019, according to the Correctional Association, parole accounted for about 50 percent of releases and conditional release about 32 percent. As of May 1, 2026, conditional release was about 43 percent and parole about 41 percent.

Why Case Analytica is studying this

The dashboard cannot say how much of the shift comes from Board denials. The Amsterdam News, reporting on it, credits part of it to a move toward determinate sentences, flat terms that never go before the Board and get their own conditional release date. Neither the Correctional Association nor DOCCS publishes the split. Nobody publishes what the Board writes when it denies someone who holds a certificate, which DOCCS issues to people who have been programming successfully, or whether the decision explains how both statutory findings were met.

That is the gap the Case Analytica parole reporting project is built to fill: document Board decisions for people with Earned Eligibility Certificates, quote what the Board actually writes, and publish the outcomes. When a denial can double the time someone serves and shrink what Less Is More can give back, the reasons for that denial should be on the record. Our guide on how a certificate holds up in the interview covers what a person can put on the record themselves.

The math the percentages hide

Parole is a vote. A panel of the Board of Parole decides whether a person with an indeterminate sentence, a minimum and a maximum, goes home once the minimum is served. Conditional release is a date. Under Penal Law § 70.40, it arrives when a person's good behavior time equals the unserved part of the maximum, with no hearing.

Three statutes set the two dates. For most felonies, Penal Law § 70.00 says the minimum can be no more than one-third of the maximum. Correction Law § 803 caps good behavior time on an indeterminate sentence at one-third of the maximum, which puts conditional release at two-thirds of it.

Take a 3-to-9 sentence. The first parole hearing comes at 3 years. The conditional release date is 6 years. A person the Board denies at every hearing serves twice as long as a person released at the first one. The sentence still ends on the same day, at 9 years, so the extra years inside come straight out of the years that would have been spent at home under supervision.

In plain terms

Each parole denial pushes release to the next hearing. If the denials continue, the person goes home on the conditional release date, which for most felony sentences sits at least twice as far out as parole eligibility.

Less Is More only works on the time left

The Less Is More Act starts after release. Its earned time rule, now in Penal Law § 70.40, cuts a person's supervision by thirty days for every thirty days they go without violating a condition, counted from the first day of supervision. It does not apply to anyone whose sentence carries a maximum of life or lifetime supervision.

The credit can only shrink what is left. On the same 3-to-9 sentence:

  • Released on parole at 3 years: 6 years of supervision remain. With no violations, earned time can end it after 3, so the person is fully done at 6 years.
  • Released on conditional release at 6 years: 3 years of supervision remain. Earned time can end it after a year and a half, so the person is fully done at 7 and a half years, six of them in prison.

Same sentence, same clean record after release. Each parole denial moves more of the sentence inside the walls, where Less Is More does not reach.

Where the certificate is supposed to change this

New York already has a rule meant to stop this for people who do what the prison asks. Under Correction Law § 805, a person serving a sentence with a minimum of eight years or less who earns an Earned Eligibility Certificate "shall be granted parole release" at the minimum, unless the Board finds a reasonable probability that the person would not stay at liberty without breaking the law and that release is not compatible with the welfare of society. Both findings are required.

DOCCS's own semiannual report for October 2025 through March 2026 shows DOCCS issued 1,102 certificates for first interviews. The Board approved 700 of them, 63.5 percent. In the other 402 interviews, a person held the certificate and was still not approved for release at the minimum. Unless a later hearing grants release, the conditional release date becomes that person's way out.

Release on either date still needs a home

The State Comptroller's June 2026 audit of DOCCS community-based residential programs found that 4,443 of the 9,883 people released from prison in 2024, 45 percent, had no permanent residence. Of those, 2,489 went to homeless shelters. DOCCS contracts for just 149 beds at 12 locations in nine counties outside New York City. A conditional release date arrives whether or not anyone has checked the housing plan.

What the data does not say

The dashboard counts release transactions, not unique people, and the Correctional Association says it is not a complete count of every release. It does not split conditional releases by sentence type, so it cannot show how many came after parole denials. The DOCCS report counts Board appearances, not people, and covers only sentences with a minimum of eight years or less. The 3-to-9 example assumes the full good behavior allowance and no violations after release. Lost good time or a violation changes both dates.

If you or someone you love is coming home

  • Find out whether the sentence is determinate or indeterminate, and get both dates in writing: the parole eligibility date and the conditional release date. Ask the facility counselor or your lawyer.
  • If a Board hearing is ahead, ask whether DOCCS will issue an Earned Eligibility Certificate. Our parole hearing preparation guide covers what to bring.
  • Work on a housing plan before either date. Ask whether a community-based residential program referral exists for the county you are going to.
  • Learn what supervision will require and how earned time works. See how parole differs from post-release supervision and the Less Is More Act.

General information, not legal advice

Percentages are the Correctional Association's approximations as reported. The 3-to-9 example applies the statutory maximums for good behavior time and earned time. Which release mechanism and which dates apply to a given person depend on the sentence, and an attorney who has the sentence and commitment papers can confirm them.

The exact question to ask

Ask the facility counselor, in writing: "Is my sentence determinate or indeterminate, what are my parole eligibility date and my conditional release date, and will I have an Earned Eligibility Certificate at my Board interview?" If the Board denies you while you hold a certificate, ask for the written decision and check whether it states both findings Correction Law § 805 requires.
