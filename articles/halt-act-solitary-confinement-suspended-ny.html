New York Suspended Its Own Solitary Confinement Law. Here's What the HALT Act Still Says

New York passed a law in 2021 that caps how many days in a row a person can be locked in a cell alone. In February 2025, the agency that runs the state's prisons decided, on its own, that it did not have to follow that cap. More than a year later, a lawsuit over exactly that decision is still open in an Albany County courtroom, and the cap still is not being enforced the way the law describes it.

What the HALT Act actually caps

Before 2021, a person in a New York state prison could be held alone in a cell for up to 23 hours a day, for any number of consecutive days, months, or years. The Humane Alternatives to Long-Term Solitary Confinement Act, signed into law by Governor Cuomo on March 31, 2021, changed that baseline. Source: Albany Law School Government Law Center.

Under Correction Law § 137, a person can be placed in segregated confinement for up to three consecutive days, or six days total in any thirty-day period, on ordinary discipline alone. Going beyond that requires an evidentiary hearing and a written decision finding the person committed one of a specific, narrow list of acts (causing serious physical injury, sexual assault, extortion, inciting a riot, possessing a weapon, or attempting escape) that would make general population housing a real danger to staff or other people. Even then, the absolute ceiling is fifteen consecutive days, or twenty days within any sixty-day period. People 21 or younger, 55 or older, pregnant, in the first eight weeks postpartum, disabled, or diagnosed with a serious mental illness generally cannot be placed in segregated confinement at all.

Anyone held in segregated confinement is supposed to get at least four hours out of the cell a day, including one hour of recreation. The department is also required to publish monthly public reports on exactly who is in segregated confinement and who is in a residential rehabilitation unit, broken down by age, race, gender, and length of stay.

The strike that led to the suspension

On February 17, 2025, corrections officers at Collins, Elmira, and Groveland correctional facilities walked off the job, citing unsafe conditions, an action that spread to nearly all forty-two of the state's prisons and was never authorized by the officers' own union. Days earlier, 43-year-old Robert Brooks had been beaten to death by corrections officers at Marcy Correctional Facility. While the strike was underway, 22-year-old Messiah Nantwi was beaten to death at Mid-State Correctional Facility. Ten officers were indicted over Brooks's death. Sources: Albany Law School; Prison Legal News.

On February 20, 2025, DOCCS Commissioner Daniel Martuscello III issued a memo suspending elements of the HALT Act, as part of what became the deal to end the strike. That suspension has been extended repeatedly since. It's also worth knowing this wasn't the department's first compliance problem: a New York State Inspector General report released in August 2024, before any of this happened, had already found DOCCS was not properly implementing HALT and urged the agency to fix its own recordkeeping.

The exact status right now

The Legal Aid Society's Prisoners' Rights Project sued over the suspension and won an injunction in July 2025 ordering DOCCS to stop it. DOCCS's response wasn't to comply, it was to argue semantics. At an October 2025 hearing, a DOCCS attorney told the court plainly, about HALT's cell-confinement limits: "It does not apply to general population." A senior attorney with Prisoners' Legal Services called that "playing it both ways." In February 2026, the Albany County Supreme Court declined to sanction DOCCS for that position, but it did certify a class action covering everyone in DOCCS custody currently held in cell confinement beyond seventeen hours a day under what the court itself calls "the HALT Suspension." That case, Smalls v. Martuscello, is still open. Source: Prison Legal News.

What this means if someone you love is inside

This is the same pattern that shows up everywhere else in this system: a rule exists on paper, the practice inside the building is something else, and nobody sits the person living it down to explain which one actually applies to them. New York's incarcerated population has already been shown, in other contexts, to be held past what the law technically allows simply because nobody asked the right question at the right time. The cost of that silence is never small. See what New York's largest jail system alone actually costs per person, per year, and how an overloaded system creates exactly the conditions where a technical label like "general population" can decide what happens to someone, discussed in why your public defender might be carrying 100+ cases.

The exact question to ask

"How many hours a day is my loved one actually out of their cell right now, and is the facility calling this general population or segregated confinement?" That distinction, not the label sitting in a file somewhere, is exactly what the current lawsuit turns on. If the hours don't add up to what the law requires, Prisoners' Legal Services of New York is the organization currently litigating this fight and the right place to raise it.

Know the law as written. Ask the exact question about what's actually happening in the cell. Don't take a paperwork label as the truth of what someone is living through right now.
