The Question Before You Plead Guilty

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-07-29

Almost every criminal case in this country ends the same way: a guilty plea, not a trial. Most people plead before they find out what else was on the table, and by the time anyone mentions the alternatives, the case is closed. This article goes with our video on the same problem: read on for what a 60-second clip can't cover.

Why people plead so fast

None of this comes down to people being careless. Losing income while a case drags on for months is a real cost. So is the fear that fighting a charge and losing means a harsher sentence than taking the deal now. And a public defender juggling dozens of cases at once can't give every one of them the time it deserves. That's just the math of the caseload, not a knock on the attorney. Put all of that together and pleading quickly starts to look like the only reasonable move.

The trouble is nobody stops to check whether fast and good actually mean the same thing here. Usually they don't. And the gap between them comes down to one question that nobody is required to ask on your behalf.

The one thing worth asking first

In New York, before entering any plea, it's worth asking directly: is there a diversion program or restorative justice option for this case? These can resolve things without a conviction ever landing on the record, through community service, treatment, or a facilitated conversation with whoever was harmed.

Nobody has to bring this up: not the DA, not the court, and not always the defense attorney, even a decent one drowning in cases. Which is exactly why it falls on you: you can't negotiate for something nobody told you existed.

What this looks like in practice

If a plea hasn't been entered yet, this is the moment to ask, at arraignment, or as soon as a lawyer is assigned. Wait too long and that window closes. If someone was just arrested and hasn't gotten this far yet, start with what you're facing in the first 24 hours.

This isn't a one-off problem, either. It ties back to two bigger gaps in how the system treats people after an arrest, which we cover in The Two Failures Nobody Warns You About.

And once a case has gone through the system, the work isn't over at sentencing. Groups like GOSO (Getting Out and Staying Out), based in East Harlem, spend every day helping young men 16 to 24 rebuild after time in NYC jails or New York State prisons, through school, jobs, and real support instead of just watching a calendar. Fewer than 9% of their participants end up back inside, against a national average of 54% for the same age group. That's the kind of difference this work makes.
