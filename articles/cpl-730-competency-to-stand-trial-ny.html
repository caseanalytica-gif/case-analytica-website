Found Unfit to Stand Trial in New York? What CPL Article 730 Actually Allows

A case doesn't disappear because a defendant is found unfit to stand trial. It pauses. And for a lot of families, that pause turns into more time in state custody than the underlying charge would have carried if the person had simply been convicted and sentenced.

Nobody explains that part up front. Families hear "he's getting evaluated" or "she's not fit right now" and assume that's a good outcome, something softer than prosecution. Sometimes it is. But it's also a specific legal process with its own clock, its own custody rules, and its own limits, and almost nobody outside the courtroom knows where those limits actually sit.

What "unfit to proceed" actually means

New York defines it under CPL § 730.10: an "incapacitated person" is a defendant who, as a result of mental disease or defect, lacks the capacity to understand the proceedings against them or to assist in their own defense. That's a narrow legal standard, not a general question of mental illness. A defendant can have a documented psychiatric diagnosis and still be found fit to proceed, if they can understand the charges and work with their attorney.

Either side, the defense or the district attorney, or the court itself, can raise the question of fitness. Under CPL § 730.30, once it's raised, the court must issue an order of examination if it has reasonable grounds to believe the defendant may be incapacitated.

The exam itself has a clock, and it's the first one that matters

Under CPL § 730.20, once the order is issued, two qualified psychiatric examiners are designated to evaluate the defendant. If they don't agree on the finding, a third examiner is brought in and the court must hold a hearing. If hospital confinement is needed to complete the exam, it can run up to 30 days, extendable once for another 30, before the reports even reach the court.

This is not a punishment and not a conviction

A finding of unfitness carries no guilt determination. But the custody that can follow it is real, and it runs on its own separate legal track from anything resembling a sentence for the underlying charge.

How long the case can actually stay paused

The limits differ depending on what's charged. On a felony complaint that hasn't reached indictment, CPL § 730.40 caps a temporary order of observation at 90 days; once that period runs and the defendant is still in the commissioner's custody, the local court proceedings terminate and the felony complaint must be dismissed.

Once a case reaches indictment, CPL § 730.50 sets a harder ceiling: the total time a person can be held in the commissioner's custody under this article cannot exceed two-thirds of the maximum authorized prison term for the highest-class felony charged. A first retention order can run up to one year, with later orders extending it up to two years at a time, but that two-thirds figure is the outer wall. Once it's reached, the criminal proceedings terminate and the court must dismiss the indictment.

Do the math on the actual charge

Two-thirds of a maximum sentence is not a small number for a mid-level or serious felony. Someone facing a charge that carries a shorter realistic sentence on a plea, diversion, or an actual conviction can end up held under this article for longer than any of those outcomes would have taken. That comparison is worth making with an attorney directly, not assuming the hospital route is automatically the shorter path.

What happens if fitness is restored, or never is

If treatment restores the defendant to fitness within the custody window, the case resumes where it left off. If it doesn't, and the statutory ceiling is reached, the underlying criminal case ends. That doesn't mean the person walks free automatically; separate civil commitment proceedings under the Mental Hygiene Law can still apply if a treating facility believes continued care is necessary, a process explained in the procedural rules the courts themselves publish for handling Article 730 cases. That's a different legal track from the criminal case, with its own separate standards and its own separate hearing rights.

Where the information gap actually costs people

The defense has real rights inside this process that families rarely hear about: the right to have a retained psychiatrist or psychologist present at the exam, the right to receive copies of the examination reports, and the right to demand a hearing if the finding is contested. None of that gets offered automatically. It has to be asked for, at the right moment, by someone who knows to ask. A public defender juggling a heavy caseload can raise a 730 exam correctly and still never walk a family through what the custody clock actually means for that specific charge; see why your public defender might be carrying 100+ cases for what that caseload pressure actually looks like in practice.

The exact question to ask

If a 730 exam is being requested for you or someone you love, ask directly: "What happens to the bail or custody status while this is pending, and given the top charge here, what's the actual maximum length this custody could run under the statute?" Get the answer in writing where possible, and ask again at every retention order, since the numbers change as the case moves.

Custody surprises are not unique to this process. A low bail number doesn't guarantee a fast release either; see what a bail source hearing actually does for another way a case can hold someone in custody far longer than the paperwork on its face suggests.

Know what the statute actually allows before agreeing to anything or assuming the process will move quickly. That's the same principle behind every diversion and restorative justice program covered on this site: sit with the actual rule, not the version someone summarized for you in a hallway, before a decision gets made that's hard to undo.
