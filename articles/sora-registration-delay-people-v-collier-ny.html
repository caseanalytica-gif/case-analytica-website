New York Forgot to Register Him as a Sex Offender for Five Years. The Court of Appeals Just Ruled on What That Cost

In 2009, Roderick Collier pleaded guilty to sexual misconduct, a misdemeanor, and served eight months of a one-year sentence. Under New York's Sex Offender Registration Act, that plea meant he owed the state a risk-level classification and a registration obligation the moment he walked out. Nobody told him. No court certified him as a sex offender. No hearing was scheduled. For nearly five years, New York simply forgot Roderick Collier existed under SORA, and he built a life: he got hired as a "Youth Specialist/Cook" at a nonprofit running a non-secure placement program for at-risk kids, he stayed a present father to his sons, he took online classes, he leased an apartment, he rebuilt his credit. His only contact with the legal system in those five years was a traffic ticket.

Then in November 2014, the state's own sex offender registry flagged the gap, and the machinery Collier had never been put through finally started. The Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders recommended a level two, moderate-risk classification. Collier fought it, arguing the five-year delay itself violated his due process rights. On January 8, 2026, New York's Court of Appeals ruled against that argument and upheld his classification, but the opinion, and a striking concurrence from the state's Chief Judge, said something bigger than one man's case: government delay in sex offender registration isn't just a procedural footnote. Left unchecked, it can be the very thing that destabilizes the job and the home that keep a person from reoffending in the first place.

How SORA is actually supposed to work

New York's Sex Offender Registration Act, codified at Correction Law Article 6-C, sorts people convicted of qualifying sex offenses into three tiers: level one, "low" risk of repeat offense; level two, "moderate"; level three, "high." The tier isn't a judge's gut call. The state's Board of Examiners of Sex Offenders completes a Risk Assessment Instrument, assigning points across factors like criminal history, the victim's age, and whether the offense involved violence, and that point total presumptively sets the level. A sentencing court then has to independently review the evidence and either accept the Board's recommendation or depart from it.

The statute builds in specific deadlines. Under Correction Law § 168-n, the sentencing court must hold its risk-level hearing no later than 30 calendar days before the offender's discharge, parole, or release. The Board is supposed to make its recommendation to the court within 60 days before that same release date. And under Correction Law § 168-d, the original sentencing court is supposed to certify the person as a sex offender and advise them of their registration duties at the time of the plea itself.

None of that happened for Collier. And under the statute, a missed deadline doesn't cancel the obligation. Correction Law § 168-l(8) says a failure by the Board to act, or by a court to render a timely determination, "shall not affect the obligation of the sex offender to register." The Court of Appeals has already held, in an earlier case, that a court's failure to even warn a defendant about SORA registration at the time of a guilty plea doesn't undo the plea's validity. The system is built so that the person's duty to register survives the government's own mistakes. What was actually being litigated in Collier's case was narrower: given that his registration duty survived the delay regardless, did the five-year gap itself violate his constitutional rights.

Why the court said no, and why that answer cuts both ways

Writing for the majority, Judge Garcia held that a due process violation from delayed SORA proceedings requires a defendant to show the delay actually prejudiced his ability to present his case, not just that years passed. Collier couldn't make that showing, because the opposite happened: the SORA court, weighing his nearly five years of stable, law-abiding, unsupervised life, declined to assess him 15 points under the risk factor for "release without supervision." That single adjustment dropped him from a presumptive level two to level one, the least restrictive tier, with a 20-year registration period the court backdated, a legal move called ordering it "nunc pro tunc," meaning retroactive, to his original 2009 release date rather than his actual 2015 registration date. In effect, his registration clock is already running out roughly six years earlier than it would if it had started on time.

The court was explicit that this outcome doesn't generalize. Collier "materially benefitted" from the delay because of how his specific years played out. A different person, delayed just as long but without a stable job or a clean record to show for it, would have no comparable argument. The ruling doesn't require the state to move faster. It just says that this delay, for this person, with this result, didn't shock the conscience enough to be unconstitutional.

That's where Chief Judge Rowan Wilson's concurrence goes further than the majority needed to. He agreed the outcome was correct on these facts, but wrote separately to flag what a longer delay, or a harsher result, could do. Sketching a hypothetical of a 20-year delay ending in a level three designation instead of a favorable one, Wilson wrote that forcing someone to "give up his home and his job" that late, after decades spent rebuilding a life, wouldn't serve public safety, it would undermine it. He cited academic research directly: a statistical analysis, cited in a Sixth Circuit ruling on Michigan's registry, found that laws like SORA "actually increase the risk of recidivism, probably because they exacerbate risk factors for recidivism by making it hard for registrants to get and keep a job, find housing, and reintegrate into their communities." That is the same link Case Analytica keeps returning to in its coverage of New York's Clean Slate Act: stable work and stable housing are what actually keep someone out of the system, and a state process that disrupts either one for no public-safety reason tends to produce more of the harm it claims to prevent, not less.

What a level two or three designation actually restricts

The stakes in Collier's case were concrete, not abstract. His level one designation requires him to register on a confidential list for 20 years, appear to be photographed every three years, and report any address change within 10 days. His information isn't in the public online directory, though it's available by phone request, and he can ask a court to end his registration in 2029. Had he been designated level two or three instead, the opinion notes he would face registration for life, a place in the searchable public internet registry, and a requirement to report his employer's name and address for public posting, on top of specific statutory bars: New York law blocks anyone on the registry from operating an ice cream truck, working at a daycare, or working at a children's day or overnight camp. A level three offender, or anyone convicted of an offense against a victim under 18, also falls under separate residency restrictions barring them from living within 1,000 feet of school grounds.

Those are exactly the kind of barriers Case Analytica has written about in the context of Correction Law Article 23-A's employment protections and the Clean Slate Act's record-sealing timeline: a conviction doesn't just carry a sentence, it carries a second set of barriers to work and housing that can outlast the sentence by decades. SORA is a different statute with a different purpose, registering people based on an assessed risk of reoffending rather than sealing records automatically. But the mechanism by which delay does damage is the same one Case Analytica has documented in Clean Slate's implementation gap: every year a status determination sits unresolved is a year someone can't fully plan a job search, a lease application, or a stable life around where they actually stand.

General information, not legal advice

SORA risk-level classification and its due process protections involve specific deadlines, specific evidentiary standards, and a hearing right. How any of that applies to a real case depends on facts only a licensed attorney reviewing the record can evaluate.
