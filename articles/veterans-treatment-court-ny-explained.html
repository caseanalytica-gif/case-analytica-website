What Is Veterans Treatment Court in New York? How the 2021 Transfer Law Made It Statewide

Ask a veteran facing a criminal charge in New York whether anyone raised Veterans Treatment Court before a plea got discussed, and too often the answer is no. Not because the program doesn't exist. Because it depends on someone in the room, a defense lawyer, a judge, a screener, actually knowing to ask whether the person standing there served. If nobody asks, nobody finds out, and a program built specifically to keep veterans out of prison never enters the conversation at all.

What Veterans Treatment Court actually is

New York created the nation's first Veterans Treatment Court in Buffalo City Court in 2008, the model of Judge Robert Russell. It has since spread as a specific alternative track for justice-involved veterans, run by a dedicated judge working alongside an interdisciplinary team: prosecutors, defense counsel, probation, treatment providers, volunteer veteran peer mentors, and representatives from the New York State Division of Veterans' Services and the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. The New York State Unified Court System's own description is direct about who qualifies: the court accepts veterans with all characters of military discharge, not just an honorable one. The premise is that criminal behavior tied to combat-related mental health conditions or substance use disorder is better addressed with judicial supervision and treatment than with a straight prosecution, and the federal Bureau of Justice Assistance, which has funded the model since 2013, describes it the same way.

Only 26 of New York's 62 counties actually have one

As of the most recent count, there are 35 Veterans Treatment Courts operating in 26 New York counties, according to a 2026 New York Health Foundation analysis of state court data. Coverage is uneven: the Mohawk Valley region has no county with a VTC at all, and the North Country and Central New York regions each have only one. Where you get arrested still, in practice, affects whether this option exists in the building.

The 2021 law that was supposed to fix that

In 2021, New York passed Criminal Procedure Law § 230.21, part of Chapter 91 of the Laws of 2021, allowing a case to be removed to a Veterans Treatment Court in an adjoining county when the county where charges were filed doesn't have one. It requires a motion from the defendant, and it requires the consent of both district attorneys, the one in the county where the case sits and the one in the county receiving it. The statute carves out one hard exclusion: it cannot be used to move a family offense charge into a Veterans Treatment Court when the accused and the alleged victim are members of the same family or household, as defined under CPL 530.11. On paper, this made VTC access statewide instead of dependent on geography.

In practice, the same 2026 NYHealth Foundation analysis found the transfer process is barely used: only 13 people were transferred under this law across all of 2024 and 2025 combined, and the report attributes the slow uptake in part to limited awareness of the transfer process among the lawyers and judges who would actually have to invoke it. A statute that exists but that the people running your case don't know how to use might as well not exist, for you.

What's actually at stake if nobody raises it

The same analysis, drawing on a peer-reviewed national study of VTC outcomes, found that about one in seven veterans who participate in a VTC (14.0 percent) go on to reoffend, compared with nearly half of civilians (46.0 percent) who reoffend within one year of release from incarceration. Between 2013 and 2025, three quarters of VTC program closures in New York (75.1 percent) were successful completions. DUI and DWI charges account for more than a third of all VTC cases statewide, which means this option is relevant to far more veterans than the ones facing the most severe charges. None of that helps a specific person if the option is never mentioned to them before a plea is already on the table.

The exact question to ask

Ask directly, and ask before any plea discussion goes further: "Have I been screened for veteran status and eligibility for Veterans Treatment Court, and if this county doesn't have one, has anyone looked at whether my case can be transferred to one under CPL 230.21?" Bring your discharge paperwork (DD-214) to that conversation. Eligibility and available treatment tracks vary by county, and someone has to actually check.

Veterans Treatment Court is one specific diversion track among several New York doesn't advertise well. See what judicial diversion under CPL Article 216 allows for an eligible felony drug case, which runs on a similar logic: treatment supervised by a judge instead of straight prosecution, available only if someone raises it.
