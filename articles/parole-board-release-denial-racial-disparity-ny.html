New York's Parole Board Denies Release by Race, Not Just by Risk

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-02

Picture two people walking into the same parole interview room in the same year, with the same risk score, the same clean disciplinary record inside, and the same release plan on paper: a job lined up, a place to live, a family ready to receive them. One is white. One is Black or Latino. According to a 2024 report from NYU's Center on Race, Inequality and the Law, the second person is significantly less likely to walk out with a release date.

The report, titled "Freedom Delayed, Justice Denied," found that between 2022 and 2024, New York's Board of Parole was about 32 percent less likely to release a person of color than a white person. That's not a lifetime average. That's the last three years, under the current governor's appointees. And it's worse than it used to be: the same researchers found the disparity is 71 percent wider than it was over the six years before Governor Hochul took office. Tolu Lawal, a staff attorney at the center who worked on the report, put a number on what that gap actually means in reporting from Spectrum News: "It shows that as many as 1,500 Black and brown folks would be home with their families today if there were not racial disparities in the parole release."

The Board's own numbers don't support "risk" as the explanation

This isn't new. In December 2021, the Vera Institute of Justice published "Toward a Fairer Parole Process," a review of 168 parole interview transcripts belonging to people serving life sentences. Every single case in the sample ended in denial. Most of those denials happened despite the person carrying a low COMPAS score, the state's own tool for estimating the odds someone reoffends. Most of the people denied had also done the things a release plan is supposed to require: completed prison education or vocational programs, held a job inside, and shown up with a concrete plan for housing and employment on the outside, backed by letters from family, corrections staff, and reentry organizations. Vera's own summary of the pattern: commissioners justified the large majority of these denials by pointing only to the original crime, an "immutable fact," in the researchers' words, that the commissioners already knew before the interview even started.

That's the part worth sitting with. A release plan with a job and an apartment attached to it is exactly the kind of stability this site keeps coming back to: the single biggest factor in whether someone stays out of the system once they're out. And under New York's own parole statute, Executive Law 259-i, "post release plans" are one of the factors the Board is required to consider. DOCCS confirmed this directly when it responded to the 2024 NYU report, describing the statutory factors the Board weighs, including "an incarcerated individual's criminal history, institutional adjustment and accomplishments, potential to successfully reintegrate into the community, such as post release plans, and perceived risk to public safety." The factor is in the law. The research says it frequently doesn't change the outcome.

The number, from the state's own calendar

New York's statewide parole release rate has swung sharply in recent years: roughly 40 percent in 2019, 36 percent in 2021, 33 percent in 2022, back up to 38 percent in the first half of 2023, and 46 percent in a sample month, May 2025. The state's own Parole Board Interview Calendar is the public source these figures come from, tracked over time by the Vera Institute's New York parole data visualization.

Two bills, still stuck in committee

Two pieces of legislation currently sitting in the state Senate would change how much discretion the Board has. The Fair and Timely Parole Act, S.159, would flip the presumption: instead of the Board needing a reason to release someone, it would need to show "a current and unreasonable risk" that can't be managed through supervision before it can deny release. The Elder Parole Act, S.454, would guarantee a parole interview to anyone 55 or older who has already served at least 15 years, something that isn't automatically guaranteed today regardless of age or time served. Both bills have been introduced in prior sessions and haven't passed. State Sen. Joseph Griffo, quoted in the same Spectrum News report, argued the current standards already provide balance and that changing them risks public safety; Jose Saldana, director of Release Aging People in Prison and someone who served 38 years before his own release, told the outlet the racial pattern reflects "the nature of the prison system because it's founded on the legacy of racism."

A separate but related problem: reincarceration after release

Getting past the Board is only step one. New York also has a documented history of sending people already on parole back to custody for technical violations, meaning no new arrest, no new crime, just something like a missed curfew or a missed check-in. The Less Is More Act, which went into full effect March 1, 2022, was built specifically to stop this: it eliminated automatic jail time for most technical violations, capped how long someone can be held for repeated ones, and guaranteed a right to counsel and faster hearings throughout the violation process. Whatever job and apartment someone secured after release doesn't survive a stretch back in custody over a missed appointment. That's the same throughline running underneath this entire piece: every point where New York's system defaults toward incarceration instead of supervision is a point where an already-fragile foothold in a job or a lease gets knocked loose. For the mechanics of how a violation and revocation hearing actually works today, see our explainer on what happens if you violate parole under Executive Law 259-i.

If you're preparing for a hearing

None of this means a release plan doesn't matter. It means it isn't self-executing. See Case Analytica's guide to preparing for a New York parole hearing for how to build a release plan that holds up and what to say in the room.

The Board's own public defense of its record is that it follows the statute and weighs public safety alongside reintegration potential. The data says that in practice, two people can bring the Board the identical case for reintegration, jobs and housing included, and get different answers depending on race. That gap isn't an abstraction. It's measured in people who stay locked up instead of going to the job and the apartment they already lined up.
