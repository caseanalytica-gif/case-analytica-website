The Clean Slate Act Got Watered Down Before It Passed. Here's What That's Costing New Yorkers Still Waiting

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-02

Every year someone waits on a record that isn't sealed yet is a year they're filling out job applications and rental applications with a conviction attached to their name. That's not a side effect of the Clean Slate Act's timeline. It's the whole point of why the timeline matters, and it's exactly what got extended before the bill ever became law.

New York's Clean Slate Act sounds like it happened in one clean legislative motion: bill passes, governor signs, records seal. It didn't work that way. The version Governor Hochul signed on November 16, 2023 is not the version Senator Zellnor Myrie first introduced. It's a compromise, and the compromise made the wait longer for the people it was written to help.

What actually changed before the vote

According to City & State's reporting from June 6, 2023, the bill that finally moved had two substantive changes from the versions that came before it. First, the felony waiting period grew from seven years after release to eight. Second, the exclusion list widened: earlier drafts only excluded sex offenses, but the final language also excludes most Class A felonies, carving out an exception for Class A drug offenses, which can still be sealed. The misdemeanor wait, three years, didn't change.

Assembly Member Catalina Cruz, the bill's Assembly sponsor, told City & State the final language represented a three-way deal between the governor and both legislative chambers, not just a deal between the Senate and Assembly. The Clean Slate Coalition, the advocacy coalition that had pushed the bill for years, said publicly that the changes were the result of "pressure from the governor" that would bar "thousands" of people from the law's benefits. They still backed the bill's passage, because a compromise that helps most people beat no bill at all.

No last-minute chapter amendment. The compromise was baked in before the vote.

It's worth being precise about how this happened, because it's not the story people assume. When Hochul signed the bill in Brooklyn that November, she confirmed there was no chapter amendment: she signed exactly what passed the Legislature in June, unchanged. The negotiation that lengthened the felony wait and widened the exclusions happened earlier in the session, in the room where the bill's actual text got written, before it ever reached a floor vote. The watering down isn't a footnote added after the fact. It's built into the law itself.

One piece of that earlier negotiation actually went the other way. During 2022 budget talks, Hochul had proposed that the waiting period start only after a person's full sentence and the entire parole or probation period ended, meaning someone sentenced to 10 years plus 5 years of probation would wait 15 years before the sealing clock even started. Lawmakers held their ground on that point, and the final bill keeps the clock starting at release from incarceration, not at the end of supervision. So this isn't a story where every disputed point moved in one direction. It's a story where the number that decided how long millions of people would keep carrying a record moved the wrong way, and the number that would have made things far worse got fixed early enough that most people never had to think about it.

The part that isn't finished yet

Longer waits are only half of the gap between what Clean Slate promised and what it's delivered so far. The law makes roughly 5 million records eligible for sealing, but courts and state agencies have until November 16, 2027, three full years after the law took effect, to actually get through them. As of March 2025, according to Spectrum News reporting on a state budget hearing, the Division of Criminal Justice Services confirmed it had not yet received a single record from the Office of Court Administration to actually seal. State agencies told lawmakers they didn't need extra funding to hit the deadline. Nobody asked what that meant for the people whose eligible records were still sitting open in the meantime.

See Case Analytica's full breakdown of the Clean Slate Act's waiting periods and exclusions for the mechanics of who qualifies and when. This piece is about something different: why the length of that wait was never just a technical detail.

Why the wait itself is the harm

Here's the connection that gets lost in coverage of this law: a sealed record and a job aren't two separate outcomes. They're the same outcome. People with a criminal record face an unemployment rate over 27 percent, according to Prison Policy Initiative's analysis of formerly incarcerated people's labor market outcomes, several times the rate for the general population, and that unemployment is worst in the first two years after release, which is exactly when someone is trying hardest to stabilize. Senator Myrie made the same point directly in his statement the day the bill was signed: Clean Slate "allows people to stabilize themselves with gainful employment, housing and education," and "reduces recidivism by offering those with conviction histories a pathway out of the cycle of crime." Even Hochul said it herself at the signing: "When people have steady work, they're less likely to commit crimes and less likely to be homeless."

Everyone who negotiated this bill agrees on the mechanism. A record blocks a job. A blocked job delays stability. Delayed stability is where recidivism actually comes from, not from some abstract failure of willpower. That agreement is exactly why the length of the wait matters as much as it does. Extending the felony clock from seven years to eight isn't a rounding error. It's an extra year, for millions of people, of exactly the instability that the bill's own sponsors say drives people back into the system. Widening the exclusion list to cover most Class A felonies means a whole category of people convicted decades ago, now well past any reasonable definition of risk, still can't get this record off their back at all.

What to actually ask about your own record

If a felony conviction is approaching the eight-year mark from release, don't wait passively for a letter or a background check to change. Ask directly whether the specific record is on the excluded list (most Class A felonies and sex offenses are not eligible under any version of Clean Slate), and ask whether the state has processed it yet, since as of the most recent public reporting, the backlog was still being worked through years into the three-year implementation window. If a record doesn't qualify or the wait hasn't run yet, a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities removes the same employment and licensing barriers without waiting on Clean Slate's clock at all.

The throughline

This site keeps coming back to one point because it keeps holding up under scrutiny: the faster someone can get a real job and stable housing after release, the less likely they are to end up back in the system. Every part of the criminal legal system that slows that down, whether it's a sealing law with a longer wait than advocates asked for, a parole system that leans toward reincarceration over technical violations, or an implementation window that stretches three years past the law's own effective date, isn't a neutral delay. It's a delay with a measurable cost, paid by people who did everything the law asked of them and are still waiting for the record to catch up.

A record that isn't sealed yet doesn't have to keep costing someone a rental application either. See the Fair Chance for Housing Act explained for what NYC landlords can and can't consider from an unsealed record. And if the conviction involves old marijuana charges specifically, check whether it was already automatically expunged under the MRTA before assuming Clean Slate's longer timeline even applies.
