New York's Parole Board Is 33% Less Likely to Release a Person of Color: The Data Behind the Gap

By Dean Mustaphalli·2026-09-02

Jose Saldaña sat in front of New York's Board of Parole four times before he made it out. Thirty-eight years, total, before release in 2018. He's since become a director at the Releasing Aging People in Prison campaign, and he's described what those hearings actually looked like from inside the waiting room: "You've got maybe 20 people in there, sometimes more, waiting to see the New York state parole commissioners. We're looking around at each other, and everybody basically has the same thought in their head. They look at the around three to five white guys and we all know that they got a better chance than us." That's not a feeling. According to a New York University report, it's a documented pattern.

What the data actually shows

New York's Board of Parole releases people of color at a rate roughly a third lower than white applicants, according to a report from NYU School of Law's Center on Race, Inequality, and the Law, based on parole board data obtained from the Vera Institute of Justice. The report's original release, covering 2022 through June 2024, found the Board was 32.28 percent less likely to release a person of color than a white person in that window, up from an 18.80 percent gap during 2016 through 2021, a 71.65 percent worsening the report says "coincides with Governor Hochul's time in office." An updated version of the analysis, extending the window through March 2025, put the figure at 33 percent, according to WAER, part of the New York Public News Network. An earlier snapshot of the same data, covering through mid-2024, put the release rate for non-white applicants at 32.55 percent under Hochul's administration, compared to 45.07 percent for white applicants, reported by the New York Amsterdam News.

The scale of that gap, translated into people, is not small. The report's original release estimates that if people of color had been released at the same rate as white applicants just from 2022 through June 2024, there would have been over 1,300 more grants of parole in that window alone, and over 3,650 more going back to 2016. The Amsterdam News reported a similar figure, 3,787 more people, using the mid-2024 snapshot of the same underlying data. "There's no rational explanation for that disparity," Jason Williamson, the center's executive director and the report's co-author, told WAER in coverage of the report's May 2025 update.

What the state says in response

In response to the report, the Department of Corrections and Community Supervision said the Board "cannot and do not solely consider the race of an individual when rendering their decisions," and that members follow statutory factors including victim statements, criminal history, institutional accomplishments, and "perceived risk to public safety." Governor Hochul's office has pointed out that the Board is an independent body, not one she directly controls case by case, though its 16 members are gubernatorial nominees confirmed by the state Senate, and as of the report's release, all but four members were sitting on expired terms.

Why release decisions are a different problem than violations

Case Analytica has already covered what happens when someone already on parole is accused of violating it, a process governed by Executive Law 259-i with its own hearings and deadlines. This is a different stage entirely. Before anyone can violate parole, they first have to be granted it, and that initial release decision is where this data shows the disparity concentrated. Reverend Al Sharpton called the pattern "a moral stain on our great state" in a statement to the Amsterdam News, and said thousands of Black and Brown people who are currently incarcerated "would be home with their families if people of color were released at the same rates as white people."

Two bills currently before the Legislature target this stage directly. The Fair and Timely Parole bill would require the Board to weigh a person's rehabilitation and readiness for reentry, rather than leaning on the original offense itself, and would remove a standard that lets the Board deny release if it decides parole would "undermine respect" for the law, a phrase advocates and the report's authors describe as vague enough to justify almost any denial. The Elder Parole bill would guarantee a hearing for anyone 55 or older who has served at least 15 years, regardless of their minimum sentence. Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg, Bronx DA Darcel Clark, and Brooklyn DA Eric Gonzalez have all endorsed both bills, alongside Williamson and Sharpton.

The throughline: every extra year inside is a year of stability lost outside

This site keeps returning to one argument because the evidence keeps supporting it: what actually keeps someone from ending up back in the system is whether they can get a real job and stable housing after release, and how fast. A parole denial doesn't pause that clock, it resets it. Every additional year someone spends inside on a case where a white applicant with a comparable record and comparable institutional record would likely have been released is a year further from the job and the housing that would have made re-offending less likely, not more. Saldaña was denied four times before his release. Multiply that by the thousands of additional grants of release the NYU report estimates people of color were denied at rates that don't match their white counterparts, and this isn't a story about individual bad hearings. It's a structural delay in exactly the transition this site's throughline is built around, at a scale large enough to show up in the state's own data.
