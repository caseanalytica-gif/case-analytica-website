Do Young Prosecutors Practice on Misdemeanor Cases? They Do, and the Person Charged Pays

By Case Analytica·2026-10-30

Reflects law as of October 30, 2026

A New York public defender says young prosecutors take low-level misdemeanors all the way to trial to get experience, and that the person charged pays for it. Case Analytica set out to test that claim against the public record. It holds up. District attorney offices build misdemeanor trials into how they train new prosecutors, a misdemeanor trial is rare enough that the office had to decline every exit before it happened, and the cost of that choice lands on the person charged, win or lose. Prosecutors are practicing on people's lives.

A public defender describes it, in her words. Excerpt from @carolineisocool on TikTok (bio: "NY Public Defender"), edited by Case Analytica. Captions are auto-transcribed.

Why Case Analytica is studying this

Case Analytica studies what the process does to people before any verdict, because in New York City almost all misdemeanors end before one. The same pull shows up in why innocent people plead guilty and in the bet every plea deal makes.

The claim

The clip is from a public defender on TikTok. She says young prosecutors see a chance to practice and take cases to trial. Her example is "a really ridiculous low-level misdemeanor case" tried to get experience "at the expense of a person." If the prosecutor loses, she says, it is only experience, and nothing substantial happens to them. Everything lands on the client. By the time a case reaches trial, she says, the client "has already lost so much, even if they win."

Test one: do offices put new prosecutors on misdemeanor trials?

Yes, and they say so. The Queens District Attorney's fall 2023 entry-level posting says new assistant district attorneys get "immediate courtroom experience, including arraignments, calendar calls, as well as misdemeanor hearings and trials."

It is not only New York. A county attorney in Ector County, Texas, wrote in the Texas District and County Attorneys Association's journal in 2016 that misdemeanor prosecutors "have little experience and take these jobs to gain the expertise they need" to become felony prosecutors.

The training is the design.

The low-level case is where a new prosecutor gets a first trial.

Test two: is a misdemeanor trial the exception?

Yes. In New York City in 2019, 0.3 percent of disposed misdemeanors were resolved at trial. The Center for Court Innovation counted 205 misdemeanor jury trials that year, out of roughly 129,000 misdemeanor arraignments, and said almost half of the few trials were before a judge, not a jury. Case Analytica uses 2019 only. The report's 2020 numbers come from the first pandemic year, when the courts were disrupted.

When almost every misdemeanor ends before a trial, the few that go to one passed every exit first. The office can dismiss the case, resolve it at arraignment or offer a plea, and a defendant can also refuse the offer, so not every trial is the office's pick. But the office controls the dismissal and the offer. In an office that sends new prosecutors to misdemeanor trials, a case that stays on the trial track is a case that gives someone practice.

Test three: does the person charged pay?

Yes, and the cost starts long before a verdict. The Center for Court Innovation describes misdemeanor processing in the city as arrest, booking, up to 24 hours in holding cells, and daylong waits for court appearances that last a few minutes. Its authors write that people who avoid a conviction still bear "process is punishment" effects.

A conviction adds more. The Brennan Center found in 2020 that people convicted of a misdemeanor see annual earnings fall by an average of 16 percent, using national survey data that matched them to similar people without convictions.

The wait is part of the cost. A 2013 New York Times opinion piece by former Bronx public defender David Feige, citing 2011 court figures, put the average at over 400 days to reach a jury trial and verdict outside the Bronx, and nearly 600 in Brooklyn. Those figures are old. Current wait times are a number to ask for. For more on the clock, see CPL 30.30 speedy trial rules.

For the prosecutor, a loss is experience. For the person charged, it is a pending case.

For the person charged, a trial is a pending case that, by the 2011 figures, could run past 400 days, and a possible conviction that the Brennan data ties to lower earnings.

What the record cannot show

No public data tells why a particular misdemeanor was tried. Offices do not log "tried for practice." That part of the claim comes from the person who sits across from the prosecutor, and she says it happens. The record around her account fits it: the training is built in, the trials are rare, the cost is documented.

The record does not count how often it happens. That count is the missing number, and the offices hold it.

General information, not legal advice

What happens in any one case depends on the charge, the court and the record. A defense attorney with the charging papers can say what applies.

The exact question to ask

Ask your lawyer early, before the first trial date: "Is the office offering anything short of trial on this case, and if I turn it down, how soon could it be tried and who would be trying it?" A general answer that the case is probably fine is not an answer. You are asking for an offer, a timeline and a name.
