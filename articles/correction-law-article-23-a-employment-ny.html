Can a New York Employer Reject You Because of a Conviction? What Correction Law Article 23-A Actually Requires

A conviction on a background check is not, by itself, a legal reason to turn someone away from a job in New York. Almost nobody hears that. Most employers don't volunteer it, and most job seekers assume a criminal record is simply the end of the conversation, so they never push back on a denial that the law never actually allowed.

What Article 23-A actually says

New York Correction Law Article 23-A governs the licensure and employment of people previously convicted of a crime, and it applies broadly: to any public agency, and to any private employer with ten or more employees, per the definitions at Correction Law § 750. Under § 752, an employer cannot deny or act adversely on an application or existing job because of a prior conviction unless one of two things is true: there's a "direct relationship" between the conviction and the specific job's duties, or hiring or continuing to employ the person would create an unreasonable risk to property or to the safety of specific individuals or the public. Absent one of those two showings, the conviction alone is not a legal basis to say no.

This is not a request for leniency

Article 23-A isn't asking an employer to overlook a record out of sympathy. It's requiring them to actually connect the conviction to the job before it can be used against you. That's a real evidentiary requirement, not a courtesy.

The factors an employer is legally required to weigh

Correction Law § 753 lists what a public agency or private employer has to consider before relying on § 752: the state's own stated public policy favoring employment of people with records, the specific duties of the job, whether the offense actually bears on the ability to perform those duties, how much time has passed since the offense, the person's age at the time it happened, how serious the offense was, and any evidence the applicant offers of rehabilitation or good conduct. The employer also has to weigh its own legitimate interest in protecting people and property, but that has to be a real, articulable interest tied to the specific job, not a blanket assumption.

One factor carries real legal weight on its own. Under § 753(2), a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities or a Certificate of Good Conduct creates a presumption of rehabilitation that the employer must factor in. See how to get a Certificate of Relief from Disabilities for what that certificate actually requires and why it has to be requested, since it doesn't happen automatically.

What NYC added on top of the state law

New York City's Fair Chance Act builds on Article 23-A rather than replacing it. Under the process the NYC Commission on Human Rights explains for employers, most NYC employers cannot ask about criminal history at all until after a conditional offer of employment is made, and job postings can't include phrases like "no felonies" or "clean record required." Once a conditional offer is extended and a record surfaces, the employer must complete a written Article 23-A analysis, share it along with any background check used, and hold the job open for at least five business days so the applicant can respond before any final decision is made. NYC's Human Rights Law reaches employers with four or more employees, wider than the ten-employee threshold in the state statute itself.

The written statement most people never ask for

Under Correction Law § 754, anyone denied a license or a job because of a prior conviction can request a written statement of the reasons, and the employer has to provide it within thirty days. Most people who get turned down never ask for this. It's the document that tells you whether the employer actually ran the required analysis, or just said no and moved on.

Why this connects to what happens earlier in a case

The record an employer eventually sees depends heavily on what happened at sentencing and afterward. New York's Clean Slate Act automatically seals many eligible convictions after a set waiting period, which removes them from most background checks entirely rather than requiring an Article 23-A fight down the line. The two protections work at different points: sealing keeps a record from surfacing in the first place, while Article 23-A governs what happens when a conviction does surface and an employer has to justify using it against you.

The exact question to ask

If you're turned down after a conditional offer, ask directly: "Can I get the written statement of reasons under Correction Law 754, and did you factor in a certificate of relief or certificate of good conduct if I have one?" That question alone tells you whether the process was followed or skipped.

A conviction doesn't have to be the end of the employment story in New York. It only stops being the end once someone actually knows this law exists and is willing to ask the employer to show their work.
