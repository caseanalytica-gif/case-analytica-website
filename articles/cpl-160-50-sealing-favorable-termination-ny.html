Your Case Was Dismissed. Was Your Arrest Record Sealed? CPL 160.50 Explained

A case gets dismissed, or ends in an acquittal, and most people walk out of the courthouse assuming that's the end of it. Nobody hands them a piece of paper explaining what happens next to the arrest itself: the fingerprints, the mugshot, the paperwork sitting with the police department and the district attorney's office. New York actually has an answer, and it happens automatically. Almost nobody is ever told that, either.

What CPL 160.50 actually covers

Under Criminal Procedure Law § 160.50, a case that ends in the defendant's favor gets sealed, and it happens without anyone having to file a motion or ask a judge for it. New York's own courts describe a "good result" or "favorable disposition" broadly: an acquittal at trial, a grand jury declining to indict, a dismissal of the accusatory instrument, a dismissal in the interest of justice, a case dismissed after an ACD period runs clean, a case dismissed after successful completion of a diversion or treatment program, a prosecutor declining to move forward, police declining to file charges in the first place, or a judgment later vacated or set aside. When one of those happens, the clerk of the court is required to notify the Division of Criminal Justice Services and every law enforcement agency that touched the case, and those agencies are then responsible for sealing the records in their own files.

Sealed is not the same as erased

New York's courts are direct about this: outside of certain marijuana convictions, the state has no law that erases or "expunges" a criminal record. Sealing means the record still exists, but it's hidden from public view. Fingerprint cards, palm prints, booking photos, and DNA samples tied to the sealed case are supposed to be returned or destroyed, with one exception: if digital fingerprints are already on file from a separate, unsealed case, those aren't destroyed.

Who can still see it

A sealed record isn't invisible to everyone. Per the same official guidance, sealed records remain visible to you, the person the case belongs to (with identification), to anyone you specifically authorize to request them, to an employer if the job requires you to carry a gun, to a parole or probation officer if you're arrested while already under their supervision, to a prosecutor if you move for an ACD on a marijuana charge, and to a law enforcement agency that gets a court's approval to unseal it. Out-of-state and federal arrests or convictions are outside New York's sealing rules entirely; sealing a New York case does nothing to a record sitting in another state's system.

What to do if a sealed case still shows up

Sealing is supposed to be automatic, but automatic doesn't mean error-free. The Division of Criminal Justice Services keeps the record that shows up on a background check, commonly called a rap sheet, and if a case that should be sealed is still listed on it, that's treated as a mistake that can be corrected. Pull your own DCJS record and check it against the actual outcome of every case you've had. A sealed case still appearing isn't something to wait out. It's something to get fixed.

The exact question to ask

If a case is heading toward dismissal, or already ended in your favor, ask your attorney directly: "Has the court actually sent the sealing notice to DCJS and to every agency involved, and how do I confirm my own record reflects it?" Don't assume a good outcome in the courtroom automatically means a clean record in every database that matters for a job or a background check.
