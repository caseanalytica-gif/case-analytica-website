A Queens Car Meetup Ended in a Ring of Fire. One Driver Now Faces Up to Seven Years

At around 1:45 a.m. on April 18, 2026, a crowd of roughly 100 people and 50 cars gathered at the intersection of 69th Street and Eliot Avenue, along the Maspeth-Middle Village border in Queens. According to the Queens District Attorney's office, a driver identified as Asil Assaidi, 22, of the Bronx, circled the intersection in a blue Infiniti G37, producing smoke and skid marks, while someone poured liquid onto the pavement and lit a ring of flames between his car and the crowd. A second driver repeated the stunt with a second ring of fire. When NYPD officers arrived in marked patrol cars to disperse the roughly 100 bystanders, someone used a traffic barrier to block one of the cruisers, and two people jumped on its hood and cracked the windshield before the crowd fled.

Queens District Attorney Melinda Katz announced six days later that Assaidi had been arraigned on a six-count criminal complaint: reckless endangerment in the first degree, riot in the second degree, unlawful assembly, reckless driving, operating a vehicle without a license, and operating a vehicle without insurance. "As alleged, this defendant put countless people in peril as he recklessly drove on a fiery street near pedestrians and gas stations," Katz said in the office's press release. "This careless stunt very easily could have ended in injuries and death." Police said Assaidi had no prior arrests. If convicted of the top count, he faces up to seven years in prison. The charges are not bail eligible under New York law, according to the DA's own release, so despite that exposure, no judge set a cash number to hold him pending trial.

This is an allegation, not a finding of guilt, and nothing here should be read as a comment on whether Assaidi actually did what he's accused of. His attorney's information wasn't publicly available as of this writing, and Case Analytica could not confirm the case's current status beyond the return date set at arraignment. Anyone facing a specific charge should talk to a licensed criminal defense attorney rather than rely on a general explainer like this one. What the case does illustrate clearly is a set of statutes most New Yorkers never think about until a crowd, a car, or a group chat lands them in the middle of one.

What riot and unlawful assembly actually require

New York doesn't have one "riot" law, and the two most commonly charged versions have almost nothing to do with what most people picture when they hear the word. Under Penal Law § 240.05, a person is guilty of riot in the second degree when, "simultaneously with four or more other persons," they engage in tumultuous and violent conduct that intentionally or recklessly creates a grave risk of causing public alarm. It's a class A misdemeanor, up to a year in jail. Penal Law § 240.10, unlawful assembly, is the lower-level cousin: gathering with four or more others to engage in, prepare for, or knowingly remain at conduct of that same tumultuous, violent, public-alarm-causing character. It's a class B misdemeanor.

Neither statute requires a riot in the everyday sense of a mob overturning cars or looting stores. It requires five people (the defendant plus four others) and conduct a court finds tumultuous and violent enough to create a grave risk of public alarm. A ring of fire lit in the middle of an intersection, with a crowd of 100 watching two cars spin donuts around it, comfortably clears that bar. So, prosecutors argued here, did a group of teenagers who swarmed the Mall at Bay Plaza in the Bronx on Presidents Day, and a crowd that swarmed Brooklyn Bridge Park's Pier 2 after a social media callout in March, both also charged under these same statutes this year.

Why the felony, not the misdemeanors, carries the real exposure

Riot and unlawful assembly are misdemeanors. The seven years of exposure in this case comes entirely from the sixth charge on the list: reckless endangerment in the first degree, a class D felony under Penal Law § 120.25, which applies when someone, "under circumstances evincing a depraved indifference to human life," recklessly creates a grave risk of death to another person. Two cars spinning in circles near a crowd of 100, next to two gas stations, is exactly the fact pattern that statute was written for. It's the difference between a night that ends with a misdemeanor record and one that ends with a felony conviction, and it usually turns on how close the conduct came to actually killing someone, not on how many people were charged alongside the driver.

A pattern the city is now charging aggressively

Assaidi's case wasn't an isolated one. NYPD and Queens DA records place it inside a run of social-media-organized "takeovers" across the city in the first months of 2026: nearly 500 teenagers descending on the Bay Plaza mall on Presidents Day led to 18 arrests on rioting and unlawful assembly charges; a large snowball fight in Washington Square Park a week later ended with several arrests after officers were pelted; and hundreds of teens swarming Brooklyn Bridge Park in March prompted police to shut the pier down after fights broke out. Bronx District Attorney Darcel Clark and other elected officials responded to the mall incident by sending a letter to TikTok, Snapchat, Meta, and YouTube, asking the platforms to monitor and remove posts promoting these gatherings. City Councilmember Phil Wong, who represents the Maspeth-Middle Village district, called the April incident "reckless" and "criminal," not "just a meetup," and pushed for more proactive policing.

That's the context worth sitting with: a charging pattern is forming around a specific kind of event, organized on social media, drawing large crowds, involving no intended victim, that prosecutors are now treating as felony-level conduct when a car, fire, or weapon is involved. Whether that's the right calibration is a fair public debate. What isn't in dispute is that a 22-year-old with no criminal record is now facing a felony that would follow him for years if he's convicted of it.

No bail, but not no consequence

It can look contradictory that someone facing up to seven years wasn't held on bail, but it isn't. New York's bail statute only authorizes cash bail for a specific, enumerated list of charges, mostly violent felonies. Reckless endangerment in the first degree isn't on that list, and neither are riot, unlawful assembly, or the traffic-level counts attached here, which is exactly why the DA's own release states the charges aren't bail eligible. Case Analytica's explainer on how bail actually gets set in New York after reform covers why the dollar amount, or the absence of one, often has more to do with which statute is charged than how serious the underlying conduct was.

The absence of bail doesn't mean the absence of consequence. A felony conviction here would sit on Assaidi's record for eight years before it became eligible for automatic sealing under the Clean Slate Act, versus three years for a misdemeanor, exactly the kind of gap Case Analytica has covered in the law's implementation. And a felony record raises different questions at the job and apartment application stage than a misdemeanor does; see Case Analytica's explainer on what actually shows up on a background check for how that plays out in practice. For a case built entirely around a public gathering, with no named victim and no one seriously hurt, that's a long tail for one night.

General information, not legal advice

Riot, unlawful assembly, and reckless endangerment each carry specific statutory elements, and whether a particular set of facts actually meets them is something only a licensed attorney reviewing the real evidence can answer. This article explains the law in general terms and reports what's publicly alleged in one case; it isn't a comment on any individual's guilt.

The exact question to ask

For any charge that turns on being part of a group, ask your attorney directly: "What specific act am I alleged to have personally done, separate from being present?" Riot and unlawful assembly charges sweep in people whose individual conduct is thin, and the answer to that question is the case. If the paperwork does not identify a personal act, ask whether that is a motion worth making before any plea discussion.
