The Clean Slate Act New York Almost Passed vs. the One Hochul Signed

A criminal record doesn't decide, on its own, whether someone ends up back in the system. What decides that, more than almost anything else, is whether that person can get a job and a stable place to live once they're out. That's not a slogan. It's the finding across the recidivism research, and it's the same logic Governor Hochul used herself when she signed New York's Clean Slate Act in November 2023: "When people have steady work, they're less likely to commit crimes and less likely to be homeless." Every year it takes New York to actually seal a record that qualifies is a year that logic gets tested against a job application and a rental application that still show a conviction the law says should already be invisible.

That gap is real, it's documented, and it didn't happen by accident. Here's what actually changed in the bill before it became law, and what the delay since has been costing the people it was supposed to help.

The bill that almost passed, versus the one that did

Clean Slate, sponsored by state Sen. Zellnor Myrie and Assembly Member Catalina Cruz, spent years stalling in Albany before it finally passed both chambers in June 2023. According to reporting from the Queens Daily Eagle, the version that originally moved through the Legislature allowed for a shorter waiting period before records would seal, and it didn't carve out most Class A felony convictions from eligibility at all. The version Hochul actually signed does both: a three-year wait for misdemeanors and eight years for felonies, and a blanket exclusion for most Class A felony convictions, including murder and sex offenses, with drug-related Class A felonies as the one exception. That's confirmed directly on the bill's own history at nysenate.gov, Senate Bill S7551, which shows the amended version, S7551A, as what actually reached the floor.

It's tempting to assume the governor quietly rewrote the bill after the fact with a same-day chapter amendment, the way New York governors sometimes do to get a bill they'll actually sign. That's not what happened here, and it's worth being precise about it. After the signing ceremony, Hochul told reporters she did not attach any chapter amendment, and that she was signing exactly what passed the Legislature in June. City & State's coverage of the signing confirms she said the final version came from negotiations with legislative leaders earlier in the year, meaning the waiting periods and the Class A felony carve-out were built into the bill before the June floor vote, not tacked on afterward. The compromise is real. The mechanism was negotiation before passage, not a rewrite after it.

Why this distinction matters

A same-day chapter amendment would mean the Legislature voted for one bill and the governor quietly signed a different one. That's not what the record shows. What actually happened is that advocates, business groups, and law enforcement fought over the bill's terms for years, and the version that finally had enough votes to pass was already the narrower one. The narrowing happened in the room, not after the vote.

The three-year gap nobody put on a poster

Clean Slate took effect November 16, 2024, one year after Hochul signed it. What doesn't get said in most of the celebratory coverage from that week is that the law itself gave New York's Unified Court System until November 16, 2027 to actually build the systems that seal records automatically. That's confirmed directly on the New York State Unified Court System's own Clean Slate Act page: courts need to pull every conviction a person has across every county, match it to time spent on parole, probation, or incarceration, and confirm no case is pending, all before a record can seal. Until that system exists, DCJS keeps handing out records that are supposed to already be gone.

The same page states plainly that the form allowing someone to request a manual review, for a record they believe should already be sealed and isn't, won't even be published until November 16, 2027. In other words, if the automated system misses your record, there is currently no formal mechanism to fix that. You wait for the deadline.

If you think your record already qualifies

Check your status through the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services record review process. See our full breakdown of the underlying waiting periods and exclusions in The Clean Slate Act: When Does Your NY Record Actually Get Sealed? if you haven't already read it. Being past the waiting period on paper and having the record actually sealed in the state's system are not the same thing right now, and that gap is exactly what's costing people jobs and housing in the meantime.

What the delay is actually costing

This is where the throughline matters. Prison Policy Initiative's research library puts the unemployment rate among formerly incarcerated people at 27 percent, roughly five times the general population rate, and identifies poverty itself as the single strongest predictor of recidivism. A federal Bureau of Justice Statistics cohort found 68 percent of people released from prison in 2005 were rearrested within three years. None of that is abstract. A person who can't pass a background check can't get hired. A person who can't get hired can't qualify for a lease. A person without stable income or a stable address is, by every metric the research tracks, more likely to end up back in the system, not less.

There's also direct evidence of what happens when automatic sealing actually works instead of sitting in a backlog. The Clean Slate Initiative's YouGov survey of 800 people with records in Pennsylvania, Utah, and Michigan, states where automated sealing systems are already operating, found that 42 percent of respondents whose records were sealed reported improved employment outcomes, and 24 percent reported improved housing outcomes. Those states aren't New York, and the comparison isn't perfect. But it's the clearest evidence available that sealing works exactly the way the law promises, once it's actually implemented. New York's own system isn't there yet, and won't be, by the state's own timeline, until November 2027.

A sealed record changes what's possible on a job application and a rental application. See Does a Conviction Actually Show Up on a Background Check in New York? for how that plays out in practice, and what Correction Law Article 23-A actually requires of a New York employer even before a record seals. If housing is the more immediate problem, New York City has its own separate limits on how a landlord can use an unsealed record; see the Fair Chance for Housing Act explained.

What to do if the sealing timeline doesn't help right now

Not everyone can afford to wait out a multi-year backlog, and not every record qualifies for Clean Slate sealing at all. A Certificate of Relief from Disabilities removes many of the same employment and licensing barriers without waiting on the sealing clock; see who qualifies and how to request one. There's also the older, motion-based sealing route under CPL 160.59, which covers a narrower set of convictions but has to be filed rather than waited out.

The people who wrote Clean Slate said the point was jobs and stable lives, and they were right about the mechanism. The problem isn't the theory behind the law. It's that New York gave itself until 2027 to actually build it, and every year between now and then is a year someone qualifies on paper and still can't prove it to an employer or a landlord.
